# Isamu Tanaka

Mizuki's paternal grandfather, Hiroshi's father, founder of the family okonomiyaki shop. Died suddenly in the winter of 2005, when Mizuki was fourteen. Character page: keep him consistent with `show/mizuki/SPINE.md` (which wins any conflict) and the chapters in `show/mizuki/history/`. His death is told rarely and gently, in one sentence. His life, his shop and his watch are warm, everyday material.

## Basics

- **Born:** 1932, Abeno, Osaka.
- **Died:** February 2005, aged 72, suddenly and peacefully in his sleep (his heart), at home. Mizuki was fourteen, in the third term of her second year of junior high.
- **From:** Osaka (Abeno). The elder of two brothers; his younger brother Tsutomu is grandfather of cousin Daisuke (Tsutomu's son Makoto, Hiroshi's cousin, moved to Kobe for work).
- **Job:** okonomiyaki cook. Through his late teens and twenties washed dishes and then cooked at a busy okonomiyaki place in the south of the city, then **opened his own shop in Abeno in early October 1964**, the year of the Tokyo Olympics. Made Hiroshi his partner at the hotplate in 1987, handed it to him fully around 1994, and "supervised" from seat one every morning until 2005.
- **Lived:** with Chiyo in the two rooms above the shop until 1987; then in a small house three streets away, near the Hankai tram line, with a narrow concrete yard full of potted bonsai that he talked to.
- **Married:** Chiyo, in 1956.
- **Relationship to Mizuki:** her paternal grandfather, "Jiichan". She wears his watch every day.

## Looks and style

Short, broad and strong, with big cook's hands covered in old small burns he called "medals". A shaved head under a white twisted towel (hachimaki) at the hotplate; a flat cap outside. A loud laugh with a gold tooth in it. Grey work trousers, a white undershirt, and in his retirement a cardigan with a Tigers pin. On his wrist always the same plain steel **Seiko** with a cream face gone slightly yellow, a little date window at three o'clock and a steel bracelet that pulled the hairs on his wrist, bought to mark the shop's opening in 1964. Hand-wound every night; slow by about two minutes a week, so he reset it every Sunday against the radio time signal.

## Personality and sayings

The opposite of his son: a talker, a joker, a performer at the counter. Loud, generous, quick-tempered for exactly one minute, then sorry. A born shopkeeper who called every customer "taisho" (boss) and every child "shachō" (company president). Superstitious about the hotplate and about baseball. Sentimental, and pretended not to be. Hiroshi's quietness, the family says, is "Chiyo's side".

Quotes Mizuki repeats:
- "Okonomi means 'what you like'. So I make it how *I* like, and you like it." (His whole business philosophy.)
- "A shop is a promise. You open at the same time every day, even when nobody comes."
- "Shachō! Your order?" (To every child who came through the noren, including Mizuki.)
- "Mizuki-chan, this watch says you are a disaster." (Tapping the glass whenever she came in late. When she said his watch was slow, he said it had never been slow in forty years. It was.)
- "The Tigers lose so we can enjoy the beer."
- "Mada mada" (he said it first; Hiroshi inherited it with the hotplate).

## Likes and dislikes

- **Likes:** his hotplate; the smell of new cabbage; the Nankai Hawks until they were sold off to Fukuoka in 1988, then (after a month of being "retired from baseball") the Hanshin Tigers; the 1964 Olympics (he watched them on a second-hand TV in the brand-new shop and never stopped talking about it); his Seiko; sumo on TV with Chiyo; Shinsekai kushikatsu and its no-double-dipping rule, which he taught Mizuki himself ("Once. If you dip twice, the police come."); Misora Hibari; grandchildren on his knee at the end of the counter; a good argument with a regular.
- **Dislikes:** Tokyo Giants; wasted batter; anyone pressing an okonomiyaki down ("you're squeezing out the air, and the air is free"); hospitals; slow walkers; the new chain okonomiyaki restaurants of the 1990s ("factories"); being told to retire.

## Life story

- **1932** born in Osaka. A boy of twelve in 1945, when much of the city burns; he never talks about it except to say "I was hungry". After the war he grows up alongside the one-sen griddle stalls, then works wherever there's a hotplate, washing dishes and then cooking at a busy okonomiyaki place in the south of the city, where an old cook teaches him the rules.
- **1956** marries Chiyo, a sharp young woman from Kishiwada who keeps his money straight. **1958** son Hiroshi born.
- **1964** opens his own okonomiyaki shop in Abeno in early October, in a former tobacconist's: eight counter seats, two tables, an indigo noren, one long black iron hotplate, and two rooms upstairs where the family lives. The Tokyo Olympics open on 10 October; the shinkansen has just started running; the Tigers win the Central League pennant and lose the Japan Series to his Nankai Hawks, the other Osaka team. On 23 October, when the women's volleyball team wins gold, he bangs two spatulas together and gives everyone a free beer, which Chiyo adds to the next week's bills. He buys the Seiko to mark the opening because Seiko are the official timekeepers of the Games: a man starting a business that year ought to be on Olympic time.
- **1964 to 1987** runs the hotplate with Chiyo at the till. Six days a week, closed Wednesdays, a rule he set and nobody has changed.
- **1977** takes Hiroshi on full-time at the hotplate; **1982** finally pays him a proper wage, and is secretly impressed when Hiroshi spends it on a camera (Chiyo is appalled).
- **1987** Hiroshi marries Yumiko; Isamu cries during the sake-sharing and denies it at the reception. Hiroshi becomes his partner at the hotplate, and Isamu and Chiyo move to the bonsai house three streets away so the newlyweds can have the rooms upstairs.
- **1988** the Hawks are sold and leave for Fukuoka. He declares himself "retired from baseball" and is watching the Tigers with Hiroshi within a month.
- **Around 1994** hands Hiroshi the hotplate fully. From then on he comes in every morning at ten (Wednesdays excepted) to sit on seat one, the stool at the end of the counter, with tea or a can of coffee and the sports paper.
- **1990s** "supervises". Watches toddler Mizuki on her stool at the end of the counter shouting "irasshaimase"; teaches her "ookini" from the old customers; gives Kenta his first Mini 4WD.
- **2002** teaches Mizuki the two-spatula flip on the cooled hotplate after closing, with a folded tea towel instead of batter: "Wrists. Not shoulders."
- **2003** the Tigers win the pennant again. He is seventy-one and weeps openly on seat one, blowing his nose on a shop towel.
- **February 2005** on a cold weeknight he watches the news, winds the Seiko as he does every night, goes to bed and doesn't wake up: his heart. The shop closes for a week; when it reopens, Hiroshi cooks with Isamu's old spatulas and the first okonomiyaki of the evening goes on a plate on seat one with a can of coffee. A few weeks after the funeral Chiyo gives the watch to Mizuki, as he had asked every New Year's Day: "Because you were always late, and somebody in this family had to learn."

## With Mizuki

**Key memories:** being called "shachō"; sitting on his knee at the end of the counter after school while he read the Tigers results to her as bedtime stories, with voices for the pitchers; learning to say "ookini" to the old regulars; the kushikatsu rule ("no double dipping, or the police come"); the two-spatula flip in the summer of 2002; Jiichan weeping on seat one at the 2003 pennant; the cloth bundle Chiyo put in her hands.

**How she carries him now:** his Seiko on her wrist every day, which she calls her "old man's watch". It's hand-wound, so she winds it every night as he did, and it still runs about two minutes slow a week, so she sets it every Sunday against a time signal, as he did. A jeweller in the Abeno arcade took out three links for free in 2005. It has been serviced twice and the strap swapped once, and she is rude about the price. When she's late she taps the glass the way he did.

**Shinto bits:** put up the shop's kamidana, high behind the hotplate, the night before opening in October 1964, with an ofuda from Sumiyoshi Taisha ("You don't want to be the only shop on the street the gods have to look for"); practical, not pious. Took small Mizuki to Toka Ebisu at Imamiya Ebisu (9 to 11 January), last year's bamboo under one arm and her on the other, and hoisted her above the crowd to see the shrine maidens handing out the fukuzasa: "That's where the money comes from."

**What she feels:** fondness, and a little guilt about being late, which he was right about. One sentence on air, never more.

## Running details for the show

- The watch: plain steel Seiko, hand-wound, cream face gone yellow, date window at three, bought in 1964 for the shop's opening. She checks it instead of her phone.
- The shop's opening year, 1964, the Wednesday closing day and seat one are his.
- Fun fact she tells: Seiko really did time the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, the first time a Japanese company did it.
- "Okonomi means 'what you like'": her favourite explanation of okonomiyaki to Australians.
- Every 10 October (the old Sports Day, the 1964 opening ceremony date) she winds the watch fully and says "kanpai, Jiichan".
