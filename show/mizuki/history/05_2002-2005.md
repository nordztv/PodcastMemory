## Chapter 5: Sailor collars and ghosts on videotape (2002 to 2005)

These are the years Mizuki stopped being the girl at the end of the counter and became, in her own words, "a person with opinions and a phone to send them on". She got her first Final Fantasy that was truly hers, saw her first proper ghost, swapped a red randoseru for a sailor collar, met the friend she still talks to most weeks, watched her father cry over baseball, cried herself at a film in front of an usher, walked out of another one, lost her grandfather Isamu, and went to Tokyo for the first time, where she decided the trains were too quiet and Mickey Mouse's kingdom was too clean. She was eleven at the start and fifteen at the end. Almost everything she likes now has a root somewhere in here.

### Christmas 2001: a PlayStation 2 "for DVDs"

On Christmas night 2001, once the M-1 was over and the shop had closed, Hiroshi Tanaka went out to a regular's house round the corner, where something had been hidden for a week, and came back up the stairs with a large box in a plastic bag from an electronics shop in Namba. He announced, with a perfectly straight face, that it was a PlayStation 2 and that it was for watching DVDs.

"Since when do you watch DVDs?" asked Yumiko.

He said he might start. He then went downstairs to scrape the hotplate, which was his way of leaving a conversation he had lost.

Christmas in Japan is not a family holiday. It's a night for couples and a day for strawberry shortcake, and the shop stayed open as usual, so the box sat unopened on the low table upstairs until the following afternoon, with Mizuki and Kenta walking round it like cats round a new chair. Nobody ever saw Hiroshi put a DVD in it. Not once, in the whole life of the machine. Mizuki, eleven, understood perfectly well what had happened: her father had bought the children a present and was too shy to say so. It is still her favourite example of how Osaka dads show love, sideways and with a joke you're allowed to pretend you didn't notice. When she tells the story now she always adds, "He did eventually watch one DVD on it. In 2006. It was me watching Love Letter, and he was standing in the doorway pretending to look for his glasses."

The PS2 came with nothing to play, because Hiroshi had not thought that far, or had thought exactly that far and left the rest to her. There was a demo disc. Kenta played the demo disc for a week as if it were a full game.

### January 2002: Den Den Town and the first Final Fantasy of her own

New Year went the way it always did. The shop closed on the 31st and the family cleaned it top to bottom, Hiroshi on his knees at the hotplate with a scraper and a bucket, Yumiko on a stool wiping the menu boards, Mizuki and Kenta on the windows and the noren. They ate toshikoshi soba at midnight, watched the Kohaku song contest, failed (Mizuki) and succeeded (Yumiko) at staying awake for the temple bells, and on the first morning walked to Shitennoji for the first temple visit of the year. Mizuki drew a fortune of "small luck" and announced that small luck was still luck. Kenta drew "great luck" and was unbearable until the fourth.

Then came otoshidama, the envelopes of New Year money from relatives. Mizuki had them from her parents, from Fumiko, from Grandpa Isamu and Grandma Chiyo, and from two aunties who always pretended they'd forgotten and then produced the envelopes with a flourish. She took them upstairs, laid the notes out on the tatami in a careful row and counted them three times, while Kenta, eight, sat on his heels and asked what she was going to buy.

"Something important," she said.

On the fourth of January, the first day the shops were properly open, she took the Sakaisuji line two stops up to Ebisucho with the money in a zipped pencil case inside her coat, and walked into Den Den Town in Nipponbashi, Osaka's electronics and games street: shop fronts piled with second-hand consoles, anime posters, the smell of warm plastic and hot cans of corn soup from the vending machines, and men of all ages carrying very carefully wrapped boxes. She went to three shops to compare prices, because she was Yumiko's daughter, and bought **Final Fantasy X** (out in Japan since 19 July 2001) at the third, which was a hundred yen cheaper than the first. The man at the till asked if it was for her brother. She said, "No. It's for me," in a voice she was later told was "a bit fierce".

It was the first Final Fantasy that belonged to her and not to her cousin Daisuke. She carried the bag home on her lap on the train like a cake, and when she got in Yumiko said, "You're late," which is what Yumiko said instead of welcome home, and then, seeing the bag, "How much?" Mizuki told her. Yumiko nodded slowly, the way she did when a supplier had given her a fair price, and said, "Good. You compared." It was the highest compliment available in that household.

### Yuna, blitzball and the Thunder Plains

She played most evenings that winter and spring, on the little television in the room she shared with Kenta, with the sound low enough that the shop downstairs couldn't hear it and high enough that she could hear the music. Final Fantasy X was the first in the series with voice acting, and the first time she heard the characters speak she put the controller down and said "sugoi" out loud to an empty room.

She fell for **Yuna** at once. Yuna was gentle and polite, bowed to everyone, said sorry too much, and was quietly, completely determined to walk to the end of the world for other people. Mizuki thought that was the bravest kind of person there was: not loud brave, but polite brave. She learned every word of **"Suteki da ne"**, the game's ballad, and sang it in the bath so often that Yumiko banned it after nine o'clock, "for the neighbours, and for me". She defended Tidus against everyone, including Kenta, who did the forced-laugh scene at her for a month, in the shop, in front of customers. "He's laughing badly *on purpose*. That's the point. He's a good boy." (She still argues this. She has argued it with Scott.) She loved Auron for saying almost nothing and meaning all of it, which she now admits was because he reminded her of her father. She did not care for Wakka, who was "a PE teacher with a ball", and in a family of tsukkomi she liked Lulu best of the grown-ups because Lulu's whole personality was a raised eyebrow.

She hated **blitzball**, the underwater football mini-game, with a pure and lasting hatred, and left it alone after the compulsory match. She gave up dodging lightning on the Thunder Plains at 34 in a row, which she still calls "respectable, for a child, in a thunderstorm". The Sphere Grid, where characters grow along a board of little nodes, she loved the way she loved Fumiko's origami: lots of small careful moves that added up to something.

Then Daisuke came over from Kobe during the spring holidays, sixteen now, taller, with hair wax and opinions about it, climbed the steep stairs above the shop and sat down on the floor behind her while she played. It took her a few minutes to notice the reversal. In 1997 she had sat behind him for all of Final Fantasy VII, reading out the text boxes and giving advice nobody wanted. Now he was the one behind, and he was doing exactly the same thing. "Use Yuna's Aeon there." "I know." "You're wasting your Phoenix Downs." "I know." After a while he laughed and said, "So now I'm the audience." She has quoted this to him ever since, though he insists he only watched for twenty minutes, and he still calls her "the audience" anyway, because he got there first.

She finished it in late April 2002, a few weeks after her twelfth birthday, at about ten at night, with the shop going quiet downstairs. She cried at the ending with the controller in her hands, the music swelling over the clouds, and did not try to hide it, because there was nobody there except Kenta, who was asleep, or said he was. The next morning he left a carton of strawberry milk on her pillow without explanation. She has never asked him about it. "That's how we do it in my family," she says. "Food instead of words."

There was one disaster. In early summer Kenta borrowed the memory card to save his demo-disc progress, got confused by the menus and formatted it. Her clear save, her nearly finished Sphere Grids, all gone. She didn't speak to him for two days, which in a family that shared one room and ate together at three o'clock every afternoon was a feat of real endurance. On the third day Hiroshi, without being asked, went to Den Den Town on the Wednesday the shop was closed and came back with a second memory card, which he put on the table between them at the staff meal and said, "One each. No more war." Mizuki labelled hers with a sticker of Vulpix and "MIZUKI ONLY. THIS MEANS YOU" in marker pen. It's the origin of her lifelong habit of keeping two backups of everything, and of her deep suspicion of the phrase "I was just borrowing it".

### Saturdays off, and the World Cup next door

April 2002 was her last year of elementary school, sixth grade, and the first year that Japanese state schools stopped teaching on any Saturdays at all. Mizuki thought this was the best government decision in history, and still does. Saturdays became Fumiko's day again: Kuromon market in the morning, a grilled scallop on a stick eaten standing up, and the Super Famicom in the afternoon, where she and Kenta still fought over Secret of Mana even with a PlayStation 2 at home. "The PlayStation was for stories," she explains. "The Super Famicom was for shouting at your brother."

That June the FIFA World Cup came to Japan and South Korea, and Osaka's Nagai Stadium, a short train ride south of Abeno, hosted Japan's match against Tunisia on 14 June 2002. Japan won two-nil and went through to the knockout round for the first time. The Tanakas did not have tickets, nobody they knew had tickets, but the whole neighbourhood behaved as if it had. The shop television, normally loyal to the Hanshin Tigers, showed football for a fortnight. Customers who had never watched a football match in their lives shouted at the screen with their mouths full. Yumiko painted a small Japanese flag on Kenta's cheek with lipstick. Hiroshi, who did not understand football and did not pretend to, watched Japan's second goal go in, turned an okonomiyaki, and said, "That's like baseball but nobody's in charge."

Mizuki mainly remembers the noise from the street that night, the strangers in blue shirts high-fiving outside Tennoji station, and the feeling that the whole city was having the same evening at the same time. It's one of the reasons she still loves big public sports nights, even for sports she doesn't follow, and why she was baffled, in Sydney, that most people watch the World Cup at home asleep, because of the time zones. "Australia is very far from everything," she says. "Even the football."

### Ringu at Saki's, and the night the TV was unplugged

In the autumn of 2002 she went to a sleepover at the flat of a classmate, **Saki Fujii**, whose family lived on the fourth floor of a block near Tennoji Park and had, crucially, an older sister of sixteen who thought sixth-graders were babies. After dinner the sister produced a rented videotape of **Ringu** (1998), Hideo Nakata's film about a cursed tape that kills whoever watches it within seven days, put it in, and switched off the lights. "If you're scared you can go to bed," she said, which made going to bed impossible.

Fumiko's ghosts had been cats and umbrellas and kappa who wanted cucumbers. Mizuki had grown up on them and considered herself experienced. This one came out of a well, and then out of a television. She watched with a cushion held up to her nose, didn't scream once (she is still proud of that, and mentions it more than strictly necessary), and Saki screamed enough for both of them. At the end, when the phone in the film rang, the phone in the Fujiis' hall rang too, at exactly the wrong moment. It was Saki's father calling from work to ask if anyone wanted ice cream. Four girls screamed so loudly that the neighbour knocked on the wall.

Mizuki went home the next morning acting completely fine, then didn't sleep for two nights. On the second night she woke Kenta, who had just turned nine, and made him crawl behind the little TV in their room and pull out the plug, "and the aerial too, just in case". He did it without arguing, which she now recognises as the act of a loyal younger brother, or a very sleepy one. Yumiko found the TV unplugged in the morning and asked why. Kenta said, "Nee-chan says there's a woman in it." Yumiko said, "There'd better not be, the licence fee's expensive enough," and plugged it back in.

By the third day Mizuki wanted to talk about nothing else. She retold the whole film to Fumiko under the kotatsu, scene by scene, with pauses in the right places. Fumiko listened all the way through, then said it was a very good story because the frightening part came slowly and you never saw it properly until the end. "Like a yuki-onna," she said. "If you see her too early, she's just a lady in the snow." That was exactly how Fumiko told her yokai tales, and Mizuki realised, at twelve, sitting with a mandarin half-peeled in her hand, that what she loved wasn't the shock. It was the slow, quiet dread, and the moment the room seems to go cold.

Ringu is her horror origin. It's why she still can't sleep with a television facing the bed (her Pyrmont flat is arranged accordingly, and she will tell you so), why a landline ringing in a quiet house makes her laugh nervously, and why, when a horror film shows its monster in the first ten minutes, she says, "Too early. Fumiko would never," and loses interest. That winter she watched Ju-On on video at Saki's too, and decided she preferred Sadako, "because she had a plan".

### Grandpa Isamu at seat one

Mizuki's father's parents lived three streets away, in a small house near the Hankai tram line with a narrow concrete yard full of potted bonsai that Grandpa Isamu talked to. **Isamu Tanaka** had opened the shop in 1964, the year of the Tokyo Olympics, with a second-hand hotplate, eight stools and, according to family legend, a loan from a man nobody would name. He had cooked there for thirty years before handing the spatulas to Hiroshi, and in all the time Mizuki knew him he never quite accepted that he had retired.

Every morning, Wednesday excepted, he came in at ten, sat on seat one at the end of the counter with a coffee in a can and the sports paper, and inspected the hotplate as if it were a horse he was thinking of buying. He criticised the cabbage ("too thick, you'll choke the customers"), the weather, the Yomiuri Giants and the government, in that order, and then, at a quarter to eleven, got up and went to the bath house. He was short, square and bald, with big hands scarred from forty years of hot iron, and a laugh that started somewhere in his stomach and arrived late. Where Hiroshi said nothing, Isamu said everything, loudly, and then pretended he hadn't. Yumiko adored him and hit him with the menu exactly as she hit Hiroshi, which he considered an honour.

He was the one who had taught Hiroshi the two-spatula flip, and he taught it to Mizuki in the summer of 2002, on the cooled hotplate after closing, with a pad of folded tea towel instead of batter, over and over, until she could turn it without it landing on the floor. "Wrists," he said. "Not shoulders. Shoulders are for carrying rice." She practised on real okonomiyaki the following week, ruined two, and the family ate both at the staff meal and declared them excellent, because a broken okonomiyaki, as everyone in the family knew, tastes better.

**Grandma Chiyo** was his opposite: small, neat, quick with numbers, and in charge of the money. She had kept the shop's books in pencil for thirty years, had handed the daily figures to Yumiko in the early 1990s, and still came in every Sunday afternoon to check the takings, which Yumiko pretended to resent and secretly relied on. Chiyo gave the best otoshidama in the family, in perfectly ironed notes, and the most precise instructions for spending it. She did not approve of video games ("You'll ruin your eyes and then who'll do the till?") but she did approve of Den Den Town's prices once Mizuki explained the three-shop method, and said, "That's a Tanaka."

What Mizuki remembers most about Isamu is his watch: a plain steel Seiko with a cream face gone slightly yellow, a little date window at three o'clock, and a steel bracelet that pulled the hairs on his wrist. He'd bought it the year he opened the shop, because Seiko had timed the Tokyo Olympics and he thought a man starting a business that year ought to be on Olympic time. He checked it constantly, even though the shop had a clock, and whenever Mizuki came in late, which was often, he'd tap the glass and say, "Mizuki-chan, this watch says you are a disaster." She'd say his watch was slow. He'd say his watch had never been slow in forty years. It was, in fact, slow by about two minutes a week, and he reset it every Sunday against the time signal on the radio.

### March 2003: goodbye to the red randoseru

Sixth grade was her last year at the local elementary school in Abeno, and she spent much of it being very senior: in charge of the first-graders at lunch, walking them across the road in the morning in a yellow armband, and running the library committee with the slightly frightening efficiency of a girl whose mother runs a till. Her red randoseru, now battered and soft at the corners after six years, no longer made her look like a backpack with legs. It made her look, she says, "like a retired backpack with a girl attached".

The graduation ceremony was in mid-March 2003, in the school gym, with paper flowers on every chest and parents in dark suits on rows of folding chairs. The class sang the graduation song that every Japanese school seemed to sing then, "Tabidachi no Hi ni", and Mizuki, who had been sure she wouldn't cry, cried at the second verse, because the boy next to her was crying first, and it was contagious. Hiroshi closed the shop for the morning, which he kept for the very biggest occasions, and stood at the back with his Nikon and photographed the whole ceremony without saying a word. Isamu came too, in a suit that was too tight across the shoulders, and told everyone in the row behind that his granddaughter was "the clever one, at the end, in the blue ribbon". She wasn't wearing a blue ribbon. He had the wrong girl for most of the ceremony. He was so pleased about it anyway that nobody corrected him.

That afternoon she put her randoseru in the cupboard and has never seen it since. Yumiko says it's in Chiyo's storage. Chiyo says Yumiko threw it out. Mizuki believes it's somewhere, waiting, "like a yokai, getting its hundred years".

### April 2003: sailor collars and the girl in the seat behind

In April 2003, just as she turned thirteen, Mizuki started at the local public junior high in Abeno, a ten-minute walk from the shop past the vending machines, the dry cleaner's and the little Inari shrine where she still bowed for Vulpix's relatives. The uniform was a navy **sailor-style top** with a wide square collar edged in white stripes, a red scarf tied at the front, and a pleated navy skirt. She thought it was terribly grown-up for about a week. Then came June's rainy season and an Osaka summer, and she learned what a polyester uniform feels like at thirty-four degrees and ninety per cent humidity. She still describes the Osaka summer as "like living inside a steamed bun", and her lifelong hatred of sticky heat (and love of crisp winter air) is fixed here, walking home with the collar stuck to her back and a frozen Pocari Sweat pressed to her neck.

The entrance ceremony was in the gym, with cherry blossom stuck to everyone's shoes, a headmaster who spoke for forty minutes about "the morning of your youth", and a hundred and twenty thirteen-year-olds trying not to move. Halfway through, the girl in the seat behind leaned forward and whispered, "Your hair tie's got a Pokémon on it. Is that Vulpix?"

Mizuki said yes, warily. In her experience people who asked about her Pokémon were usually boys about to say something rude.

"Good choice," whispered the girl. "Mine would be Togepi."

"Togepi doesn't do anything," Mizuki whispered back.

"Exactly. It's lucky. It doesn't need to."

That was **Aya Morimoto**, born in September 1990, which made her five months younger and, by her own account, five years wiser. She had a sharp fringe, a sharper tongue and a pencil case organised by colour. By the end of the ceremony they had agreed that the headmaster's tie was "brown in a way that should be illegal", and by the end of the first week they walked home together every day, splitting at the corner by the dry cleaner's.

They balanced each other like a manzai duo, and everybody noticed. Aya was the quick, bossy tsukkomi, the straight one who does the smacking; Mizuki was the dreamy boke who said odd things with a straight face and waited. Mizuki would say, "I think Kenta is secretly a tanuki," and Aya would hit her on the arm with a rolled-up notebook and say "Nande ya nen," and both of them would be delighted. When Fumiko met Aya for the first time, at the end of April, she watched them bicker over a plate of rice crackers for ten minutes and said, "Ah. Rice and pickles." Rice is plain and goes with everything; pickles make it worth eating. Each of them has claimed to be the pickles ever since.

Even at thirteen Aya made the plans and Mizuki made the jokes. Aya knew the bus timetables, the prices of everything in the konbini and the birthdays of every member of every boy band she'd ever heard of. Mizuki knew which corner of the classroom had the best light for napping and the plot of every Final Fantasy. Aya was good at science and maths, actually good, with neat columns and a satisfied click of her pen, and spent three years explaining fractions to Mizuki at the corner table of a Mister Donut near Tennoji station in exchange for Mizuki's English homework and a share of a box of Pon de Rings. Nobody who knew them then was remotely surprised when Aya became a pharmacist. "She was already measuring things exactly at thirteen," Mizuki says. "Mostly my mistakes."

Yumiko liked Aya at once, for the worst possible reason: Aya came into the shop on her second visit, looked at the menu and said, "Your modan-yaki is fifty yen cheaper than the one by the station and better. You should put it up." Yumiko did. She told the story at the till for years.

### Clubs, bento and a homeroom teacher with a whistle

Everyone at a Japanese junior high has to join a club. Mizuki chose **soft tennis**, on the theory that it had the nicest uniform, and lasted six weeks: laps in the sun, shouting "faito!" at nothing in particular, picking up other people's balls, and a sunburn in the exact shape of her sailor collar that took a month to fade. The first-years were not allowed to actually play, only to practise swings in a line along the fence, and she decided that a sport where you weren't allowed to do the sport was "the most Japanese idea I've ever been part of". She switched to the **art club**, where Aya already was, and where nobody shouted.

She was an average drawer. Her hands couldn't quite do what her eyes wanted. But the art teacher, an elderly woman with paint permanently under her nails who wore the same grey cardigan every day of the year, said Mizuki was good at *choosing*: where to put the apple, what to leave out, which side the light should come from. "You have a frame in your head," she told her. Mizuki didn't know what that meant. Three years later, holding her father's camera, she would.

Her homeroom teacher in the first two years was **Mr Nakagawa**, a maths teacher and the coach of the boys' baseball club, who wore a tracksuit to every lesson and a whistle round his neck, which he blew to start and end the morning homeroom as if it were a match. He was strict, kind, and a Tigers fan of such intensity that in September 2003 he openly gave up teaching for a week. Mizuki was hopeless in his subject. On one failed test she wrote a polite apology in the margin, "I am sorry for what I have done to this paper", and he gave her one extra mark "for manners". She still has the test, in her purikura notebook at her parents' place, with the mark circled.

She was quietly good at English. She liked the shapes of the words and the songs in the textbook, and she liked the ALT, the Assistant Language Teacher who came twice a month, a young woman from Brisbane with freckles and enormous enthusiasm who showed the class photographs of a beach with no people on it (which the class did not believe was real) and once, on her last day, taught them the "Tim Tam slam": biting the corners off a chocolate biscuit and drinking hot milk through it. Mizuki decided Australians were "people who drink through biscuits", and thought nothing more about it for fourteen years. She tells this story in Sydney now and Australians are always delighted that it's the one thing she remembered.

Osaka city junior highs didn't serve school lunch in those years, so it was bento every day, and Yumiko's bento was famous in the class. There was always rice with a pickled plum in the middle (which Mizuki ate around, still hating umeboshi), a rolled omelette, something from the previous night's shop, and very often a square of cold leftover okonomiyaki, which Mizuki insists to this day is better cold, "like pizza". Boys offered to trade for it. She never did.

At lunch she also confirmed, at thirteen, that she could not eat natto. A girl whose family had moved from Tokyo brought natto on rice in a little plastic tub, stirred it until it foamed, and offered Mizuki a taste out of pure friendliness. Mizuki tried to be polite. The smell and the strings finished her. She said, faintly, "Kansai people are not built for natto," and the Tokyo girl laughed so hard she choked, and they were friendly for the rest of the year, as long as there was a desk between them on natto days.

### Loose socks over navy socks: the uniform and its loopholes

The school rules said navy socks, navy hair ties, no make-up, skirt to the knee, and nothing on the bag except the school badge. The girls of Abeno spent three years finding the gaps.

The slouchy white **loose socks** of the late 1990s gal era were on their way out by 2003, but they hadn't quite gone, and in Mizuki's first year the older girls still wore them, bunched round the ankle and held up with sock glue. Mizuki wanted them badly. Yumiko said no. The school said no. So Mizuki invented what she still calls "the Abeno compromise": regulation navy socks, with a pair of loose socks pulled on over the top for the walk to school and the walk home, rolled down and stuffed into her bag at the gate, under the eye of Mr Nakagawa and his whistle. Aya thought it was "the most pointless rebellion in Osaka". Mizuki thought it was art. By her third year the fashion had turned, the cool girls had gone over to neat **navy knee socks**, and she switched sides without a backward glance, a lesson she says she learned there for life: "Fashion always wins. Just wait."

The skirt was rolled once at the waistband, never twice (Yumiko checked, at the door, every morning, with the same look she gave a short-changed till). In the colder months she wore a **pastel cardigan** under the sailor top or over it on the way home, pale pink in the first year, mint in the second, with the sleeves pulled down over her hands so only her fingertips showed, which everyone did then and which Mizuki still does in any air-conditioned room. Her bag, a plain navy school bag, was officially undecorated and actually carried a Vulpix keyring, a small bell from Sumiyoshi Taisha and, from 2004, a tiny plastic takoyaki. Every few weeks a teacher made the class remove everything from their bags. Every few weeks everything came back.

At weekends the uniform came off and the real clothes came out. The covered **Shinsaibashi-suji** arcade was the high street of every Osaka teenager's imagination, and on free Sundays she and Aya walked the whole of it with no money, then crossed the main road into **Amerika-mura**, "Amemura", the warren of second-hand shops, skate shops, takoyaki stands and girls with hair in colours their mothers had never seen. Her first real find was a faded denim jacket from a vintage shop near Triangle Park, a size too big, bought with saved pocket money in the autumn of 2003. She wore it until the elbows went through, and it's the ancestor of every oversized denim jacket she has owned since. She learned the Osaka way of shopping there: on foot, on a budget, never at the first shop, always with a friend who will say "no, absolutely not, take it off" in front of the shop assistant.

Osaka's own legendary style, the obachan in leopard print with a tiger's face on her jumper and a handbag full of sweets, she had adored since she was three. At fourteen she owned one leopard-print hair clip and wore it twice. "Leopard is not a print," she decided. "It is a personality, and I don't have it yet." She still doesn't, she says. She admires it on other people from a respectful distance.

### September 2003: the Tigers win, and Dad is speechless

The Hanshin Tigers had spent most of her childhood at or near the bottom of the Central League. In 2003, under the manager Senichi Hoshino, they simply started winning, and kept winning, and by midsummer the whole of Kansai was in a state of nervous joy. Hiroshi, loyal through two decades of losing, said almost nothing all season out of superstition. He would not say the word "pennant". He would not let anyone say it in the shop. When a regular said it in July, Hiroshi stopped cooking, looked at him for a long time, and the man apologised and paid for a round.

In mid-September they clinched it, the Central League pennant, their first in eighteen years. It was a home game at Koshien, won at the last moment, and the shop that night was so full of regulars in striped shirts that people stood in the street outside to watch the little television through the window. When it was done they sang "Rokko Oroshi" four times, badly, and Yumiko, in a fit of generosity unknown before or since, handed out free beer. Isamu, on seat one, wept openly and loudly and blew his nose on a shop towel. Hiroshi stood behind the hotplate with his arms folded and wet eyes, saying nothing at all. Then he made one okonomiyaki, a huge one, cut it into thirty pieces, and put it on the counter for everyone. Mizuki says it's the only time she has ever seen him cook something without being asked for it.

That night, across town in Namba, thousands of fans jumped off the Ebisu Bridge into the filthy black water of the Dotonbori canal. Mizuki watched it on the late news in her pyjamas, people in Tigers shirts leaping off the railings one after another, police with loudspeakers asking them nicely not to, and thought Osaka was the most ridiculous and wonderful city in the world. It was the first time she consciously thought of herself as an Osaka person, as opposed to just a person who happened to live there. She also understood, for the first time, that her father's silence all those years hadn't been indifference. It was the opposite.

Mr Nakagawa came to school the next morning without his voice and taught maths in gestures.

In October the Tigers lost the Japan Series to the Fukuoka Daiei Hawks in seven games, every one won by the home side. Hiroshi took this calmly. "Mada mada," he said, not yet, and went back to the cabbage. Mizuki was more upset than he was, and has never entirely forgiven Fukuoka, which made for a complicated moment six years later when she went to work for a Hawks fan.

### Shirahama in August

Every August the shop closed for its only holiday, three days, and the family went to **Shirahama** on the Wakayama coast, the same trip every year since 1998. The Kuroshio limited express from Tennoji took about two and a half hours, past rice fields, then plum orchards, then sudden flashes of blue sea, and Mizuki always sat on the right, the sea side going south, so she could see the water first. They stayed at the same small family inn with thin futons and a big shared bath, and did the same things in the same order, which is exactly what she loved about it: the white sand of Shirarahama beach, the evening walk to see the sun set through the hole in Engetsu island, the cliffs at Sandanbeki, and a whole day at **Adventure World**, the animal park with the pandas, which Mizuki photographed every year with a disposable camera and which were, every year, asleep.

These were the last years with all of them together on that trip. In 2002 Isamu and Chiyo came too, for the first and only time, and Isamu sat under a beach umbrella in a white vest and a fishing hat, timing everyone's swims on the Seiko and shouting the results. In 2003 Mizuki and Kenta built a sandcastle shaped like Koshien stadium in honour of the Tigers and Hiroshi photographed it from four angles. In 2004 she was fourteen and too old for sandcastles, and spent most of the beach day under the umbrella with Aya's borrowed copy of the NANA manga and a bottle of sunscreen, coming out only to swim, carefully, along the line of the shore. She's a careful swimmer still: she never forgot that summer in a cast in 1999, watching everyone else in the sea.

Holiday food was its own ritual: grilled turban shell and squid on sticks from the beachfront stalls, a box of local plums from the station shop that Yumiko negotiated down by "only" ten per cent, and, in the evening, sashimi at the inn so fresh that Hiroshi, a man of few words, said two in a row: "This is correct." Mizuki adopted the phrase, in its full Hiroshi sense: the highest praise a food can receive is not "delicious" but "correct".

### Keitai, emoji and the language of tiny pictures

For her fourteenth birthday in April 2004, Mizuki got a pale pink **flip phone**, a keitai. She had campaigned for eight months. Yumiko's terms were strict: mail only, calls for emergencies, no paid sites, and if the bill went over three thousand yen it would come out of her otoshidama. Within a week it had three straps hanging off it: a little bell, a mini Vulpix and a tiny plastic takoyaki that clacked against the hinge every time she moved. The *snap* of shutting a flip phone is still one of her favourite sounds in the world, and she has complained ever since that smartphones have no proper way to end an argument. "You can't slam a screen. You just tap it, like you're sorry."

The phone had a camera in the hinge, a small grainy one, and although she would never have called it photography, she used it constantly: the hotplate at closing time, Kenta asleep with his mouth open, the cherry blossom on the canal path, the sky over Tennoji. She'd stand very still, wait for someone to stop moving, and take one photo instead of five. Aya complained that taking a picture with Mizuki took longer than taking a bath. In hindsight Mizuki thinks this was the very beginning.

She and Aya sent dozens of mails a day, never calls (calls were expensive and also, Mizuki felt, "too loud for feelings"), all decorated with Japan's own invention, emoji: hearts, sweat drops, the bowl of ramen, little sparkles, a crescent moon for goodnight. And endless kaomoji, faces made of punctuation, like (^_^) and (T_T) and (>_<), with her own signature, a shy, blushing (*^^*). Yumiko, who couldn't read the tiny screen without her glasses, called it "the hieroglyphics" and asked Kenta to translate. Mizuki still believes that a message without an emoji sounds angry. When Scott sends her a plain "ok", she assumes she's in trouble, and she's told him so.

### Purikura, and her first portfolio

On Saturdays they went to the game centres in Tennoji and Namba, past the UFO catchers and the taiko drum games and the boys at the fighting cabinets, to the bright back corner full of **purikura** booths: photo-sticker machines with curtains printed with giant smiling girls, three or four hundred yen a go, five or six poses, then a frantic minute at the screen outside with a plastic pen adding stars, hearts, whiskers, the date and silly captions before the timer ran out.

Mizuki took it far more seriously than Aya, or than anyone. She worked out which machines had the softest light (the new ones at the back, never the old one by the door with the harsh flash), how to tilt her chin so her face didn't go round as a moon, and where to stand so she wasn't half in shadow behind Aya. Her posing rules were first tested in those booths: don't stare straight at the lens, turn your shoulders a little, put your weight on your back foot, and think of something funny just before the click, so your eyes smile too. Aya, who just wanted to draw cat ears on everyone, called her "Director-san".

The best purikura pose of 2004, and the one they did most, was Aya and Mizuki doing a manzai tsukkomi chop for the camera, Aya's hand frozen an inch from Mizuki's head and Mizuki's face in exaggerated shock. She's done the same pose in photo booths in Sydney with Fiona and Nana, and has had to explain it every time.

The stickers went into a notebook, a thick spiral-bound one with a Sanrio Cinnamoroll cover that she now says was "a mistake of taste but not of judgement". It holds three years of junior high: Aya, Saki, the art club girls, Kenta under protest, and one sticker of Fumiko, who agreed in the summer of 2004 to come into a booth in Tennoji "to see what the fuss is about", and was so startled by the flash that every frame shows her mid-blink, laughing. It's one of Mizuki's favourite pictures of her grandmother. The notebook lives in a drawer at her parents' place, with Mr Nakagawa's maths test. She calls it her first portfolio.

### Ayumi Hamasaki, and what she copied and refused

If Utada Hikaru was the music Mizuki listened to alone, **Ayumi Hamasaki** was the music she listened to with every other girl in Japan. In those years "Ayu" was everywhere: on the covers of the fashion magazines at the konbini, on giant posters in Namba, on the karaoke screens where every girl knew "Dearest" (the Inuyasha ending song, which made it count as anime too, Mizuki argued) and "Voyage" and, in 2004, "Moments". Mizuki was a little more devoted than she now admits. She bought the album *Memorial address* with birthday money and played it on a borrowed MD player on the walk to school, and she cried, privately, at the video for "Voyage", which was a short film and therefore, in her opinion, cinema.

Ayu was the queen of what people called the gal look in its glossier, post-ganguro form: light brown hair, big sunglasses pushed up on the head, pink lip gloss, nails done, a dozen accessories, fur when it was cold and very little when it wasn't. In Osaka, where people dress louder than in Tokyo anyway, the girls in Amemura did it with extra volume. Mizuki was fascinated and slightly frightened.

What she **copied**: the pink lip gloss (strawberry-flavoured, from the drugstore, worn only at weekends and wiped off before she got home, because Yumiko could detect lip gloss from across a room); the hand pose, fingers spread near the cheek, which she used in purikura for a year; big hair clips with rhinestones; the habit of pushing sunglasses up on her head even indoors, even at night, which Hiroshi watched without comment for a month and then said, "Is the ceiling too bright?"; and, most lastingly, the idea that you choose your look on purpose and wear it like you mean it.

What she **refused**: the tan, absolutely ("I'm from a family that cooks over a hotplate all day. I'm already warm enough"); false eyelashes, after trying one pair borrowed from Saki's sister and spending a whole afternoon feeling as though spiders were standing on her eyes; the platform boots, after she watched an older girl fall over in a pair on the Shinsaibashi arcade and decided "never have I seen a clearer warning from the universe"; the bleached hair, because school forbade it and because, as Aya pointed out, Mizuki with orange hair would look "like a takoyaki that went to Tokyo"; and anything with a giant brand logo on it. That last one was already a rule, the rule she still lives by: "No logo bigger than my hand." She had first said it at eleven, in front of a price label in Kintetsu; at fourteen, standing in front of a rack of shiny handbags in a Shinsaibashi department store, after Aya asked whether she'd rather be a person or an advertisement, it became a rule for life.

She stopped buying Ayu albums around the end of junior high, but she never stopped liking the ballads, and Ayu is still on her karaoke list. "Dearest" is her solo karaoke warm-up, "because if you can do that, your throat is open".

### Music in the headphones

Utada Hikaru stayed her real love: "Hikari", the theme of **Kingdom Hearts**, which came out on PS2 in March 2002 and which she played that summer with Final Fantasy characters turning up in Disney worlds ("like meeting your cousins at a theme park"); "Sakura Drops" in 2002; "Colors" in early 2003. She played "Hikari" until the CD skipped at the same place every time, and she still sings the skip, as a joke, when she does it at karaoke.

At home the shop radio still played enka for the older customers and Southern All Stars for Hiroshi, who hummed "Itoshi no Ellie" at the hotplate when he thought nobody was listening, and Yumiko's Takarazuka cassettes played on Wednesdays while she did the accounts. SMAP's "Sekai ni Hitotsu Dake no Hana", the song about every flower being unique, was so inescapable in 2003 that Isamu, who otherwise only sang war-era songs, learned the chorus and sang it to customers who complained about the wait. Aya, meanwhile, had found **Kanjani Eight**, the Kansai boy band, and in 2004, when they released their first CD singing in exaggerated Osaka-ben, she became a lifelong fan overnight. Mizuki's verdict was "half music, half comedy, which is very Osaka", and she was dragged along to buy the single on release day and complained the whole way, and knows all the words.

Then in April 2004 came **Ken Hirai**'s "Hitomi wo Tojite" ("Close Your Eyes"), the theme song of Crying Out Love in the Center of the World, a slow ballad that sold over a million copies and that Mizuki says she can't hear in public even now, "because it makes me cry in a way that is inconvenient".

### Games and anime: X-2, the one that cost money, and Kenta's dragon

When **Final Fantasy X-2** came out in March 2003 she bought it too, with the last of that year's otoshidama, mostly for the dresspheres, the costume changes that switched Yuna's job in the middle of battle. She admitted the story was "a bit like a music video", and Yuna had gone from praying to pop stardom, which some fans found shocking; Mizuki thought it was fair, after everything. She spent an embarrassing amount of time choosing the best-looking dressphere for each fight rather than the most useful one. It was an early sign of something permanent: in any game, she spends a ridiculous amount of time on clothes. Her Final Fantasy XIV glamours, years later, are the direct descendants.

**Final Fantasy XI** had come out in May 2002 for the PS2: an online game, with a monthly fee and a separate hard disk to buy. Mizuki wanted it more than anything. She made a careful presentation to her parents at the staff meal, with costs written out on the back of an order pad. Yumiko read the figures and said, "Every month? Forever? For a game?" Hiroshi said, "Like a Tigers season ticket, but you can't drink beer." The answer was no. She was furious for weeks. She now thinks it was the right decision, because "fourteen-year-old me would have lived in that game and failed every exam", and she got her revenge in 2013 when she started paying for XIV herself.

Kenta, now ten and eleven, had gone the other way. Where Mizuki was Final Fantasy, Kenta was **Dragon Quest**, and in November 2004 Dragon Quest VIII came out on PS2, five days after his eleventh birthday. He had saved for it since summer. He played it with the same methodical calm he now brings to systems engineering, mapping every dungeon and never running from a fight, and on the shared television that winter the siblings conducted a slow, polite war over whose game was better. Mizuki's position was that Dragon Quest was "a lovely game for people who like walking". Kenta's position was that Final Fantasy was "a fashion show with a plot". They're both still right, they say.

On Saturday evenings at six there was **Fullmetal Alchemist**, from October 2003, made by MBS, an Osaka station, which she considered a point of civic pride. She loved the brothers, and the ending of the first series, and watched the sadder episodes "through my cardigan sleeves". **One Piece** was still every week, and Nami was still the only sensible person on the ship. And in November 2004 she and Aya went to see Ghibli's **Howl's Moving Castle** at a cinema in Namba, and Mizuki spent the next week insisting the castle was "basically a yokai", a walking house made of junk with a fire demon inside, and that Fumiko would approve. Fumiko, when she saw it on video, did.

### May 2004: Crying Out Love, and the usher with the tissues

In May 2004 **Crying Out Love in the Center of the World** (*Sekai no Chūshin de, Ai o Sakebu*) opened in cinemas, adapted from Kyōichi Katayama's best-selling novel, and became a national craze known simply as "Sekachu". A man about to marry finds an old cassette tape and goes back to the small seaside town of his high school years, and the film moves between past and present through the tapes he and his first love recorded for each other, until you understand what happened to the girl he lost.

Mizuki, just fourteen, went with Aya on a Saturday afternoon to a cinema in Namba, with a bag of caramel popcorn and no idea what she was in for. She started crying about halfway through and didn't stop. Aya, beside her, held out for a while longer and then gave in too. By the end Mizuki was making a noise she describes as "a kettle with feelings", and as the credits rolled and Ken Hirai's song began, an usher came down the aisle, looked at her, and handed her a whole packet of tissues without a word. Aya has told this story at every important event of Mizuki's life since: her high school graduation, her leaving party before Sydney, and Kenta's wedding in 2021, which Mizuki could only attend by video call from Sydney.

What hooked her wasn't only the sadness. It was the structure: the past hidden inside the present, the cassette tapes as a slow-burning secret, the sense that the story was being unlocked rather than told. Romance with suspense in it, love with a ghost in the corner. It was Fumiko's storytelling again, but about people instead of yokai. She has looked for that kind of film ever since, and it's why her favourite films, Love Letter and later Your Name, all have a mystery folded into the love story.

There is a small strange footnote. The film's last scenes take place in Australia, in the red desert at Uluru, which the characters call the centre of the world. At fourteen Mizuki thought it looked like another planet. She has lived in Australia for eight years now and still hasn't been, and she says she's saving it, "because I'd cry, and there's nowhere to buy tissues".

That summer TBS ran a television drama version and the two of them watched every episode, Aya at her flat and Mizuki at hers, mailing each other emoji of crying faces during the adverts.

### Autumn 2004: Battle Royale in the hallway

That autumn, still fourteen, she was at a classmate's house with a group from the class when someone's older brother produced the DVD of **Battle Royale** (2000), the film in which a class of junior high students is sent to an island and forced to fight. It was rated R15, which was exactly why every boy in the year had talked about it for weeks, and why everyone pretended to be relaxed.

Mizuki lasted about halfway. Ringu had been frightening and she'd loved it; this was blood and cruelty presented as fun, and the people on screen were wearing her uniform. Quietly, without fuss, she said, "I'm getting a drink," went out to the hallway and sat on the step by the shoe cupboard with a glass of barley tea for the rest of the film, listening to the screams through the door. After twenty minutes Aya came out and sat down beside her, claiming she didn't like it either and wanted to look at the family's umbrella stand. Mizuki has always suspected this was kindness. Aya has never admitted it.

From then on she knew her line. Ghosts, dread, a twist that makes the room go cold: yes. Gore, and people hurting each other for entertainment: no. If something violent is supposed to be very good, she'll watch it with her fingers over her eyes and say so: "I'm watching this through my fingers, just so you know." When Kenta puts on an action film at New Year he still pauses it and asks, "Nee-chan, fingers?"

### Small disasters

Not everything in these years was cinema and stickers. In January 2004 she caught a proper flu in the week before the third-term tests, a week of fever under the kotatsu with a cold towel on her head, Yumiko bringing rice porridge with an umeboshi in it (she ate round the umeboshi even with a temperature of 39) and Fumiko sitting with her in the afternoons folding paper cranes in silence. She missed two tests, took them late, and did worse in maths than she ever had, which Mr Nakagawa said was "medically understandable and mathematically tragic".

In the spring of 2004 she lost her first keitai strap, the tiny takoyaki, somewhere between the shop and school, and walked the route three times looking for it. Kenta bought her a new one from a gachapon machine in Tennoji with his own money, and it was a squid instead, and she kept it anyway, and the capsule toy habit that now fills a shelf in her Pyrmont flat with tiny plastic food may have started right there.

And in the summer of 2004 she and Aya had their first real fight, over something so small neither can agree what it was. Aya says Mizuki told a boy in their class about Aya's crush. Mizuki says she only told him "a general direction". They didn't walk home together for nine days. It ended at Fumiko's, where both of them happened to turn up on the same afternoon, which Mizuki has always suspected was arranged, and Fumiko made them shell edamame side by side at the low table until one of them laughed. "Rice and pickles," Fumiko said, "fight in the same bowl."

### Winter 2005: Grandpa Isamu

In the early part of 2005, in her third term of the second year of junior high, Isamu began to complain of being tired, which nobody took seriously, because Isamu complained of everything. He still came to seat one every morning at ten. He still criticised the cabbage. On a cold weeknight in February he watched the news, told Chiyo the weather forecaster had no idea what he was talking about, wound the Seiko as he did every night, and went to bed. He didn't wake up. The doctor said his heart had simply stopped in his sleep. He was seventy-two.

Mizuki was fourteen. She remembers the phone ringing very early, before it was light, and the sound of her father on the stairs, and then her mother coming into the room she shared with Kenta and sitting on the edge of her futon, not saying anything for a long moment. It was the first time someone she saw every day had gone.

The shop closed. Hiroshi pulled the shutter down and Yumiko taped a small handwritten notice to it, and for the first time Mizuki could remember there was no sound of spatulas under her floor in the evening. Regulars came anyway, one by one, to stand in front of the shutter for a minute, or to knock and leave an envelope, or a bottle of sake, or, in the case of the taxi driver who always complained about the spring onions, a whole box of spring onions, which made Yumiko laugh and then cry.

The wake and the funeral were held at a funeral hall in Abeno over two days, the way most are in Japan, with incense, a Buddhist priest chanting, and a long line of people in black. Mizuki wore her school uniform, as Japanese students do at funerals, the navy sailor collar and the red scarf, and stood with Kenta behind their parents. So many people came that the hall ran out of chairs: suppliers, old customers who had been eating at that hotplate since the 1960s, men from the bath house, the fishmonger from Kuromon who had known Isamu for forty years, Mr Nakagawa in a black tie, and Aya with her mother, who stood at the back and caught Mizuki's eye exactly once, which was enough. Daisuke came from Kobe with his parents and stood beside her for a while without saying anything, as he had in 1995, which she understood perfectly.

Hiroshi said almost nothing for those two days. At the end, at the crematorium, there is an old custom in which the family pass what remains into the urn together, two people holding one pair of long chopsticks. Mizuki did it with her father, and his hands were steady and hers weren't. Afterwards, outside, in the cold, he said one sentence to her: "He'd have said the incense was too thick." It made her laugh, and she was grateful, and she thinks he knew she would be.

The shop reopened after a week. On the first night Hiroshi cooked with Isamu's old spatulas, the pair with the wooden handles worn pale, and the first okonomiyaki of the evening went not to a customer but onto a plate on seat one, with a can of coffee next to it, and stayed there until closing. Nobody sat on seat one for the rest of that year. Regulars who didn't know would start to sit down and another regular would stop them. Fumiko, who had lost her own husband in 1988, came to the shop every evening that month and sat with Chiyo at the end table, two widows with cups of tea, not talking much. Mizuki says it taught her that comfort is mostly sitting near someone.

### The old man's watch

A few weeks after the funeral, Chiyo came upstairs with a small cloth bundle and put it in Mizuki's hands. Inside was Isamu's Seiko: the steel case, the cream face gone a little yellow, the date window at three o'clock, the bracelet that pulled the hairs on his wrist. "He said it was to go to you," Chiyo said. "Because you were always late, and he said somebody in this family had to learn." Mizuki asked when he'd said it. Chiyo said, "Years ago. And every year since. He said it on New Year's Day."

It was much too big for her. The jeweller in the Abeno shopping arcade took out three links for free, because he'd known Isamu, and told her it was "a working man's watch, not a fashion watch", which she took as a recommendation. She wore it to school the next day, under her cardigan cuff, where teachers couldn't see it. It still ran slow by about two minutes a week. She started setting it every Sunday against the radio time signal, as he had.

She wears it still, every day, in Sydney, and calls it her "old man's watch". It's been serviced twice, the strap swapped once, and the face is more yellow than ever. People at the ramen shop occasionally ask why a woman who loves clothes wears a heavy old steel watch with everything, and she says, "Because it says I'm a disaster, and it's right." When she's running late, which is still often, she taps the glass the way he did. She has never been on time in her life, and she thinks he'd be pleased she's trying.

That August was Isamu's first Obon, hatsubon, and the family lit a white lantern for him at the door. Fumiko made the cucumber horse and the aubergine cow, and Mizuki, fifteen, put the Seiko on the altar beside his photograph for the three days, "so he could check what time it was when he came home".

### Spring 2005: the school trip to Tokyo

In May 2005, at the start of her third and final year of junior high, the whole year went on the **school trip** to Tokyo: three days and two nights, the shinkansen from Shin-Osaka, matching bags, a printed booklet with every minute planned, and a teacher counting heads every twenty minutes and losing count every time. Mizuki had never been further east than Nagoya. She had been reluctant to go so soon after Isamu, and Chiyo had told her firmly that he'd have been furious if she stayed home "to look after a shop that has three adults in it already". She took a disposable camera with twenty-seven shots and was so careful with it that she came home with one frame still unused, "for emergencies".

They did what every Osaka school trip did: Asakusa and the great red lantern of Sensoji, where she bought Fumiko a charm; the Diet building, where a guide spoke for an hour and Aya fell asleep standing up; Tokyo Tower, which Mizuki loyally declared "only a bit taller than Tsutenkaku, and less friendly"; and a free afternoon in groups in Harajuku, where they walked up Takeshita-dori in their sailor uniforms past crepe stands and gothic lolita girls and shops she'd only seen in magazines. Harajuku, she admitted, was "more interesting than Amemura, a little bit, but don't tell anyone in Osaka".

She came home full of opinions. Tokyo trains were silent, and nobody talked to anybody, and when she said something silly to a station attendant on the platform he just looked at her. "Nobody does tsukkomi. You say something funny and they wait for you to finish." People stood on the left of the escalator, not the right as in Osaka, and she was tutted at twice before she worked it out. The udon at a station stand came in soy broth so dark she thought there had been a mistake in the kitchen, and she defended clear, golden Kansai dashi to her whole group for the rest of the trip. On the second evening the class was taken to Tsukishima to try **monjayaki**, Tokyo's runny, scraped-off-the-griddle cousin of okonomiyaki, and she ate it politely and phoned home that night, from a hotel corridor, to report to her father that it was "like okonomiyaki that gave up". Hiroshi repeated it to customers for years, and it's still on his list of jokes, which has about four things on it.

Her lifelong position was set on that trip: Tokyo is the smart cousin who wears a suit and has a good job, and you respect him; Osaka is the funny cousin who brings food and tells you your haircut is terrible, and you love her.

### Tokyo Disneyland: too clean, but the churros were correct

The third day was **Tokyo Disneyland**, which she points out, every time, is actually in Chiba. She'd been to Nara Dreamland, Hirakata Park, Expoland and USJ, and she considered herself a theme park expert at fifteen, with standards.

The class arrived at opening with a plan drawn up by Aya in four colours. They did Splash Mountain and got soaked, which reminded her of the Jurassic Park ride at USJ in 2001 and made her briefly homesick; Big Thunder Mountain twice; Space Mountain in the dark, where she screamed the whole way and came out saying it was "fine"; and Pooh's Hunny Hunt, where the honey pots move on their own without tracks and Mizuki, who knew nothing about how it worked, decided it was "the most magic thing I've ever seen, including Final Fantasy". Her favourite, inevitably, was the **Haunted Mansion**: spooky, not scary, ghosts at a party, a ballroom of transparent dancers, and a cast member at the entrance with a perfectly grave face. "That's a yokai house," she told Aya. "That's just a yokai house with better lighting."

But the thing she couldn't get over was how clean it was. A girl dropped a popcorn kernel and a cleaner in white swept it up before it had finished bouncing. Everyone was polite, the queues were perfectly straight, the bins were spotless, the music came out of the flowerbeds, and nobody was shouting. Coming from Osaka, where a theme park is meant to be loud, a bit sticky and full of aunties arguing with their husbands, it was almost eerie. "It was like being inside an advert," she said. "Very beautiful. A little bit suspicious. Too clean. Where do they hide the mess?"

And then there were the **churros**. She bought one from a cart near the castle in the afternoon: long, star-shaped, hot, crisp outside and soft inside, rolled in cinnamon sugar. She ate it standing up and walked straight back to the cart and bought a second one with the money she'd been saving for a keyring. Her verdict, delivered to the whole family at the counter the next evening and repeated faithfully ever since, was the one now in the family canon: "Too clean. But the churros were correct." Hiroshi, recognising his own highest praise, nodded slowly, as if a matter of state had been settled.

She brought home a Haunted Mansion keyring for Kenta, a box of chocolate crunch for the shop, a tin of biscuits for Chiyo, and nothing for herself except the photos. When the disposable camera came back from the developer at the camera shop by Tennoji station, the best picture was not of a ride or the castle but of Aya, in uniform, mid-bite, with sugar on her chin and her eyes shut against the sun, not knowing she was being photographed. Mizuki looked at it for a long time. She couldn't have said why it was better than the posed ones. A year later, with her father's camera in her hands, she'd find out.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes Final Fantasy X and Yuna**, "polite brave"; sings "Suteki da ne". Her first FF of her own, bought with otoshidama at Den Den Town after comparing three shops. Defends Tidus's laugh forever. Likes Auron because he's like her father.
- **Hates blitzball**; gave up on the Thunder Plains lightning at 34 ("respectable, for a child, in a thunderstorm").
- **Dresspheres and clothes in games** (FFX-2): she'll always choose the best-looking outfit over the most useful one; the ancestor of her FFXIV glamours.
- **Keeps two backups of everything**: Kenta formatted her FFX memory card in 2002.
- **Slow, quiet horror and twists**: Ringu at Saki's; Sadako "had a plan". Can't sleep with a TV facing the bed; nervous of ringing landlines; loses interest if the monster appears too early ("Fumiko would never").
- **No gore or cruelty as entertainment**: Battle Royale, the hallway, the barley tea. Watches "through my fingers"; Kenta asks, "Nee-chan, fingers?"
- **Aching romance with a mystery inside it**: Crying Out Love (the usher's tissues, Ken Hirai's song she can't hear in public). Saving Uluru, "because I'd cry".
- **Flip phones and emoji**: misses the snap of a flip phone ("you can't slam a screen"); her kaomoji (*^^*); a message without emoji sounds angry.
- **Purikura and posing**: soft light, turned shoulders, think of something funny before the click; her purikura notebook is her "first portfolio".
- **Clothes shopping in Shinsaibashi and Amemura**: on foot, on a budget, never the first shop, with an honest friend; oversized denim since autumn 2003; pastel cardigans with sleeves over her hands.
- **Copied from Ayu**: lip gloss, rhinestone clips, sunglasses on her head, choosing a look on purpose. **Refused**: the tan, false eyelashes ("spiders standing on my eyes"), platform boots, bleached hair, giant logos. **"No logo bigger than my hand."**
- **Leopard print on other people**: "a personality, and I don't have it yet."
- **Music**: Utada ("Hikari", skip and all), Ayumi Hamasaki ballads ("Dearest" is her karaoke warm-up), Ken Hirai; teases Aya about Kanjani Eight and knows all the words.
- **The Hanshin Tigers and big public sports nights**: the 2003 pennant, Dotonbori jumpers, her father's wet eyes. Slightly unforgiving of Fukuoka.
- **Shirahama**: same inn, same order, sunset through Engetsu; sleeping pandas; careful swimming.
- **"Correct" as the highest praise for food**: from Hiroshi at Shirahama; applied to the churros.
- **Food**: cold leftover okonomiyaki ("like pizza"), clear Kansai udon dashi, Pon de Rings at Mister Donut, churros. **Can't eat natto** ("Kansai people are not built for natto"); still eats round umeboshi; monjayaki is "okonomiyaki that gave up".
