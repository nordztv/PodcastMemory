## Chapter 14: Hana, Tasmania and a cinema in the city (2024 to September 2025)

### Summer 2024: thirty three and settled

The new year of 2024 found Mizuki in the Pyrmont flat, thirty three, six years into Sydney and, for the first time, feeling that her life there had a shape that would hold. The trip home in 2023 had tied the rope back on to Osaka; now she could be in Sydney without feeling she had abandoned anyone, which made Sydney much nicer. On the balcony the mint, there since 2022, still grew like a rumour beside the shiso, and the chilli plant that produced exactly four chillies in its whole life and which she defended against all criticism ("it is trying its best, like Dad at a wedding").

The shop at World Square was in its steady years. Mr Kondo, fifty two going on fifty three, still wore the towel round his head and still hummed when worried: a low, tuneless hum by the stockpot meant the pork order was late, a higher one meant his mother in Sapporo had not answered her phone. As shift lead Mizuki did the roster, trained the casuals, spoke to suppliers in "my hotel voice" and handled anyone who wanted a manager. Mr Kondo said she ran the front the way her mother ran hers; her mother said, "Of course. I trained her."

**Lily**, finished at UTS, was still doing shifts and announcing a new plan every few weeks (marketing, a master's, a year in Seoul, "maybe I just make really good TikToks"). Mizuki took the big-sister job seriously: making sure Lily ate something besides staff noodles, not noticing when she cried in the storeroom over a boy, and once marching her to a bank to open a savings account "because you cannot live on bubble tea and hope". The regulars came and went: the Wednesday tradie in hi-vis who called her "boss"; the Monday shoyu lady, who sent a Christmas card addressed "To the Japanese girl who knows my order" even though she came in every Monday; and new ones, two nurses still in scrubs, a programmer who read manga at the counter, and a big, gentle security guard from one of the towers who ordered two bowls "for strength".

Her week held its shape: the walk over Pyrmont Bridge with the camera, the lunch rush, Tsukimi Noodles raids on Tuesdays and Thursdays, the Wednesday face mask, the Saturday fish market, Sunday okonomiyaki never pressed down. Once a month there was brunch with **Fiona** in Camperdown, at a café whose breakfast Fiona criticised in detail and finished to the crumb, and most Saturdays the K-pop dance class with **Nana**, in a studio full of girls ten years younger who could actually do the moves.

### January 2024: the Hottest 100 and a summer of anime

Australia Day meant the triple j Hottest 100 on the balcony with cold barley tea and a notebook, scoring how many songs she knew (seven, a personal best) and hoping, as always in vain, for Tame Impala, "music for walking home in the heat".

At home, though, one Japanese song took over. Creepy Nuts' "Bling-Bang-Bang-Born", the opening of the anime *Mashle*, became the kind of hit where everyone in Japan does the dance. Kenta sent a video of their father behind the hotplate, very deliberately not doing it while a group of high school girls at the counter did it at him; Hiroshi's face did not move at all. Mizuki watched it about forty times. She learned the dance with Nana and performed it once in the staff room; Mr Kondo said, "Very good. Now the bins," which she has decided was a review.

She was watching *Frieren*, "a ghost story where nobody is a ghost", and *Delicious in Dungeon*, about adventurers who cook the monsters, which she treated as a cooking show; when a customer asked if the chashu was "real pork", she very nearly told him it was a walking mushroom.

### February 2024: the Zf

For nearly six years in Sydney she had photographed with two things: her phone, which she used the way most people do, and her father's Nikon FM2, which she used the way her father had taught her, slowly, one frame at a time, with film developed at a small lab in the city. She loved the FM2 more than any object she owned. But film had become expensive (a roll of black-and-white and the developing came to more than a long lunch), and a phone, she said, "takes the photo for you, and then you have nothing to be proud of".

When Nikon announced the Zf in September 2023, she saw the first pictures of it on her break and made a noise in the staff room that Lily still imitates, a sort of high, strangled "eeeh?" that sounds like a kettle with an opinion. It looked almost exactly like the FM2: the same shape, the same silver and black, and, most importantly, real dials on top for shutter speed, ISO and exposure compensation, so you could set the camera by feel and see your settings with the camera switched off, the way her father had. It even had a small dedicated switch for black and white. "They made a camera for someone who learned in a darkroom," she told Lily, who did not know what a darkroom was, and was told, at length.

She did not buy it at once: Tanakas do not buy things the week they come out, because "the price drops and then you feel stupid twice". She stuck the price on the fridge next to Miho's thank-you card and saved: fewer brunches, the Christmas bonus, no new clothes for three months (her greatest sacrifice since moving to Australia). In February 2024 she bought it at a camera shop in the city, in the kit with the small 40mm lens with the retro silver ring, a lens that sees roughly the way your eyes do, "for photos of life, not of things". The very young man at the counter offered to explain the dials. She said, as politely as she could, "My father taught me these dials in 2006 on a camera from 1982," and he wrapped the box very carefully.

That night she put the Zf next to the FM2 on her shelf, the old camera and its grandchild, and photographed them side by side with her phone, and sent it to her father. He replied the next morning, Osaka time, with one word: "Kakkoii." Cool. She stared at the word for some time. She considers it the highest praise she has ever received from him, higher than his reaction to her school prize photograph of Fumiko's hands, which had been "Mm". She told her mother, who said, "He showed it to three customers," which was even better.

The first photo she took with the Zf was on the walk to work the next morning: the ibis on the rubbish bin at Tumbalong Park, in black and white, looking like a disgraced Egyptian god at dawn. She still thinks it is one of her best.

Learning it was "like cooking in someone else's kitchen". It could practically see in the dark, so she could shoot the Darling Harbour lights on the water handheld at night; it found people's eyes by itself, which she found useful and slightly creepy ("always watching the face, like a Kansai auntie at a wedding"). She set it up to be as much like the FM2 as she could: the dials doing the work, black and white on the switch, the little screen turned in so she would not check every shot. "If you check every photo, you stop seeing the street." The FM2 stayed on the shelf with a roll of black-and-white for special days, one roll a month, a promise she mostly kept. The Zf went everywhere.

### February 2024: Final Fantasy VII Rebirth, and the exception to the rule

Final Fantasy is the one exception to her rule about modern games (too long, too loud, too many menus), and *Final Fantasy VII Rebirth*, out on the PlayStation 5 on 29 February 2024, tested the exception hard. She had watched the original over Daisuke's shoulder at Fumiko's in 1997, and it was sacred. Her verdict on *Rebirth*: the story parts were wonderful and the rest was a theme park with too many queues. She loved Costa del Sol and the Gold Saucer rebuilt, and the party's banter, but there were so many towers, card games, side games and chocobo races that by the third region she was keeping a list on a notepad "like a shopping list at Kuromon". She finished it in June, cried at the ending as she had known she would, and complained, as she had also known she would, that it had taken her four months. Kenta, a Dragon Quest man, sent a single laughing sticker.

### Waiting for the baby

All through the summer and into the autumn of 2024, the family group chat (still called "Mizuki's barefoot country" from her first week in Sydney) had one subject: Miho's baby, due in April.

Kenta had told her the news the previous autumn, in the most Kenta way possible: a photo, sent without comment, of a tiny pair of white socks on the counter of his and Miho's flat in Tennoji. Mizuki looked at the photo for a full minute, thinking her brother had started a new and alarming hobby, before she understood. Then she rang him, at a time that was inconvenient for everyone, and screamed, and Kenta held the phone away from his ear and said, "Yes. Yes. April. Yes. Please stop," and she could hear Miho laughing in the background.

Her little brother, the boy who played the sprite in Secret of Mana and stole her rice crackers, was going to be a father. Under the excitement she noticed a small ache (thirty three, single since 2017, the other side of the world) and was honest enough to name it to Aya one night. Aya said, "You'll be the aunty who sends strange presents from Australia and teaches her bad Osaka jokes. That's the best job in the family." Mizuki said Aya was right, which she does not often say.

So she bought things: tiny Australian clothes with koalas and kookaburras, which her mother thought "very foreign"; a Vulpix plush, because a Tanaka child must start with the correct Pokémon; and a picture book of gentle yokai stories, a kappa and a tanuki and an umbrella yokai, for when the baby was older, "so I can tell them the proper way, like Obaachan did". She wrote in the front of it, in careful Japanese: "From Aunty Mizuki in Sydney. Ask me about the umbrella one."

### Easter 2024: Tasmania

Tasmania had been on her list since 2018, when Fiona came back from Hobart calling it "the only bit of Australia that's got the weather right", meaning cold. With the Zf new and the baby not due until mid-April, Mizuki took the Easter long weekend and two days' leave and decided it was now or never. Fiona said yes at once, being the only one who could drive on the left "without praying". Nana said yes before Mizuki had finished the sentence, because Nana said yes to everything; it was her best and most alarming quality. They flew to Hobart on the Thursday evening before Good Friday, at the end of March: Mizuki with both cameras, too many layers and a borrowed thermal; Nana with a suitcase twice anyone's size and her nails painted with tiny Tasmanian devils; Fiona with a thermos and the air of a woman going home to a colder country.

**Hobart and the Salamanca market.** Hobart in the morning was cold and clean and smelled of woodsmoke and the sea, and Mizuki, on the waterfront in her denim jacket, beret and borrowed thermal, declared she had found the one place in Australia with proper seasons. Above the city, kunanyi, Mount Wellington, sat with cloud on its head like the towel on Mr Kondo's.

On Easter Saturday they went to the Salamanca market along the old sandstone warehouses of Salamanca Place, a proper market of people selling what they made or grew, and it felt like Saturdays at Kuromon with Fumiko: walk slowly, taste everything, buy one good thing. Her one good thing was a jar of leatherwood honey, strong and almost perfumed, which she discussed like fine wine. Then a dark green merino beanie, because her ears were cold, and a little Huon pine box that smelled faintly of pepper, and she stopped counting. She approached a Tasmanian scallop pie, scallops in curry sauce in pastry, with deep Osaka suspicion (curry belongs on rice, seafood belongs alone with a little soy), took one bite, went silent, and finished it in four. "This should not work. It works. I am very confused and I want another one."

**MONA.** On Easter Sunday they took the camouflage-painted ferry up the Derwent to MONA, the private museum dug into a sandstone cliff, which Fiona called "mental, but in a good way" and Nana called "very you, very weird". Her verdict, delivered on the ferry home and for years afterwards: "too much".

She tries to be fair. The building was extraordinary, like descending into a temple cave. She loved one work without reservation, a waterfall of words, where falling drops of water spelled out words from the news for a moment and were gone; she stood in front of it for twenty minutes, could not make the shot work because every word vanished before you could read it, and decided that was the point. "Like the news. Like everything. Obaachan would have understood that one." The rest was too loud, too dark and too keen to shock: a famous machine that "makes the thing machines should never make" (she went in, understood, came straight out), and rooms about death and bodies she walked through with her fusion-sushi face. "I like a ghost story. I don't like a museum that grabs your arm and says, aren't you shocked? No. I am hungry and I want to go back to the scallop pie." Fiona called her "a wee conservative". Mizuki concedes it is the most memorable museum she has ever visited and that she will never go back. "Like a bowl of ramen with every topping. Each thing is good, maybe. Together, I need to lie down."

**Cradle Mountain.** On Easter Monday they drove north in a small hire car, Fiona at the wheel, Nana choosing the music, Mizuki in the back with the camera on her knees: green hills, sheep, old stone churches, then forest and a long climbing road, and one stop because Nana was carsick on the bends and stood in a paddock for ten minutes while a cow looked at her.

They stayed two nights in a cabin near the national park, with a wood heater Fiona lit expertly and Mizuki watched like television. At night the stars were so many that Mizuki, a child of Osaka's orange sky, lay on the cold deck in every layer she owned and did not speak for a long time. She tried to photograph them and gave up: "the camera could see them, which is not the same as being there."

Next morning they walked the Dove Lake circuit under the jagged peaks, through mossy rainforest, to the little wooden boatshed every visitor photographs. She waited forty minutes for the cloud to lift off the mountain behind it; it lifted for about ninety seconds, and she got the shot, "the most patient photo I have ever taken". Then the mist came back and they walked inside a cloud; she said it was the forest in a Ghibli film where you meet a spirit, Fiona said it was Scotland, and they agreed both were true. The fagus, the native beech that turns gold in autumn, had not turned yet; a ranger said come back in late April. She put it on her list for one day.

She also lost something there: she put the Zf's lens cap on a rock to photograph a stream and left it behind, and only noticed at the car, with the mist in. The replacement cost "a criminal amount for a piece of plastic", and she still thinks of it on its rock in the moss. "Maybe a wombat has it now. He's using it as a hat."

Because what she remembers best is the wombats. In the late afternoon, on the boardwalk across the button grass at Ronny Creek, they were everywhere, grazing like small furry boulders, utterly uninterested in humans. She lay flat on the boardwalk for a portrait at wombat height, the only correct height, and one came within two metres, looked at her with total indifference and went on eating: square, solid, unhurried, deeply unimpressed, "the most Hiroshi-like animal I have ever met". She sent the photo to her father captioned "your Australian brother". He did not reply; her mother said he laughed out loud.

They drove to Launceston on the Wednesday and flew home. Her verdict: the best Australian holiday she had ever had. Cold air, honest food, old buildings that weren't pretending, real seasons and wombats. "The part of Australia that would be fine in Japan." MONA excepted.

### 14 April 2024: Hana

Eleven days after Tasmania, and four days after her thirty fourth birthday (a quiet one: an izakaya in the city with Nana and Lily, and a video call in which her father said "happy birthday" and then talked about the Tigers' bullpen), her phone lit up at five in the morning on Sunday 14 April.

It was a message from Kenta. There was one photo and no words. In the photo was a very small, very red, very cross-looking baby in a white hospital blanket, with a full head of black hair and her fists up like a boxer.

Mizuki sat up in bed in the dark and looked at it for a long time. Then she cried, the good kind, and then she laughed, because it was the most Kenta thing he had ever done. Her brother had become a father and announced it to his only sister in the manner of a man sending a photo of a parking space. Ten minutes later a second message came: "Hana. 3.1 kg. Both fine." Then, after a pause that Mizuki could feel across the Pacific: "She looks like you when you are angry."

Her name was **Hana**, flower: simple, old-fashioned, easy to write, easy to say in both countries. Mizuki thought it was perfect. "Short, strong and nobody in Australia will spell it wrong. Well. Fewer people."

Her mother, at the hospital all night, sounded happier and more tired than Mizuki had ever heard her: Kenta had gone silent and very pale, Miho had been "a samurai", and Hiroshi had sat in the corridor with his hands on his knees for four hours, said nothing at all, and then, given the baby, held her very carefully and said, "Mada mada," the Tigers fans' "not yet, more to come", which nobody in the family fully understands in that context and everybody agrees was correct.

Mizuki could not go home; her leave and a good deal of her money had gone on Tasmania. On Monday, in the quiet hour, she showed the photo at the shop. Mr Kondo studied it and said, "Strong baby. Good fists. Sapporo fists." Lily said, "Oh my God, she's angry, I love her." The two-bowl security guard asked to see and said, "She looks like a little boss." Mizuki has called her niece "my small boss" ever since.

For the next year Hana was a person on a phone, in photos Kenta sent without words: Hana asleep on Miho; Hana in the koala onesie, looking betrayed; Hana at the shop in her grandmother's arms behind the till, regulars crowding round; Hana's first real smile, sent with the single word "there". Miho asked for tips on photographing a baby and got an essay (window light, get down to her level, no flash, talk to her and then stop talking). On Sunday calls Mizuki discovered the one thing that always made Hana laugh: a full Sailor Moon pose, right up close to the screen, "in the name of the moon". It worked every time. Her father, off-camera, could sometimes be heard trying it too. The parcel she posted in May (the yokai book, the Vulpix, Tim Tams for Kenta, leatherwood honey for her parents) was a success: the Vulpix became, according to Miho, Hana's favourite thing to chew.

### Winter 2024: flu, the shop and the long nights

In early June 2024 the winter flu came through the shop. Lily got it first, and then one Wednesday Mizuki woke with aching bones and a head full of wet cotton wool. A Tanaka does not take a sick day "unless there is blood or a typhoon", so she set off anyway and got as far as Pyrmont Bridge before she had to hold the rail. Mr Kondo took one look at her, said "No," in a voice she had never heard from him, and sent her home in a taxi he paid for. She spent four days under every blanket she owned with Japanese dramas on the laptop. Lily, recovered and "immune", delivered his miso broth and a note: "Eat. Sleep. Don't come in." The best thing she ate all winter, she says, "too strong for normal people, perfect for flu".

Her mother prescribed rice porridge with umeboshi, ginger tea with honey, and a long green onion tied round the neck (Fumiko's cure, which nobody in the family has ever done and everybody recommends). Mizuki did the porridge and the tea and refused the onion. The worst part was being ill alone in a small flat: at three in the morning, feverish, she remembered being small and ill above the shop, her mother coming up between customers with a cold towel. "When you are sick in a foreign country, it stops being a foreign country and starts being far away." She was back at work after five days, thinner and crosser. Lily said she looked "like a Victorian ghost". Mizuki said thank you.

### July 2024: Dawntrail

**Dawntrail**, the fifth expansion of Final Fantasy XIV, opened for early access on Friday 28 June 2024 and launched properly on Tuesday 2 July. Mizuki had booked two days off months before, and Mr Kondo had sighed and written it on the roster, having learned she would be useless otherwise. Tsukimi Noodles held a launch party in the FC house in the Lavender Beds; **Kazu**, the leader, whose raids end at half past ten sharp on school nights, stayed up until one, which the FC considered an act of great personal courage. Aya logged on from Osaka with her Paladin, and they ran the first dungeons in fast Osaka-ben on voice chat while the Australians tried to follow.

The graphics update meant Mizu Nyan's short blue Sailor Mercury hair finally looked like hair rather than "a plastic helmet from a toy shop". Mizuki took about two hundred screenshots of her own character the first night and sent the best to her father, who did not understand it and replied "kakkoii" again, presumably out of habit.

Her honest opinion: a beautiful holiday with a slow start and a wonderful ending. She loved the new continent of Tural, its colours, music and food, a sunny place after years of the end of the world. The first half, a contest of trials to choose a nation's next ruler, was too gentle after Endwalker. "It's a school trip. Lovely, very educational, everyone eats a lot, but I already knew who would win." She defended Wuk Lamat, the young, clumsy, sincere heroine whom many players found annoying, because she liked a heroine who keeps trying and gets things wrong and keeps going ("she is a little bit Osaka; loud, and means well"). But she agreed the pacing tested everyone's patience, including hers.

Then came the second half, the city of the dead and its memories, and the quiet goodbyes in it, and she cried exactly as she had at the end of Shadowbringers. It was her favourite kind of story: gentle and ghostly, about people who are gone but still loved, and about letting them rest. She played the last part on a Thursday night after work and sat on her cushion afterwards in the dark for some minutes. "They put the good part at the end, like Obaachan's stories. You have to be patient. Most people are not patient."

She tried the new Pictomancer, a painter who fights with a brush, loved it for about a week, and went back to White Mage, "where I belong, healing you people who stand in the fire". Her ranking of the expansions, settled that autumn and never changed since: Endwalker, Shadowbringers, Heavensward (for the snow and the music), Dawntrail, then Stormblood.

The one unhappy part of Dawntrail was not the story but the Sydney ping. Playing on a Japanese server from Australia had always added a small delay, and in the new fights, with their fast mechanics, she died more than she liked, and blamed every single death on the ping. Aya, who was playing in Osaka with no delay, said, "It's not the ping, it's you standing in the orange." This became a running argument that has never been resolved.

### Spring 2024: magpies, jacarandas and Dandadan

September in Sydney meant two things: the magpies swooping, and the jacarandas coming. In her first spring a magpie had swooped her, and every September since she had walked under certain trees with her umbrella open, rain or no rain. In 2024 she added a new tactic, recommended by Lily: drawing eyes on the back of her cap, because magpies supposedly don't swoop if they think you can see them. She drew the eyes with a white marker. The magpie swooped anyway. She now says the eyes "were not convincing enough, they were too kawaii".

The jacarandas came at the end of October and in November, and she did her annual "purple hanami" photo walk, this time with the Zf, through the streets of Kirribilli and McMahons Point where the trees make purple tunnels over the footpaths. She shot the whole walk in colour, because there was no point photographing a jacaranda in black and white. "It's like photographing a Takarazuka show with the lights off."

In October the anime *Dandadan* began: a schoolgirl from a family of spirit mediums and a boy who believes in aliens, fighting ghosts and aliens at once, including a yokai called Turbo Granny who chases cars at high speed. Mizuki said it was the first new anime in years that understood yokai properly: frightening and ridiculous at the same time, the way Fumiko told them. "Obaachan would have liked Turbo Granny. They have the same attitude to traffic." The opening song, Creepy Nuts again, "Otonoke", went straight on to her walking playlist. She watched the violent parts through her fingers, as promised.

She also caught up on *Shōgun*, the American series set in Japan in 1600, expecting to wince at bad Japanese, and found the Japanese real and the bowing correct. Too much blood and nobody tells a single joke in ten hours ("in 1600 Osaka people also made jokes"), but eight out of ten, higher than any ramen in Sydney.

### October 2024: the leak

The worst thing that happened to Mizuki in 2024, and, she says, the hardest small loss of her whole life in Australia, happened on a wet Tuesday in October while she was at work.

Her building was an older brick block, three storeys, no lift, with plumbing that the strata committee described in its notices as "original", which she had learned meant "very old and nobody wants to pay". Above her lived a young couple she nodded to on the stairs. On that Tuesday, while everyone was out, a hot water pipe inside the wall between their bathroom and her bedroom gave way, quietly, and leaked for several hours down inside the wall and into the back of her built-in wardrobe.

She came home at half past seven to a smell of damp and a dark stain spreading down the wall beside the wardrobe and across the carpet. She opened the wardrobe door, and the floor of it was standing in water.

At the bottom of the wardrobe, where she had kept it since she moved to Pyrmont in 2021 because it was the only dark, cool place in the flat, was a cardboard box of film negatives. Not all of her negatives: the most precious ones from Japan, including the negative of her school prize photograph of Fumiko shelling peas and most of her Osaka rolls, were safe in Abeno, in a biscuit tin in her mother's cupboard, where she had left them in 2018 because she did not trust a suitcase with them. But the box held almost everything she had shot on the FM2 since arriving in Sydney, and above all her rolls from 2018: the first year, the year everything was new.

The box had been sitting in water for hours. When she lifted it, the bottom fell out.

The agent's emergency line and the strata manager both gave her recorded messages. The young couple upstairs, horrified, turned their water off at the mains and came down with towels and apologies she kept refusing, because it was the pipe's fault, not theirs. Then she sat on her kitchen floor until two in the morning with negatives laid out on every towel she owned, peeling the sleeves apart one by one. Fiona, on a night shift, talked her through it on her break like a nurse at a bedside. In the morning she rang her father, who had developed film in a tank in the shop's back room in the 1980s: rinse them in clean water, hang them to dry, don't touch the emulsion, don't hope too much. Then: "Photos are for looking at. You looked at them. That counts." For Hiroshi it was a long and very kind speech, and she cried more at that than at anything else.

Most of it could not be saved. Negatives left for hours pressed in wet sleeves stick to the plastic and to each other, and the image comes away. At the lab in the city, the older photographer with reading glasses on a cord who had developed her rolls since 2018 went through them with her, gently and honestly. A few strips from 2019 and 2021 could be rescued; almost nothing from 2018. Gone: her first week in Glebe, Bronte on her first Australian Christmas, the old men at chess in the park, Rafa barbecuing, Bia dancing in the kitchen, the fruit bats over Jubilee Park, the barefoot man in the supermarket (photographed from behind, as evidence), and Fiona in her paper crown. What survived she clung to: small, soft scans the lab had made at the time, a handful of prints, including the very first photo she took in Australia, the fence and the gum tree from the bus window, safe on her fridge under a Vulpix magnet, and her phone photos from those months, "not the same, but at least they remember".

The pipe was fixed within a week; the wall was opened, dried by a dehumidifier that roared for six days "like a small aeroplane", and replastered. The landlord's insurance covered the repairs; nobody's covered six years of film. The couple upstairs left wine and a card, "We are so sorry about your photos", which she kept. She was quiet for weeks. Lily noticed first, because Mizuki stopped arguing about K-pop, and brought her a bubble tea every morning for a week without saying why. Mr Kondo hummed near her and once, very awkwardly, put a hand on her shoulder in the storeroom: "Things, you can lose. The year, you keep." Aya rang every night. Her mother sent okonomiyaki sauce, rice crackers and a note: "You still have the eyes that took them."

She talks about it with care. She knows it was only film, and that people lose far more; it does not compare to losing Fumiko or the years away from home. But it was the hardest small loss of her time in Australia, because it took her first year, the year she saw everything for the first time. "Those rolls were the Sydney I saw before I understood it. I can't see it like that again. Nobody can. Now I have to remember it the old-fashioned way, like Obaachan remembered Grandpa whistling at every tram stop."

Now every roll is scanned at full resolution and saved in three places, and her negatives live in archival sleeves in a sealed box on a high shelf. She touches the wall behind the wardrobe now and then without meaning to. And she started, that November, a small project with the Zf: going back to the places she had photographed in 2018, the Glebe terraces, the supermarket on Glebe Point Road, Jubilee Park at dusk, Bronte on a Sunday, and photographing them again in black and white. She calls it "Glebe, again". It is not the same. She says that is fine, because neither is she.

### Christmas 2024 and New Year

By December she was mostly herself again, helped by the end-of-year rush and her tradition of a strawberry shortcake from the Japanese bakery, carried home on the bus on her knees "like a newborn". Christmas Day was Bronte with Fiona and Nana: prawns, shortcake on a picnic blanket, a swim between the flags. On the evening call Hana, eight months old and sitting up, laughed at the Sailor Moon pose on cue and hit the table with a spoon, which her grandmother called "the Morita side".

Kenta was playing the HD-2D remake of *Dragon Quest III*, out in November, every night after Hana's bedtime, and sending wordless screenshots, which reopened the oldest argument of their childhood. She told him Dragon Quest was "a very good game for people who like to be told what to do"; he said Final Fantasy was "a very good film with some walking in it". Their mother told them both to grow up. Meanwhile TWICE released "Strategy" in December, and Lily, a Red Velvet loyalist, admitted after a week of it in the shop that it was "catchy, unfortunately". Mizuki wrote the date down.

On New Year's Eve she ate toshikoshi soba and watched the Sydney fireworks on television, photographing the one corner of them visible from her balcony ("my one-chair fireworks"), and two hours later, at midnight in Osaka, rang her parents for the temple bells.

### Early 2025: thirty five, and a ticket home

The new year began with a plan. The Osaka Expo would open on 13 April 2025 on Yumeshima, the artificial island in Osaka Bay that she had seen as a building site in 2023, and run until October. Hana would be walking, or nearly. Her parents were getting older; her grandmother Chiyo, in the care home in Abeno, was more forgetful each time her mother described a visit. Mizuki saved through the summer, asked Mr Kondo in January for two weeks in June (he hummed, then said yes, then hummed again), and booked a flight to Kansai for the middle of the month: the start of the rainy season, which every Osaka person knows is the worst time to go anywhere, but the time the shop and the roster allowed.

Her thirty fifth birthday, 10 April, was a Thursday, and she worked; Lily and Mr Kondo produced a cake in the quiet hour with "35" in Mr Kondo's careful chocolate handwriting, and the Wednesday tradie, who had somehow found out, left a card signed "from the boss's best customer". For Hana's first birthday four days later she posted a picture book about wombats, in memory of Cradle Mountain, and tiny dungarees.

The Expo opened, and the news from home was mixed in the way Mizuki loved: long queues on the first day, complaints about the reservation app, a storm of arguments about the mascot, people swearing they would never go and then going four times. Aya went in the first week, on her day off, and reported: "Too many people. The ring is amazing. Myaku-Myaku is horrible. I bought three." Mizuki started a list.

### June 2025: home

She landed at Kansai Airport in the middle of June with one suitcase, the Zf, the FM2 with three rolls of black-and-white, a bag of Tim Tams, two jars of Tasmanian leatherwood honey and a stuffed wombat for Hana. The air outside the terminal hit her like a warm wet towel. She stood in it for a moment and grinned, because it was the air of every June of her childhood.

Her mother met her at Tennoji station, said "you're late", and took the suitcase out of her hand. They walked to the shop through the backstreets of Abeno, past the wall of Shitennoji temple where she had broken her arm in 1999, and her father was behind the hotplate, moving more carefully than before (his back, since the slipped disc in 2023, had good weeks and bad ones), and he looked up and nodded and started a pork and kimchi okonomiyaki without being asked, for the third seat from the left. Some things, she says, it would be a crime to change.

**Hana.** She met her niece that evening, in Kenta and Miho's flat in Tennoji, ten minutes away. Hana was fourteen months old, walking in the determined, unsteady way of a drunk salaryman at a year-end party, with black hair that stuck straight up at the back and Kenta's serious eyebrows. She stopped dead in the middle of the room when Mizuki came in, and stared at her for a long time, with the same expression, Mizuki says, that her father uses on a new customer. Mizuki knelt down and did not move. Then Hana walked over, holding on to the low table, looked at her very closely, and held out the Vulpix plush, chewed almost bald.

Later that evening, after dinner, Hana climbed into her lap, put her head on Mizuki's shoulder and fell asleep. Mizuki did not move for an hour. Her legs went numb, her back ached, Kenta offered twice to take the baby, and she said no both times without looking at him. Miho took a photo of the two of them on her phone, which is now the lock screen on Mizuki's phone. "The first time I held her. The most important photo I'm in, and I didn't take it."

Over the next ten days she had as much Hana as Miho would allow: walks in Tennoji Park, the wombat introduced to the Vulpix (they are friends), an afternoon at the shop with Hana on her grandfather's knee banging the till while the regulars worshipped her. The Sailor Moon pose, live for the first time, made Hana laugh so hard she fell over. And one evening Mizuki told her her first yokai story, the umbrella one. Hana understood none of it and was asleep by the end. "Perfect. That's exactly how I heard it from Obaachan."

**Family and old friends.** She visited her grandmother **Chiyo** in the care home in Abeno, bringing a box of sweets from Sydney. Chiyo, nearly ninety, was a little forgetful: she asked twice where Mizuki lived and called Australia "that place with the kangaroos and the high prices". But she was still sharp about money, and asked what Mizuki paid in rent, and when told, said "robbery", and told her to come home and marry a nice man with a shop. Mizuki laughed and held her hand and promised nothing.

On her first morning she came down early and watched her father do the kamidana before opening, as he had every morning of her life: up on the beer crate, more carefully now because of his back, water, salt and rice, then down, two bows, two claps, one bow, and the gas. She clapped along from the stairs without thinking. He didn't turn round. "Still switches it on," he said. Later that week she took the Hankai tram to **Sumiyoshi Taisha** on her own, crossed the drum bridge, washed her hands and gave thanks for Hana, then untied the travel charm from her backpack, two years of Sydney on it, dropped it in the box for old charms and bought a fresh kōtsū anzen to carry home. At the shop her mother inspected the knot and retied it.

She had dinner twice with **Aya**, once at an izakaya in Tenma until the staff stacked the chairs, once at Aya's flat, where the shiba Mame remembered her from 2023, or pretended to, and lay on her feet all evening; and they sang at a karaoke box in Namba, Kanjani Eight for Aya, Utada for Mizuki and one Takarazuka song, badly, for her mother. At Mr Ishida's near Namba he looked at her over the counter, said "Still in Australia?", and gave her a free egg and an extra slice of chashu, which from Mr Ishida is a parade. In Umeda she stood a long time in front of the electronics shops' signs for the lottery to buy the new Nintendo Switch 2, out on 5 June; the cheaper Japanese-language model was exactly what she wanted, but she had a budget, a trip and a niece to spoil. "One day," she told Aya. "Not today." Aya said she had said that about the Zf for four months.

**The Expo.** She went three times in ten days: once with Aya, once with Kenta, Miho and Hana in the pushchair, and once alone on a weekday with the Zf and a lot of patience, riding the extended Chuo subway line out to its new station on Yumeshima.

The **Grand Ring** was, she says, the most beautiful thing built in her lifetime: a vast circle of wooden beams around the whole site, about two kilometres round, recognised as the largest wooden architectural structure in the world, joined in the old Japanese way like a puzzle. Underneath, the pillars ran on like a temple corridor; on top, the skywalk had the sea on one side and the pavilions on the other. She walked it at sunset on her solo day and took hundreds of photos: beams in rows like the torii at Fushimi Inari, stripes of evening light on the crowds, a little girl in a yellow hat running ahead of her parents, an old man asleep on a bench in the shade, the sea going silver. "It's a shrine gate that goes on for two kilometres. Only Japan would build that, and only Osaka would build it for a party."

The practical side she loved less. The pavilion reservations were a lottery inside an app inside a website, and she lost every one. The queues were hours long in thirty degree humidity she had forgotten how to bear. With Aya it poured for an hour and they stood under the Ring with thousands of others, wet to the knees, laughing. Hana slept through the whole family day in her pushchair, "like a true Osaka person, unimpressed by everything". And alone, after two hours in a queue, Mizuki reached the front and found her ticket QR code was on the wrong account; a very patient young volunteer fixed it, and she bowed so deeply she nearly hit the barrier.

Then there was **Myaku-Myaku**, the red and blue mascot with too many eyes. At first she found it creepy, staring at her from every wall, bag and konbini shelf. By the end of the trip she had decided it was a yokai, which made everything all right. "Lots of eyes, nobody knows what it is, a bit frightening, a bit cute. Obaachan would have loved it." After a forty-minute queue at the official shop, a Myaku-Myaku keyring joined the Vulpix on the FM2's strap: her two friends from home, one from 1996 and one from 2025.

On the family day Kenta wanted to see the **Australia pavilion**, "where you live": a pale, flowing building inspired by the gum tree, with native plants, big skies on the screens and birdsong somewhere inside. Standing in it, in Osaka's humidity, she was suddenly and absurdly homesick for Sydney's dry light, the screaming cockatoos and the walk over Pyrmont Bridge. "I have two homes now," she told Kenta. "Which is twice the homesickness. But also twice the snacks." Kenta said nothing, and then, on the way out, bought her a small bottle of Japanese whisky at a shop "for the other home", which she thinks is the most emotional thing he has ever done.

On the last night, her father closed the shop an hour early, which he had not done for anything less than a typhoon or a funeral in her memory, and the family ate together at the counter: her parents, Kenta, Miho and Hana, and Mizuki on the third seat from the left. Her mother sang one Takarazuka song, without being asked. Her father said, at the end of the evening, "Come back sooner." She said she would. She flew back to Sydney on a grey Sunday, with Hana's photo as her lock screen and Myaku-Myaku on her camera strap, and cried at Kansai Airport, which she always does, and then bought a konbini egg sandwich at the gate, which she also always does.

### Winter 2025: back to Sydney, and KPop Demon Hunters

Sydney in late June was cold, dry and very bright, and she was glad of it. She went back to work the next day and showed the photos to everyone in the shop, including, on her phone, a hundred and twenty pictures of the Grand Ring, until Lily said, "Yes. It is a ring. It is round." The Wednesday tradie looked at them all with great interest and said he might go to Japan one day, "for the ramen". Mr Kondo looked at the photo of Mr Ishida's bowl for a very long time and said, "Hm," which in their long rivalry was a concession.

That winter, Lily made her watch *KPop Demon Hunters*, the animated film about a K-pop girl group who secretly hunt demons, which came out on Netflix in late June and became, over July and August, the biggest thing Lily had ever been obsessed with, Red Velvet included. Mizuki watched it on a Sunday afternoon with a blanket and hojicha and was ready to be cynical. She was not cynical. She loved it: the songs, the friendship, the demons who were really sad ("the villain was sad first, like Shadowbringers"), and the way it put Korean food and Korean streets into a big film without explaining them. She learned "Golden" for the dance class with Nana and did it badly, with joy. Lily claimed this as her greatest victory in their six year argument, exceeding Mizuki's TWICE "Strategy" victory, and Mizuki, for once, agreed.

### August 2025: the scam text

On a Thursday afternoon in August, on her break, Mizuki got a text message.

It said her parcel was being held at a depot because of an incomplete address, and that she needed to confirm her details and pay a small redelivery fee, a few dollars, within twenty four hours or it would be returned to the sender. There was a link.

She knew about these messages; Lily, the bank and the news had all warned her. But that week she really was expecting a parcel, a box from her mother with okonomiyaki flour, the shop's sauce and printed photos of Hana. She was tired after the lunch rush, the wording looked right, and the fee was too small to feel like a risk. She typed in her address and card details and paid. Then the page said her bank needed to "verify the payment", a code arrived on her phone, she typed it in, and the page said thank you.

An hour later, on the walk home, her bank app showed a payment of $180 to an overseas online shop she had never heard of. She stopped on Pyrmont Bridge in the cold wind and understood at once, "like someone poured cold water down my back". The bank cancelled the card straight away and the young man on the line was kind. But the code she had typed was the bank's own approval code for that $180, so by the rules she had authorised it, and a fortnight later they wrote to say the money could not be recovered. She reported it to Scamwatch, filling in every field with great precision, which made her feel slightly better and achieved, as far as she knows, nothing.

It was only $180, and she knows people lose far more. But she was furious with herself for weeks: she was careful, she read every notice and kept every receipt, she had once warned hotel guests about exactly this, and it had worked on her anyway, on a tired Thursday with her mother's parcel on her mind. "It's not the money. It's that I was stupid, and I'm not stupid. That's worse than the money." Her mother said, "This is why cash." Lily showed her how to check where a link really goes. Mr Kondo hummed. The real parcel arrived, entirely normally, the following Tuesday, and Hana's photos went on the fridge beside the gum tree print.

She never taps a link in a text now, and tells anyone who will listen: "The post never texts you with a link. The post barely knocks." Her rule for everyone: "If it's urgent and small, it's a trap. Real problems are big and slow." She still goes a bit pink when it comes up.

### Early September 2025: the Tigers

At the start of September her father's Hanshin Tigers won the Central League pennant, clinching it on 7 September, one of the earliest clinches in the league's history, two years after they had won the Japan Series. Her mother sent a video from the shop: the regulars cheering, a Tigers flag over the counter, and her father standing behind the hotplate, holding a spatula, saying nothing at all, with an expression that, Mizuki says, "for him is dancing in the street". She watched it on her break and laughed until Lily asked what was wrong. She sent back a photo of the Tigers towel she keeps on her balcony chair. Her father replied with their usual single tiger emoji and then, for the first time in her life, a second one: another tiger. She was so shocked she showed Mr Kondo, who said, as a Sapporo man, that he did not understand baseball people, and then asked to see it again.

### 17 September 2025: the only two left in the cinema

In September a cinema in the city was running a re-screening of *Godzilla Minus One*, the 2023 Japanese film directed by Takashi Yamazaki that had won the Academy Award for visual effects in March 2024, on a budget that was tiny by Hollywood standards. Mizuki had only ever seen it on her laptop, on her cushion, during a bad week in 2024, and had loved it. Seeing it on a big screen felt, she said, like a duty, "like visiting a shrine you have only seen in a photo".

So on Wednesday 17 September 2025, after her shift, she went alone, with a bag of rice crackers smuggled in her handbag (cinema popcorn, she says, smells of "a car that has been crying") and a seat in the middle of the row chosen carefully on the app. It was a Wednesday evening and a two-year-old film, and the session was far from full.

She loved it all over again. For her it was less a monster film than a story about a man who survived when he wasn't meant to and had to learn how to live: a romance and a ghost story inside a disaster film, exactly her kind of thing. She watched the attack on Ginza through her fingers and cried at the end, quietly, into a rice cracker.

When the credits began she stayed in her seat, as she always does, out of respect for the people who made it; she has never understood the habit of standing up the moment the music starts. The others filed out around her. When the house lights came up, only one other person was still there: a man a few seats along the same row, also sitting, also reading every name.

That was Scott.

What happened next is the subject of a long-running disagreement. One of them said something about the credits, something about how many names there were for a film made by such a small team. Mizuki insists, and will always insist, that it was him. He spoke first. She is completely certain. She remembers his voice coming across the empty seats, and herself turning round, surprised that anyone else was still there.

Within a minute they were talking about the film. How a small Japanese effects team had made something that looked better than films costing ten times as much. How the director had supervised the effects himself. How the chase at sea, with the little wooden boat and the thing coming up underneath, was the most frightening part, much more than the city. Why Japanese films leave room for silence in a way Hollywood rarely does. She told him about Ringu, and the night the television was unplugged at the sleepover in 2002, and about *Crying Out Love in the Center of the World* and the usher with the tissues. He had opinions about Godzilla, about the old films and the new ones, that she found, she says, "wrong, but in an interesting way". She remembers being surprised how easy he was to talk to, and teasing him within five minutes.

Then the cleaner came in with her trolley, looked at the two of them still sitting there in an empty cinema with the lights up and the credits long finished, and asked them, politely but firmly, to leave. They apologised. Mizuki bowed, out of habit; the cleaner looked baffled. They kept talking on the stairs, and in the foyer, and outside on the street in the cool spring evening, for some time, until Mizuki realised it was late and she had work in the morning.

It was a friendship from the first night, and nothing else: warm and easy, the kind where you swap numbers because you want to keep arguing about a film. The next day he messaged about the film, and she replied with a list of other Japanese films he had to see, in order of importance. Within a week they were sending each other photographs and opinions about Japanese things and Final Fantasy. She found him curious, kind and easy to talk to, and, she discovered quickly, a very good audience for her teasing: especially about the way so many of the things he liked turned out to be Japanese. "You like Godzilla, you like Japanese films, you like cameras. I am starting to see a pattern. You are being collected by Japan, slowly, like a gachapon." She told Lily about him the next day, at length, and Lily asked if he was her boyfriend, and Mizuki said, "No. He's a friend. A good one. Bring me the soy sauce," and that was the end of it.

She counts 17 September as their friendship anniversary, and has decided that she will make a fuss of it every year, with a Godzilla reference. And she still says, every time it comes up, that she would never have spoken first in an empty cinema, because she is a well brought up Osaka girl, and Osaka girls only speak first at the okonomiyaki counter, where it is their job.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes the Nikon Zf**, bought February 2024 with the 40mm lens: it looks and handles like her father's FM2, with real dials and a black-and-white switch "for someone who learned in a darkroom". Her father called it "kakkoii", his highest praise.
- **Likes Tasmania**: cold air, real seasons, old sandstone, the Salamanca market, leatherwood honey, scallop pies (against her principles), Dove Lake in the mist and, above all, wombats ("the most Hiroshi-like animal").
- **Dislikes MONA**: "too much", like a bowl of ramen with every topping. She loved only the waterfall of words.
- **Likes being an aunt**: Hana is "my small boss"; the Sailor Moon pose always works; the photo of Hana asleep on her shoulder is her lock screen.
- **Likes Dawntrail's ending and Tural's colours**; finds its first half "a school trip". Tried Pictomancer for a week; White Mage forever. Blames the ping for every death (Aya blames the orange).
- **Likes Dandadan and Delicious in Dungeon**: yokai done properly, and a cooking show with monsters. Watches the frightening parts through her fingers.
- **Likes Creepy Nuts' "Bling-Bang-Bang-Born"** (her father not dancing to it), TWICE's "Strategy", and, thanks to Lily, *KPop Demon Hunters* and "Golden".
- **Dislikes open-world padding**: FFVII Rebirth was wonderful and took four months.
- **Hates leaks, old plumbing and damp wardrobes**: the October 2024 leak took most of her 2018 Sydney negatives. Now everything is scanned twice and stored high.
- **Likes the Grand Ring** at sunset ("a shrine gate two kilometres long"); **Myaku-Myaku**, eventually ("it's a yokai"); **dislikes** Expo queues, the pavilion lottery and June humidity.
- **Hates text messages with links**: "The post never texts you with a link. If it's urgent and small, it's a trap."
- **Likes staying for the credits**: it's how she met Scott.
- **Dislikes cinema popcorn** ("a car that has been crying"); smuggles rice crackers.
- **Dislikes magpies**, still; drawn-on eyes "were too kawaii" to work.
