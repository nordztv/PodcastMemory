## Chapter 4: First Love, theme parks and the millennium (late 1999 to early 2002)

### September 1999: the cast comes off

Mizuki spent the summer of 1999 with her left arm in a plaster cast, after coming off her bike by the long wall of Shitennoji in July, and by the end of August she hated that cast the way other children hate vegetables. It itched in the Osaka heat in a place no chopstick could reach (she tried every chopstick in the shop). It had kept her out of the sea at Shirahama, where she'd sat under her mother's parasol for three days with a plastic bag taped over her arm, watching Kenta splash about and pretend he was drowning. It smelled, by the end, "like a judo club's sock", and it was covered in signatures: her classmates' names in felt pen, a wobbly kappa drawn by Fumiko, and, in Kenta's careful five-year-old katakana, the word TANAKA, which he insisted was the goldfish signing, since the goldfish had no hands.

The cast came off at the clinic near Tennoji in early September, a week into the new term. The doctor cut it with a little buzzing saw that Mizuki was certain would take her arm with it; she held Yumiko's hand and looked very hard at a poster about tooth decay. Underneath, her arm was thin, pale and faintly hairy, like something that had lived under a rock, and it smelled worse than the cast. She was horrified. Yumiko laughed so much the nurse had to look away. Hiroshi, told about it that night, looked at the arm for a long moment and said, "It'll grow back," as if it were a lawn, and went back to the hotplate.

It did grow back, and by October she could hang from the bars in the playground again, but the summer stayed with her. It was the first time she understood that a body could simply stop doing what you asked, and that a summer could be lost and never given back. Being a Tanaka, she turned it into material: by Christmas she had a polished three-minute version of the bike story for the shop counter, with a proper ochi ("and the wall was fine"), and the regulars asked for it like a song. She was careful on her bike for years afterwards and still is. Hiroshi, without being asked, took the bike to the back alley one Wednesday, straightened the bent handlebar, oiled the chain and leaned it against the wall without a word. It stood there all winter. In March 2000 she got back on it, rode very slowly to Fumiko's, and Fumiko, who had been told nothing, was waiting at the door with a cup of barley tea as if she'd known.

She still tells people she has "a weather arm", which aches before rain, though nobody, including her doctor in Sydney, has ever found anything wrong with it. "It's not medical," she says. "It's Japanese. My arm reads the forecast."

### October 1999: the Grand Theatre

Yumiko kept the promise she had made the night of the fall. On a Wednesday in October, with the cast gone and the arm still thin, the two of them took the Hankyu train out to Takarazuka, just the girls, Mizuki in Chiyo's strawberry Mezzo Piano dress, let out at the seams for the occasion. They walked the flower-lined path along the river to the Grand Theatre, and Yumiko, who talked through everything, went quiet on the steps. Inside it was red and gold and enormous. The show itself Mizuki only half followed, but she remembers the orchestra starting up in the dark, a woman playing a man so well that she forgot to check, and then the finale: the whole company coming down a great glittering staircase one by one, the top star last, in a wheel of white feathers on her back. Yumiko cried, sang along under her breath and denied both. On the train home she held Mizuki's newly uncast hand the whole way, which Mizuki allowed. It was the first of many trips, and the beginning of the Takarazuka DVDs they still watch together over video call.

### The house of First Love

Utada Hikaru's debut album, *First Love*, came out on 10 March 1999 and went on to become the biggest-selling album in Japanese history. In the Tanaka flat that meant one thing: Yumiko had bought it in the spring on the recommendation of a customer (the young woman from the bank who always ordered the cheese modan-yaki), and it had gone into the stereo upstairs and stayed there. Through the summer of the cast, when Mizuki couldn't swim or ride or do much except lie on the tatami under the fan, she played that CD from start to finish nearly every afternoon. She knew the order of the tracks before she knew her nine times table.

She didn't understand most of the words. Utada was sixteen and singing about a last kiss tasting of cigarettes and about loves that were already over, and Mizuki was nine and mainly cross about her arm. But she understood the feeling underneath: something sweet with a bruise in it. "Automatic" was the one she sang, into a ladle, doing the little shoulder movements from the video, until Kenta put his hands over his ears and shouted "Onee-chan, it's not automatic, it's broken!", which was a better joke than he knew, given the arm. "First Love" itself was the one she listened to lying down, with her eyes shut. It was the first time a song made her feel something she had no words for, and she says that's when she learned she loves things that are a little sad underneath: songs, films, endings, rainy days. It's still in her shower rotation in Pyrmont. Her neighbours, she assumes, know all the words by now.

Utada also taught her something about clothes, though she didn't realise it until later. In a year when every pop star seemed to be in platform boots and tanned skin, Utada wore plain T-shirts and jeans and looked cooler than all of them. Mizuki, who at nine was mainly dressed in hand-me-downs from the girl two doors down and the one good Mezzo Piano dress for special days, filed that away: you could be plain and still be the most interesting person in the room. It took her another fifteen years and a vintage denim jacket to put it into practice.

The flat's music in those years was a three-way argument. Yumiko had Utada, and her Takarazuka cassettes, which had become even more important since she'd taken Mizuki to the Grand Theatre for the first time that autumn; the Rose of Versailles songs came on during the Sunday-night cleaning, and Yumiko sang the male-role parts in a deep voice with her hand on her chest while wiping the counter. Hiroshi had Southern All Stars and old enka, played low on the shop radio. And in January 2000 Southern All Stars released "TSUNAMI", which became the biggest hit of the year, sold more than two million copies and won the Japan Record Award. For the whole of 2000 Hiroshi hummed it at the hotplate. He never admitted to liking it more than their older songs; he just hummed it, every evening, between the pork and the mayonnaise. When Mizuki asked whether it was his favourite, he said, "It's a song," which in her father's language was a love letter. (Some years later, after the 2011 disaster, the band itself chose not to play it for a long time, and Hiroshi stopped humming it too, without a word. Mizuki noticed.)

### "Love Machine", and the incident at the Christmas party

Then there was Morning Musume. The group had come out of a TV audition programme, and in September 1999 they released "Love Machine", a disco song with the line "Nippon no mirai wa wow wow wow wow" ("Japan's future is wow wow wow wow"), sung as the economy was doing nothing of the sort. Everybody in Japan knew the dance by October: the arm pumps, the point to the sky, the little shimmy. Mizuki, freshly uncast, learned it in the long mirror on the shop's back wall after closing, with Kenta as the backing group, and Hiroshi, scraping the hotplate, supplying "wow wow" in a voice so flat it was funnier than the song.

At the class Christmas party in December 1999, the girls in her group performed it in front of the whole third-grade class. Mizuki was at the front because she'd volunteered to be at the front, which she regretted the moment the cassette started. On the second chorus she spun the wrong way, caught her slipper on a desk leg and sat down hard on the classroom floor, in the middle of "wow wow wow". There was a silence. Then a boy at the back shouted "nandeyanen!" in perfect tsukkomi timing, the whole class laughed, and Mizuki, who had been a tenth of a second from crying, decided instead to stay sitting down and do the rest of the arm movements from the floor. It brought the house down. Her teacher wrote in her end-of-term report that she had "a good sense of how to recover". She was furious about it for a week and proud of it for twenty-five years. It was, she says, her first live lesson in Osaka comedy: if you fall over, you are now the act.

She was never a proper Morning Musume fan, though. She liked the songs and loved the outfits (she drew them, with improvements, in the margins of her maths book next to her Cardcaptor Sakura redesigns), but there were too many members to keep track of and they kept changing. "I need to know who everybody is," she says. "That's why I like One Piece. You always know who's on the ship."

### Wednesday nights: One Piece comes to life

She had been reading *One Piece* since 1997, first in the regular's copy of Weekly Shōnen Jump left on the counter every Monday, and later in Kenta's. By 1999 the Jump arguments were a fixed part of the week: Kenta, who bought it with his pocket money, had the right to read it first, and Mizuki had the right to complain about how he held it. He creased the spine. He read with his mouth open. He once got curry on a Nami page and she didn't speak to him until Thursday.

Nami had been her favourite from the moment she appeared: the navigator who loves money and tangerines, draws the maps, reads the weather and hits the boys when they're idiots. Mizuki recognised Yumiko at once. "She's the only sensible person on the ship," she announced, and has said so roughly once a month ever since. In 1999 the manga's Arlong Park story, where it comes out why Nami has been stealing and lying and why tangerines matter so much to her, reached its end, and Mizuki read the chapter where Nami finally asks Luffy for help sitting on an upturned beer crate in the back of the shop. She cried and told her mother there was sauce in her eye. Yumiko, who had read it over her shoulder, said, "Mine too," and went back to the till.

The anime began on Fuji TV on 20 October 1999, Wednesdays at seven in the evening. Wednesday was the one night the shop was closed, which meant it was the one night the whole family ate dinner upstairs together at a normal hour, and so *One Piece* became, accidentally, a family programme. Yumiko cooked something that was not okonomiyaki (usually nabe, or curry, or grilled fish with too much grated daikon). Hiroshi sat at the end of the low table with a beer and pretended to read the paper. Kenta lay on his stomach far too close to the screen. Fumiko came round most weeks and said every pirate needed a haircut. By the second month Hiroshi had stopped pretending to read the paper. By the spring he had a favourite, Zoro, "because he doesn't talk much", and the family agreed this was the most Hiroshi reason anyone had ever given for anything.

The first time Nami appeared in the anime Mizuki stood up and clapped. In the Arlong Park episodes in 2000 she cried again, this time openly, because it was a Wednesday and there was no sauce to blame. She practised Nami's weapon with a broom handle in the back alley: first the plain staff of the early episodes, and later, once the manga gave Nami her weather-making Clima-Tact in the desert kingdom story, a broom handle in three sections joined with Hiroshi's electrical tape, which fell apart every time she tried to summon a thunderstorm. Her lifelong love of small, sweet mikan, she says, is one-third Fumiko's kotatsu and two-thirds Nami's tangerine grove.

Kenta, inevitably, was Luffy: he stretched his face, shouted "I'm gonna be King of the Pirates!" at the dinner table and ate everything on the table, including, once, Mizuki's portion. She still uses that line on the phone, and still means it as an insult to her brother.

### November 1999: Silver, for Rokon

Pokémon Gold and Silver came out on 21 November 1999, the day before Kenta's sixth birthday, which Kenta considered a sign from heaven and his parents considered a financial ambush. He got Gold. Mizuki, who had been saving pocket money since the summer of the cast (there had been little else to do), bought Silver with her own money a few weeks later, at the toy shop in the Abeno arcade, on the single, unshakeable grounds that Rokon (Vulpix) could be caught in Silver and not in Gold. "Loyalty," she said. Kenta, who had Growlithe, called her old-fashioned. She called him a fire type with no self-control, which was still her standard insult.

The new games had day and night that followed the real clock, which delighted her, and berries growing on trees, and the whole old Kanto map waiting at the end like a secret room. She played under the counter on the beer crate, as she had with Green, with the hotplate hissing above her, and the regulars leaned down to ask how many badges she had. She chose Totodile as her starter because it was blue and "a water girl like me", stayed loyal to Rokon long after it was any use in a fight, and the two games gave her and Kenta a new thing to fight about: trading. He wanted her Rokon. She would sooner have traded him.

### The last night of 1999

By December 1999 the television was full of the "Y2K problem", the fear that computers built to count years in two digits would think 2000 was 1900 and stop working at midnight. Banks, trains and power stations put engineers on standby. The government advised households to keep a little water and food at home over the New Year, just in case. For most of Japan this was a curiosity. For the Tanakas, less than five years after the earthquake, it landed differently.

Yumiko took it seriously in the practical way she took everything. She filled two plastic tanks with water, put a bag of rice and a box of candles by the door, checked the torch, and made sure the gas canisters for the portable stove were full. Nobody argued with her. Mizuki watched her do it and understood, without anyone saying so, that her mother had not forgotten January 1995 and never would. Hiroshi, asked what he thought would happen, said, "If the computers stop, the hotplate still works," which was his way of saying don't worry. Fumiko, at seventy-two, was entirely unbothered. "I've seen the year change seventy-two times," she said. "It's never once asked my permission."

New Year's Eve went the way it always did: the shop scrubbed top to bottom, the hotplate oiled and covered, toshikoshi soba upstairs, the Kohaku song contest on television with Yumiko scoring the outfits and Hiroshi scoring nothing. But Mizuki, nine, had made a decision: she was going to see the year 2000 arrive with her own eyes. She had failed to stay awake for midnight every year of her life. This year she drank a cup of barley tea at half past ten, sat bolt upright against the wall with her knees under her chin, and refused the kotatsu on the grounds that it was "a sleeping trap". Kenta fell asleep across Fumiko's lap before the Kohaku finished. Mizuki made it.

At midnight the temple bells began, the joya no kane, a hundred and eight strokes to ring out the hundred and eight earthly desires, and from Shitennoji, close enough to hear through the window, came the deep, slow bong of the old bell. The television counted down. The lights stayed on. The fridge kept humming. Somewhere on the news a man in a control room bowed to the camera to say nothing had gone wrong. Mizuki, who had half hoped for something dramatic, was a little disappointed. Then Hiroshi went downstairs, lit the hotplate, and made a single small okonomiyaki at quarter past midnight, "to check", and the four of them and Fumiko ate it in their pyjamas at the counter with the shutters down. It was the first food of the new millennium, and it was, Mizuki maintains, the best okonomiyaki he has ever made. In the morning Kenta woke up, asked if the computers had exploded, and was told no. He sulked until lunch.

The water tanks stayed by the door until spring. The torch stayed there for good. Mizuki still keeps one by her own front door in Pyrmont, and on New Year's Eve in Sydney, where midnight comes two hours before Osaka's, she always sends her parents a message at 10pm Sydney time: "Hotplate still works?" And her father always sends back one word: "Works."

### 2000: a year with a new number

The year 2000 felt, to a nine-year-old, like living inside the future, even if the future looked a lot like Abeno. The family kept finding small strange things about it. In July a new 2,000-yen note appeared, with the Shureimon gate of Okinawa on the front, issued for the G8 summit there. Yumiko took one look at it and declared war: there was no slot for it in the till, nobody knew whether to give it as change, and customers stared at it as if it were Monopoly money. She kept the few that came in under the cash tray and gave them to Mizuki and Kenta as pocket money "so they're your problem now". Mizuki still has one, flat in a book, as a souvenir of the only thing her mother ever lost a fight to.

In March the PlayStation 2 came out, and every boy in her class wanted one, and nobody she knew could afford one. In July, Final Fantasy IX came out on the old PlayStation, and Daisuke, fifteen now and in his last year of junior high in Kobe, brought it to Fumiko's over the summer holidays exactly as he'd brought VII three years before. Mizuki sat behind him again, now old enough to read every line herself before he could. She loved the little black mage Vivi, who worried about whether he was real, with a kind of fierce, protective tenderness, and found the whole game "like a picture book you're allowed to walk into". It was the first Final Fantasy, she says, that felt like it had been made by someone who'd also grown up on old stories. She still says IX is the most underrated of the series and will explain why at length to anyone who doesn't move away.

At the end of August Dragon Quest VII came out, and Kenta, nearly seven, found his religion. Daisuke had bought that too (he was, Yumiko said, "a bank for game companies"), and Kenta watched him play it with the same silent devotion Mizuki had given VII, and then began drawing Slimes, the little blue teardrop monsters, on everything he owned. The family split was settled from that summer: Mizuki was Final Fantasy, Kenta was Dragon Quest, and every argument they have had since, about anything, can be traced back to it in some way. "Dragon Quest is for people who like the same thing every time," she says. "Final Fantasy is for people who like to cry about a different thing every time." Kenta, now a systems engineer, says this is "not an argument, it's a mood". She says that's what makes it correct.

And in September 2000 the Olympics were held in a city Mizuki had barely heard of. On Sunday 24 September Naoko Takahashi ran the women's marathon and won gold, the first Japanese woman ever to win an Olympic gold in athletics, and the whole of Japan stopped to watch. It was on the little TV in the corner of the shop. Lunch service froze. The taxi driver stood up with his negiyaki going cold. When Takahashi came into the stadium and crossed the line, Yumiko cried and hit Hiroshi with a menu, for no reason except that she had to hit someone. Mizuki, ten, watched the shots of the city behind the runners: blue water, a big arched bridge, a white building like folded paper or a pile of shells. "Where is that?" she asked. "Sydney," said Hiroshi. "Australia. Upside down. Hot at Christmas." She thought it looked like a made-up place. Eighteen years later she would arrive there with one suitcase. She has a photograph she took of the Opera House on her first week in 2018, and when she sent it home Hiroshi replied, "Takahashi," and nothing else, and she knew exactly what he meant.

### Fourth grade, and the clothes question

In April 2000 she moved up to fourth grade. Her teacher that year, Ms Nakai, was young, wore her hair in a sensible bob and played the piano for every class song with real passion and frequent wrong notes. She noticed that Mizuki talked a great deal to the people around her and almost never put her hand up, and she fixed it in a very Japanese way: she made Mizuki the class's announcer for the morning meeting, which meant reading out the date, the weather and the day's lunch menu every morning in front of everyone. For the first week Mizuki went pink to the ears. By the end of the month she was adding jokes about the lunch. ("Today's lunch is liver stew. My condolences.") Ms Nakai let it go, which Mizuki now recognises as excellent teaching.

Fourth grade was also when she started to care about clothes, seriously and with opinions. Until then she'd mostly worn what she was given: hand-me-downs from the older girl two doors down, sturdy shorts and T-shirts for school, and the one Mezzo Piano dress Chiyo had bought her at seven (pale pink, with a strawberry print and a white collar, let out twice by now) brought out for New Year and photographs. Mezzo Piano and the other children's brands from the same company, all ribbons, frills and little embroidered logos, were what every girl in Osaka wanted around 2000, and Kintetsu department store in Abeno sold them on a floor that smelled of new cotton and money. Yumiko took her there once a year, before New Year, and bought one thing. Mizuki spent the rest of the year redesigning that one thing in her notebook.

Her actual style in those years was a war between three influences. There was the Osaka obachan in leopard print from the shop, still her style hero, who had taught her that an outfit was a decision. There was Cardcaptor Sakura, whose heroine changed costume in every episode thanks to a best friend who sewed them, and whose costumes Mizuki drew and improved in the margins of every exercise book she owned. And there was Utada in a plain T-shirt. The result, at ten, was a girl who wore a plain white T-shirt with a ribbon tied in her hair in a colour that matched her socks, and who had very strong views about socks. She wanted platform sandals like the older girls in Namba and was refused (Yumiko: "You broke your arm on flat ground"). She wanted to dye a strip of her hair brown like a girl in sixth grade and was refused by everybody, including Fumiko, who said it would make her look "like a tanuki that's been in a fire". She settled for a red hair clip shaped like a cherry, which she wore every day of fourth grade and lost at the zoo in the autumn. She looked for it for an hour. It was not found. She still buys hair clips shaped like fruit, and has never once bought a red cherry one: "That position is filled."

### Golden Week 2000: Expoland with Daisuke

At Golden Week in May 2000, Daisuke's mother, Keiko, who had once learned modan-yaki at the shop and now wore a sun visor in all weathers, took Daisuke, Mizuki and Kenta to Expoland, the amusement park in the old Expo '70 grounds in Suita, in the north of the city. They went by Midosuji line and then the monorail, which Mizuki thought was the most futuristic thing she had ever ridden: a train hanging in the sky, sliding past the tops of apartment blocks. Kenta pressed his face to the window the whole way and left a nose print she could still see on the way home.

Before Expoland, though, there was the Tower of the Sun. It stands in the middle of the old Expo park, a huge white figure by the artist Taro Okamoto, seventy metres tall, with a golden face at the top, a grumpy face in the middle of its body, and on the back, facing away from everyone, a black face for the sun of the past. Mizuki had seen it from the monorail and could not stop looking at it. It was half a god and half a yokai, beautiful and a little wrong, and the black face on the back gave her a delicious shiver: "It's watching the way we came." She made everyone walk round it twice. Of everything she saw in Osaka as a child, it is one of the things she most wants to photograph properly, and in 2023, on her first trip home, she finally did, on film with her father's FM2, at dusk, from behind.

Expoland itself was loud, bright and slightly shabby in the comfortable way of a park that had been there since before her parents married. It had eight roller coasters, and Daisuke, fourteen and newly too cool for most things, wanted to ride all of them. His favourite was Fujin Raijin II, a stand-up coaster named after the wind god and the thunder god, where you rode standing up with your shoulders clamped in a harness. Mizuki watched it go round once, heard the screaming, and declined with dignity. "I'll hold the bags," she said. Kenta, six, was too small for it anyway and held her hand while she held the bags, and they both watched Daisuke come off it pale and grinning and pretending he hadn't screamed.

What she loved was the Ferris wheel. It turned slowly over the park, and from the top the whole of north Osaka spread out: the green of the Expo park, the Tower of the Sun from above (the golden face at eye level, which felt like meeting someone important), the Senri hills full of apartment blocks, and far off to the south a grey haze that Daisuke said was the city centre and that Mizuki said was Abeno, because she wanted it to be. She asked to go round again and they did. She loved the slowness of it, the creak of the gondola, the way the world went quiet halfway up and then came back. "A Ferris wheel is the opposite of a roller coaster," she said that afternoon, and has said many times since. "A roller coaster is for proving something. A Ferris wheel is for looking." She has ridden one in every city she's lived in or visited since, including Luna Park and the big wheel on Darling Harbour, and judges every city by its Ferris wheel view.

They ate soft-serve ice cream on a bench, vanilla and matcha swirled, and Daisuke, mellowed by the sugar, told her about his junior high in Kobe and the high school entrance exams already looming, about the new PlayStation 2 nobody could afford, and, very briefly, about how Kobe still had empty lots where houses used to be. It was the first time he'd mentioned the earthquake to her since they were small. She didn't know what to say, so she gave him the rest of her ice cream, which he ate. Afterwards they rode the monorail home and Daisuke fell asleep against the window, and Mizuki remembers thinking that he was nearly a grown-up and she was glad he still came.

Seven years later, in May 2007, a car on Fujin Raijin II came off the track and a young woman was killed. Mizuki was seventeen and saw it on the news in the shop. She went cold all over, thinking of the bench, the bags and Daisuke's pale grin coming off that exact ride, and she phoned him in Kobe that night, which she had never done before; neither of them said much. The park closed in 2009. She has never ridden a stand-up coaster and never will, and she has never been able to explain her feeling about Expoland better than this: "It was a happy place, and then it was a sad place, and now it's a shopping centre. I'm still sad about all three."

### Summer 2000: back in the sea

The summer of 2000 was the summer she got back everything the cast had taken. The festivals came round in their usual order and she went to all of them with both arms. At the end of June there was Aizen Matsuri at Shoman-in, the first festival of the Osaka summer, where she wore a new navy yukata with white morning glories, ate one takoyaki and one melon shaved ice, and stood guard while Kenta tried to scoop a wife for Tanaka the goldfish. He failed. Tanaka remained a bachelor for life, which Kenta said was "his choice". In late July Hiroshi closed early on the night of the Tenjin Matsuri fireworks, and the family stood on a crowded bridge over the Okawa river, Kenta on his father's shoulders and Mizuki, now ten and too big to be carried, standing on the bottom rail and holding Yumiko's sleeve. She liked the gold willow ones best, the ones that hang in the sky and drip, because they took their time.

In August the shop closed for its three days and the family took the Kuroshio from Tennoji down the Wakayama coast to Shirahama, to the same small inn and the same thin futons, for the third summer in a row. The year before, Mizuki had sat under the parasol with a plastic bag taped over her cast. This year she was the first one into the sea. She ran down the white sand of Shirarahama, went in up to her knees, and stopped dead, because the water was colder than she remembered and because, she admits, she had suddenly become afraid of falling. Hiroshi waded in beside her in his terrible striped trunks, said nothing, and stood next to her until she was ready. Then she swam, very carefully, along the line of the shore, never further out than she could stand. She has swum like that ever since. "I am a coastal swimmer," she says. "Like a small fishing boat. I have no business in the open ocean." The rest went in the proper order: the sunset through Engetsu, the cliffs at Sandanbeki, and Adventure World, where the pandas were asleep and she photographed them anyway, twenty-seven pictures of a black-and-white heap. "Look. In this one he moved his ear."

Then came the reckoning that comes for every Japanese child at the end of August: the summer homework. Her picture diary for 2000 is, according to Yumiko, who kept it, "a work of fiction", with sunshine every day including the day of a typhoon warning. Her free research project was "Which shaved ice syrup is really different?", in which she proved, with Kenta blindfolded and nine paper cups, that strawberry, melon and lemon taste almost the same and the colour does most of the work. Ms Nakai gave it a gold sticker. Mizuki still orders melon at every shaved ice stand in the world, because "it tastes of nothing but bright green, and I respect the honesty".

### Hirakata Park, and the Elf

In the autumn of fourth grade the whole year went on an excursion to Hirakata Park, the old amusement park out along the Keihan line towards Kyoto. They went by the Loop line to Kyobashi and then on the Keihan train, sixty-odd ten-year-olds in yellow caps, with Ms Nakai counting them at every station like a duck with a clipboard.

Hirakata in 2000 was a park with two personalities. One half was rides: the coasters, the swinging pirate ship, a Ferris wheel that Mizuki rode at once and judged "good, but not Expoland". The other half was the chrysanthemum doll show, the kiku ningyo, which the park had been famous for since before her grandparents were born: life-size samurai and court ladies from the year's NHK history drama, their robes made of living chrysanthemums, gold and white and dark red, trimmed by old men with tiny scissors. The rest of the class ran past to the rides. Mizuki stopped in front of a lord on a horse whose sleeves were flowers and stayed there until Ms Nakai came back for her. The faces were painted dolls, perfectly still, and the bodies were alive and slowly growing. "They're yokai," she told Saki Fujii, who sat next to her that year. "Flowers that have decided to be people." The show ended in 2005; she still grieves a little for the lord on the horse.

Behind the fences at the back of the park, something enormous was being built: a wooden roller coaster, a great pale lattice of timber climbing into the sky, with workmen walking along the top as if it were a garden path. A sign said it would open in the spring and was called Elf. The boys stood at the fence and swore a solemn pact to ride it the minute it opened. The girls rolled their eyes and then, a few minutes later, swore one of their own. Mizuki was in it before she had thought it through.

They kept the pact. In the spring holidays of 2001, a couple of weeks after Elf opened, Saki's mother took Saki, Mizuki, Mio and two other girls from the class back to Hirakata, with a cool box of onigiri and the face of a woman who had lost an argument. Finished, Elf was a long, rattling wooden track that climbed eighteen metres, dropped, and then threw you sideways through its own timbers for a minute and a half at nearly sixty kilometres an hour, roaring like a train going through a house. Mio rode it first and came off laughing. Saki came off screaming happily, which was Saki's natural state. Mizuki, who had held the bags at Expoland and was not going to hold the bags in front of her own class, got into the front row because it was the only free seat.

The chain clanked them up the lift hill so slowly that she had time to think about her arm. Then the floor went away. On a steel coaster, she says, you fly; on a wooden one you are shaken like a bottle of Calpis. Every bump went up her spine into her teeth, and the cars rattled as if the whole thing were about to come apart. She screamed for the first half and then stopped screaming, which was worse. In the station she climbed out, walked very calmly to a bench, sat down, and burst into tears in front of four girls from her class and Saki's mother.

It was not fear, she insists. It was "everything at once": the speed, the noise, the arm, the lost summer, the fact that she had sworn to be brave. Saki's mother bought her a can of Qoo from a vending machine. Saki sat on her other side and, after a decent interval, said, "You screamed louder than me," which was the kindest lie anyone told her that year. Mizuki rode the Ferris wheel twice more and felt better at the top, looking at the river, where nothing was shaking. She has never ridden a wooden coaster again. "Steel is honest. Wood is an old house that wants to kill you." On the Gold Coast in 2022 she screamed on every ride and loved it, but she checked first that every one of them was made of metal.

### Sports day, and the anchor

Hirakata was not her only autumn triumph of 2000. At the October sports day the red team won the tug of war, and Mizuki, by her own account, won it for them. She was put at the very back of the rope because she was not strong and Ms Nakai had to put her somewhere. At the whistle she leaned back so far that she simply sat down on the sand with the rope round her middle and refused to be moved, and the red team won by about the length of a slipper. Hiroshi, at the rope with his Nikon, took the photograph: a row of red-faced children straining, and at the end of it Mizuki, sitting down, eyes shut, an expression of perfect peace. It hangs upstairs next to the first-grade one of her coming last and waving. "I was the anchor," she says. "The most important position. Look it up." Kenta, who has looked it up, says the anchor is usually the heaviest and strongest person on the team. She says that's a Dragon Quest way of thinking. Grandpa Isamu came for the lunch only and pronounced Fumiko's omelette "too sweet, as usual, perfect".

### A new century, again

On 1 January 2001 the television announced that the twenty-first century had begun, and Kenta, seven, was outraged. "We already did that. Last year. There was soba." Hiroshi explained, with the patience of a man who had read the same thing in the sports paper that morning, that there had been no year zero, so the century really started now. Kenta said in that case he wanted his okonomiyaki back. Yumiko said that had been a millennium okonomiyaki, and this year he could have a century mandarin, and gave him one from the kotatsu.

New Year went the way it always went: the shop scrubbed, the soba, the Kohaku, the temple bells (she stayed awake again, now confident in her technique), the first visit to Shitennoji, a fortune of "middle luck", and otoshidama from everyone, including an extra envelope from Grandma Chiyo "from the till", which Yumiko pretended not to see. On the tenth Yumiko took her to Toka Ebisu at Imamiya Ebisu shrine, the merchants' festival, where the crowds chant for good business and the shrine maidens hand out lucky bamboo. Yumiko chose the shop's new bamboo and its charms (a sea bream, a gold coin, a little bale of rice) with the concentration of a woman buying a car. Mizuki carried it home on foot through Shinsekai, upright like a flag, and hung it above the till herself, standing on a beer crate. She did it every year after that until she left home, and still believes, privately, that the shop's good years were the ones when she hung it straight.

On 3 February came Setsubun: roasted beans thrown at Hiroshi in his cardboard demon mask (Yumiko threw hardest), and the Osaka custom of eho-maki, a whole uncut sushi roll eaten in silence facing the year's lucky direction. Kenta lasted four bites before saying "This is hard" with his mouth full, which cancelled his luck for the year. Mizuki finished hers in perfect silence and was insufferable about it for weeks. She still eats one every Setsubun in Sydney, facing the direction an app gives her, "and nobody in Australia knows how serious this is".

### February 2001: the flu, and the ten-year-old's letter

In the second week of February she caught influenza, the proper kind, with a fever of nearly thirty-nine degrees. It went through her year like a typhoon and the school closed the whole year group for three days. She spent a week on a futon upstairs with a cold towel on her forehead while the smell of cabbage and sauce came up through the floor, which for once she couldn't stand. Kenta was sent to Fumiko's for safety and posted back drawings of Slimes with misspelled get-well messages. Yumiko came up between customers with rice porridge and grated apple, felt her forehead with the back of her hand, said "Still hot. Sleep," and went back down. Hiroshi came up once, at closing time, sat by the futon for ten minutes in silence, and left a peeled mandarin on the tray with the segments laid out in a circle. "The flu," she says, "is when you find out your family's love language. Ours is fruit."

The worst of it was missing the rehearsals for something she'd been looking forward to all year. Her school, like a growing number then, held a ceremony at the end of fourth grade called the "half coming-of-age" (nibun no ichi seijinshiki): you had turned ten, half of twenty, and each child stood up in front of the parents and read a letter to their family. She was back, pale and croaky, for the ceremony in March. Her letter thanked her mother "for the bento and for hitting Dad with the menu, which is good for him", her father "for the okonomiyaki and for being quiet in a nice way", Fumiko "for the stories, especially the scary ones", and Kenta "for nothing yet, but I am waiting". Yumiko, at the back in her good coat, laughed loudest and then took out a handkerchief. Hiroshi had closed the shop for the morning and stood beside her with his arms folded. He did, Yumiko reported, "blink a lot, for a man in good health". The letter is in a drawer upstairs. Mizuki has never asked to see it again, "because I know I spelled 'grateful' wrong".

### Music in 2000 and 2001: Utada against everybody

Utada Hikaru remained the centre of her musical universe. In 2000 there was "Wait & See (Risk)", learned in a week; in early 2001 "Can You Keep A Secret?", the theme of the Monday-night prosecutor drama that every girl in her year could hum and that Yumiko watched with the till drawer open on her lap. Then, on 28 March 2001, Utada's second album, *Distance*, came out on the same day as a greatest hits album by Ayumi Hamasaki, and the magazines treated it like a boxing match. *Distance* sold about three million copies in its first week, a record. Mizuki took the result personally, as a victory for plain T-shirts. It was her eleventh birthday present, from Fumiko, who was told the title three times and wrote it on her hand, and she played it at night on a borrowed portable CD player with the volume so low she had to press the headphones against her ears.

Ayu, she conceded, was impossible to escape; the sixth-grade girls wore her sunglasses on their heads. But at eleven Ayu was someone she looked at, and Utada was someone she listened to. Ayu didn't really get her until junior high.

In late November 2001 came Utada's "Traveling", fast and bright, with a video in which she drifted through a dreamlike ancient Japan full of city lights. Mizuki watched it on Music Station with her nose nearly touching the screen and said it looked like "the Heian period if it had trains". She still sings it at karaoke, at the speed of a woman trying to catch a train.

### Fifth grade, and the umbrella that grew a leg

In April 2001 she turned eleven and moved up to fifth grade: a new classroom on the second floor, a new teacher (a tall, gentle man in his forties who wore the same brown cardigan every day and taught mainly through stories about his dog), and the beginning of feeling senior. She joined the broadcasting committee and read the lunchtime announcements over the school speakers once a week. She became "the funny one on the radio".

At eleven the girls in her year had discovered the junior fashion brands, sweet and loud, with logos across the chest, and the teen magazine *nicola*, passed round until its pages went soft. Mizuki wanted one of those sweatshirts more than almost anything, and Yumiko, after reading the price label in Kintetsu upside down, said, "For that money it should do my accounts." So she wore a plain grey sweatshirt and a denim skirt, ribbon matched to her socks, and told everyone with enormous dignity that she didn't like big logos. It started as sour grapes. Within a year it was a principle. "No logo bigger than my hand" was born, she says, in front of that price label in the spring of 2001.

The big event of fifth grade came in July: the two-night outdoor school trip to a youth lodge in the mountains of Nara. After Osaka in July the cedar-smelling air felt like a fridge. They climbed a small mountain in a long line of yellow caps and cooked curry rice in blackened pots over wood fires in groups of six; Mizuki's group burned it at the bottom and undercooked it at the top, which she still calls "two textures, like a real restaurant". And away from the city she saw more stars than she had known existed, and the faint smudge of the Milky Way, and stood on the gravel path in her slippers with her mouth open until she was sent in.

The second night is the one she remembers. The eight girls in her room were supposed to be asleep by nine. At half past, Saki whispered that someone should tell a scary story, and everyone looked at Mizuki, because everyone knew whose granddaughter she was. She told them the umbrella yokai exactly as Fumiko told it, sweetly and slowly, with long pauses: an old paper umbrella forgotten in a storeroom for a hundred years, sad and angry about being forgotten, which one rainy night opens by itself, grows one eye, one long tongue, and then one single leg, and hops, very quietly, down the corridor towards the room where the children who forget their umbrellas are sleeping. Not footsteps, because it has only one foot: a soft tap, then nothing, then a soft tap. She paused. Somebody's stomach rumbled. Then, very gently, Mizuki tapped her knuckle once on the floor beside Saki's futon.

Saki screamed so loudly that a teacher came running down the corridor in his pyjamas, slid the door open, switched on the light, and found seven girls screaming and one, Mizuki, sitting with her hands folded and a perfectly innocent face. All eight were told off; Mizuki slightly more. It was her first proper audience, the first time she felt a whole room go still because of the way she was telling something, and she has been chasing that feeling ever since: at the shop counter, at karaoke, at the hotel front desk and now, every weekday morning, down the phone to one friend. When she told Fumiko, her grandmother asked only one thing: "Did you do the tap?" She had. Fumiko nodded with deep professional satisfaction and went back to her pickles.

### 20 July 2001: Spirited Away

The following week, at the start of the summer holidays, Studio Ghibli's *Spirited Away* (*Sen to Chihiro no Kamikakushi*) came out, and Fumiko announced she was taking the children, "because it's about a bath house, and I know about bath houses". They went to a big cinema in Namba on a hot Sunday, with a queue round the corner; it became the most successful film ever shown in Japan. Mizuki sat between Fumiko and Kenta and for two hours forgot her popcorn. It was the story of a sulky ten-year-old whose parents turn into pigs, and who has to work in a bath house for the gods to save them, and the bath house was full of exactly the creatures Fumiko had been telling her about since she could walk: river gods, a radish spirit, frog men, soot sprites carrying coal.

She loved the soot sprites ("the same family as in *Totoro*; they've moved for work"). She loved the scene where Chihiro rides the train across the flooded sea, nobody talking, water to the horizon, and still thinks it the most beautiful scene in any film. And she was frightened, in the good slow way, of No-Face, the masked spirit who follows Chihiro silently and wants to be noticed. Kenta, seven, was so frightened of No-Face's eating rampage that he climbed into Fumiko's lap and stayed there until the credits, and for a month afterwards wouldn't go to the toilet at night unless Mizuki stood outside the door.

On the train home Fumiko gave her verdict on the filthy river spirit who turns out to be noble once someone pulls the rubbish out of him. "That's Osaka. Everyone thinks it's dirty. Pull out the bicycle and it's a god." Mizuki repeated it at school, where nobody understood it. *Spirited Away* is still her favourite Ghibli film by a long way, and the template for what she likes in a story: a little sad, a little frightening, quietly kind, and full of creatures your grandmother would recognise.

### August 2001: Universal Studios Japan

Universal Studios Japan opened on 31 March 2001 on the old industrial waterfront in Konohana ward, on the west side of the city, with its own new JR station, Universal City, and for the whole of that spring it was the only thing anyone at school talked about. Mizuki had not been, and suffered. Then in July Yumiko announced at the staff meal that they were going on a Wednesday in August, the shop's day off, and that Daisuke was coming from Kobe. She did not say how much it cost. An adult ticket was about five and a half thousand yen, and she would quote the total, to the yen, for the rest of the year, usually when somebody left a light on.

They left at seven on the hottest day of the summer, by the Loop line to Nishikujo and the new line out to Universal City, and met Daisuke at the station: sixteen, in his first year of high school, taller than Hiroshi, in an Orix cap. "The audience is here," he said, as always. "The player is late," she said, though he wasn't.

Inside it was like walking into a film she hadn't seen. Back to the Future, a flying-car ride in front of a huge domed screen, made Yumiko feel sick and made Hiroshi say "Ah" very quietly when the car fell into a volcano. On the Jaws boat ride Kenta hid under the bench. On E.T., riding a bicycle over a little town at night, the alien said goodbye to each rider by name, and when he said "Kenta", Kenta was so astonished he kept waving for ten seconds after the ride had moved on. Mizuki thought it was the most magical thing in the park, and also faintly creepy: "How did he know?"

And then there was the Jurassic Park ride: a calm river boat through a jungle of gentle long-necked dinosaurs and mist, until everything went wrong, as it does in Jurassic Park. Alarms, raptors hissing in the dark, a Tyrannosaurus bursting out right over the boat, and a drop of about twenty-five metres into the lagoon below. Staff sold plastic ponchos at the entrance. Yumiko bought two, for herself and Kenta. Hiroshi declined, saying it was "a free shower". Daisuke declined because he was sixteen. Mizuki declined because Daisuke had.

They sat in the front row. The drop was much higher than it looked from outside, and she screamed so loudly she stopped hearing herself. Then the boat hit the water and a wave came over the front and soaked her and Daisuke from hair to shoes, as if someone had thrown a bath at them. She came off streaming, fringe plastered flat, sandals squelching, laughing so hard she couldn't stand up straight, and Daisuke was just as wet, his cap dripping, laughing like a much younger boy. Hiroshi, wet down one side, said, "That was a good shower." Mizuki and Daisuke went straight back into the queue and got soaked again, and by then nobody cared, because the sun was so hot they dried in twenty minutes walking round the lagoon.

It was the happiest day of that year, and she can't fully explain why. Partly the ride, the heat and the screaming. But mostly, she thinks, it was that her whole family was there on a day off, doing something silly, which almost never happened; and that Daisuke, who had been a frightened boy at Fumiko's in 1995, was laughing in a wet cap with nothing of that winter left in his face. "Some days are just right. You don't notice at the time. Then twenty years later you still have the wet sandals in your head."

They ate churros, and Hiroshi, who would not usually pay for anything he could make himself, bought everyone a hot dog the length of his forearm because Kenta said it was "what Americans eat", and they ate them on a kerb in fake New York with mustard on their wet shirts. At the end Yumiko allowed one souvenir each. Mizuki chose a small rubber T-rex, which she named Hiroshi, "because he's quiet and then he isn't". Kenta chose an E.T. key ring, since E.T. knew his name.

### Autumn 2001: the news, and the Tigers

On the night of 11 September 2001 the shop television was on for the end of a baseball game when the programme was interrupted. It was nearly ten o'clock in Osaka. Mizuki had come down in her pyjamas for a glass of barley tea and saw a tall building in New York with smoke pouring from it, and then, live, a second plane. The last customers stopped eating. Hiroshi turned off the hotplate and stood very still with the scraper in his hand. After a minute Yumiko said quietly, "Upstairs," and Mizuki went, and lay on her futon listening to the television through the floor. She didn't understand much of it. She understood that her parents had gone quiet the way they went quiet about January 1995, and that the world was bigger and less safe than it looked from Abeno. She doesn't tell this one on the phone. She just remembers the scraper in her father's hand.

The Tigers finished last again in 2001, the fourth season running. Hiroshi said "mada mada" so often that Kenta started saying it about his homework. In December the club announced a new manager, Senichi Hoshino, a famously fierce man with a legendary temper, and the regulars argued about it for a week. Grandpa Isamu, at seat one with his canned coffee, said a man with a temper was exactly what the Tigers needed, "because the players certainly haven't got one". Hiroshi said nothing, but he hummed.

### Small bad things

Not everything was theme parks. In the summer of 2000 she failed the school's twenty-five-metre swimming test in the outdoor pool; at fifteen metres she remembered falling off her bike and stood up in the middle of the lane with everyone watching. She passed it in fifth grade, slowly, at the edge of the lane, "in a time that could not be measured by science", and is prouder of that pass than of most things.

That autumn Tanaka the goldfish got white spots and floated at a sad tilt, and Kenta sat in front of the tank all evening refusing dinner. Hiroshi, who had never shown the slightest interest in the fish, went to the pet shop in the arcade on his day off, gave Tanaka a salt bath in a mixing bowl and changed the water every day for a week, without comment. Tanaka recovered. Kenta wrote his father a thank-you letter, which Hiroshi folded into his apron pocket.

In the spring of 2001, clearing Fumiko's low table, Mizuki dropped one of her grandmother's teacups, an old blue-and-white one that had been Grandpa Shigeru's, and it broke into three pieces. She burst into tears before it had stopped rolling. Fumiko looked at the pieces, said, "Ah, he never liked this one anyway," and glued it back together over two evenings. She used it every day afterwards and said it had more character. Mizuki has had a soft spot for mended things ever since: a darned sock, a re-sewn button, a cup with a seam.

And there were smaller sorrows: a shiny Kyukon card lent to a boy in her class and never returned; a recorder test where her fingers forgot "Edelweiss" three times; and the Osaka summer itself, which she hated more each year, the cicadas screaming in the trees by Shitennoji and the polyester PE shorts sticking to her legs. "I have been sweating since 1990. Sydney people do not know what they are complaining about."

### Fishing is waiting

On a Wednesday at the end of the summer holidays of 2001, Hiroshi took her fishing off the rocks in Wakayama for the first time. She was given a small rod, a bucket and a rice ball, and sat on a cold rock for four hours. She caught nothing. He caught one small fish and threw it back. On the train home she asked what the point had been. He thought about it from Wakayama to Tennoji and finally said, "Fishing is waiting. Some days the waiting is the fish." She thought it was the most annoying thing he had ever said. She now says it's the best advice she has ever had about photography.

### Winter 2001: counting the money

By the end of 2001 Mizuki was eleven, in fifth grade, and a Final Fantasy fan who did not own a single Final Fantasy game. That bothered her more every week. Final Fantasy X had come out for the PlayStation 2 in July, and whenever its advert came on the shop television, all blue sea and sad music, she turned up the volume and stood in front of it until a customer asked her to move. The family did not have a PlayStation 2, and nobody had said anything about getting one. She began, quietly, to count her savings: pocket money, all but one of the 2,000-yen notes her mother had made "your problem", and the savings book from Grandma Chiyo, which she was not allowed to touch. It was not enough. She began to think very seriously about otoshidama.

On Christmas Day 2001, a normal working day in Osaka, the shop television showed the very first M-1 Grand Prix, the new national contest for young manzai double acts, and Mizuki sat at the end of the counter with an order pad and scored every act out of ten, with her reasons written out in full. She disagreed with two of the judges and agreed with the result. She still scores comedy, cooking shows and K-pop comebacks the same way, on whatever paper is nearest, and a few of those order pads, with her merciless childish marks ("4. Tsukkomi too slow. Boke was sad"), are still in the drawer under the till.

What her father brought home after closing that same night, in a large plastic bag from an electronics shop in Namba, is the start of the next part of her story.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Utada Hikaru**: *First Love* in the summer of the cast, then *Distance* and "Traveling" ("the Heian period if it had trains"). Songs that are sweet with a bruise in them.
- **Plain clothes, chosen on purpose**: Utada in a T-shirt; ribbons matched to socks; fruit-shaped hair clips, never a red cherry ("that position is filled"). **"No logo bigger than my hand"**, born at a Kintetsu price label in 2001.
- **Ferris wheels over roller coasters**: "A roller coaster is for proving something. A Ferris wheel is for looking." Judges every city by its wheel.
- **Dislikes wooden and stand-up coasters**: Elf made her cry ("an old house that wants to kill you"); Fujin Raijin II and the 2007 news. Steel only, checked in advance.
- **Getting soaked on purpose**: the Jurassic Park ride with Daisuke, twice; the happiest day of 2001. Now buys the poncho.
- **One Piece and Nami**, "the only sensible person on the ship"; small sweet mikan.
- **Final Fantasy over Dragon Quest**: IX and Vivi ("a picture book you're allowed to walk into").
- **Spirited Away**: her favourite Ghibli; the train across the sea; "pull out the bicycle and it's a god".
- **Telling scary stories slowly**: the umbrella yokai and the tap on the floor; her first real audience.
- **Osaka comedy**: if you fall over, you are now the act; scoring comedy out of ten since the first M-1.
- **Festivals and rituals**: melon shaved ice ("I respect the honesty"); gold willow fireworks; hanging the Ebisu bamboo straight; eho-maki in total silence.
- **Mended and second-hand things with a story**: Fumiko's glued teacup; the bargain fox from the flea market.
- **Keepsakes**: a 2,000-yen note in a book (the only fight her mother lost); a torch by the front door since Y2K.
- **Dislikes**: the plaster cast and the arm under it; the Osaka summer ("sweating since 1990"); liver stew; lending things; flu; Kenta reading Jump with his mouth open.
