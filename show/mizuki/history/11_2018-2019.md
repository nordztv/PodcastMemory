## Chapter 11: Barefoot country (March 2018 to 2019)

### March 2018: one suitcase, one fox and far too much light

Mizuki landed at Sydney Airport on a warm morning in the middle of March 2018, after an overnight flight from Kansai on which she had slept for about forty minutes and watched Your Name for the fourth time (it still counted). She was twenty seven. She had one large suitcase, a backpack with a Vulpix keyring clipped to the zip so she would "have one friend from home", and her father's Nikon FM2 in her hand luggage, wrapped in a T-shirt with ten rolls of film. Her mother had packed furikake, instant miso soup, a bag of dashi powder and a small bottle of the shop's own okonomiyaki sauce, "in case Australia has no sauce". Australia did have sauce. Mizuki didn't tell her mother for about a year, because the bottle had been packed with such seriousness.

The airport train ran underground almost the whole way and cost so much she read the gate screen twice to check she hadn't paid for two people, so her first view of the city was Central Station: sandstone, a clock tower, people walking very fast with very large coffees. Her first four nights were in a backpackers' hostel near Central, in a five-bunk room with a Swedish girl who sang in her sleep. She lay awake the first night thinking, quite calmly, "What have I done?" Then she slept eleven hours.

The thing she noticed first, and still talks about most, was the light. Osaka light, she says, is soft and slightly grey, filtered through humidity and buildings and the breath of a few million people. Sydney light came down as if someone had turned the brightness to maximum and lost the remote. Shadows had edges. White walls hurt. Her first photo in Australia, taken through the window of the bus to Glebe on the way to look at a room, was of nothing at all: a paling fence, a gum tree with its bark peeling off in strips, and that light lying across both. She still has the print, one of the few from that first roll that survived. "It's a terrible photo," she says. "But look how bright the terrible is."

Her plan, as she explained to anyone who asked, was very sensible. A working holiday visa was good for one year. She would work, travel a little, practise her English on people who weren't hotel guests, photograph everything, and go home to Osaka in the spring of 2019 with a suitcase of stories. Her mother had already planned the welcome-home dinner. Her father, at the hotplate on the last night, had said only "Take the camera", which she had. Kenta had set up the family video calls and made their mother practise pressing the green button until she stopped pressing the red one.

She did not go home in the spring of 2019. That is what this chapter is about.

### The man with no shoes

On her fifth day, settled into the Glebe house but still buying groceries one bag at a time, she went to the supermarket on Glebe Point Road for milk, bread and something that looked like rice. In the cereal aisle she met a man of about forty with a basket over his arm, a calm face and no shoes. Not thongs. Not socks. Bare feet, on the supermarket floor, among the cornflakes, calm as a monk.

Mizuki stopped next to the tinned tomatoes and could not move. In Japan you take your shoes off to go inside a house, you put on slippers, you change into different slippers for the toilet, and you would sooner eat a raw onion than walk round a shop barefoot and then into your home on the same feet. Something in her brain, she says, "made the noise a computer makes before it turns off". She watched him pay, chat to the checkout girl about the cricket, and walk out into the sunshine, still barefoot, as if this were completely normal. It was completely normal. Over the next month she saw bare feet in a bottle shop, at a petrol station, on a bus and once, unforgivably, at a post office counter.

She rang Aya that night and explained, in very fast Osaka-ben, that she had moved to a country with no rules. Aya laughed so hard she had to put the phone down on her desk, and when she picked it up again she said, "So it's Mizuki's barefoot country now?" By the end of the week that was the name of the family group chat, and it still is. Her mother still occasionally types "how is barefoot country" as a whole message, with no question mark.

### The Glebe house

She found the room on a flat-sharing website before she left Japan and moved in on her fifth day. It was a narrow two-storey terrace in Glebe, painted a cream that had given up, with an iron-lace balcony, a frangipani in a front yard the size of a picnic blanket, and a back door that never quite shut, so that in winter a thin knife of cold air lay along the kitchen floor. Her room was the smallest, upstairs at the back, over the courtyard, with a ceiling fan that wobbled at every speed. It cost two hundred and sixty dollars a week, which she converted into yen every Sunday for a month and then had to stop for her health. After two rooms above an okonomiyaki shop, it felt like a palace.

The house had four people, two languages that weren't English, and one bathroom.

**Rafa and Bia** (Rafael and Beatriz Souza) were a married couple from São Paulo, a few years older than Mizuki, on student visas and cheerfully determined to stay as long as Australia would let them. Rafa worked on building sites in a hi-vis shirt he wore everywhere, including, once, to a wedding. He was loud, generous and permanently barbecuing, believed any problem could be solved by meat, and claimed Japanese people ate meat "like it's medicine, in tiny pieces". He called her "Mizu" from the first day and "chefe" (boss) from the first week, because she had already reorganised the kitchen. He sang Brazilian football songs in the shower and was, she says, "the worst singer in the Southern Hemisphere and the happiest".

Bia studied English at a college in the city and cleaned offices three nights a week. She was small and quick and danced while she cooked, forró or samba, hips going while she stirred the beans. She took Mizuki's English seriously, correcting her gently ("not 'I am boring', querida, 'I am bored', unless you mean it") and asking to be corrected back, and the two of them did their homework at the kitchen table in the evenings over Bia's thick, sweet coffee: two foreigners working Australia out together. Bia also made a Brazilian salad with raisins in it, about which Mizuki still has very strong feelings.

**Fiona MacLeod** was a nurse from Glasgow, about Mizuki's age, two years into Sydney, working nights at a big hospital in Camperdown, ten minutes' walk away. She was dry, kind and tired, and talked to the house mainly through capital-letter notes on the fridge: "WHO ATE MY YOGHURT. I KNOW IT WAS RAFA. — F." "MIZUKI, THANK YOU FOR THE LABELS, I FEEL SAFE FOR THE FIRST TIME IN TWO YEARS. — F." She slept by day with earplugs and an eye mask and emerged at three in a dressing gown to make toast and complain about the sun. She missed Irn-Bru and rain, and did not miss Glasgow winters "in any way, at all, ever". Mizuki noticed early that Fiona talked about patients the way her father talked about regulars: briefly, without names, with obvious care.

Fiona taught Mizuki that Scottish people complain about the weather the way Osaka people complain about Kyoto: constantly, lovingly and competitively. Mizuki taught Fiona the word "otsukaresama", which you say to someone who has worked hard, and Fiona adopted it immediately and began saying it to everyone coming home, including Rafa, who had only been to the shops. Of all the people in the house it was Fiona who became the friend; neither of them knew that yet.

Mizuki became the tidy one, which surprised nobody who had met her mother. Within a week she had labelled the fridge shelves (in English and Japanese, "for practice"), put a shoe rack by the front door with a sign ("SHOES OFF. This is not the supermarket"), drawn a bin calendar so the house finally knew that yellow was recycling and went out on Tuesday, and bought a small set of plastic drawers for the bathroom so that Rafa's things lived in one place. Rafa called this "the Japanese occupation". He kept his shoes off anyway, and by winter was lecturing visitors about it.

On Friday nights she made okonomiyaki on a borrowed electric frying pan at the kitchen table: "Tanaka Fridays". When the shop's sauce ran out she mixed supermarket barbecue sauce with Worcestershire, "a crime with a good result". Everyone put tomato sauce on it anyway; she fought this for six months and lost. Rafa paid her back with Saturday barbecues of picanha and sausages that Fiona called "an insult to Scottish sausages, which are already an insult". The house ate together two or three nights a week, a habit none of them had planned.

The house taught her a lot about Australia, partly because almost nobody in it was Australian. The landlord, a retired teacher in the Blue Mountains, came twice a year, admired the labels and fixed nothing. The old man next door, who grew tomatoes in his front yard and read the paper on his step, said "G'day" every morning for six months and nothing else, then one day asked whether it was true that Japanese trains were never late. She said yes. He said "Bloody hell" with deep respect and went back to his paper. She counts this as her first Australian friendship.

She loved Glebe at once and still does: the Saturday markets in the school grounds on Glebe Point Road, with vintage denim she could afford; a second-hand bookshop with a shelf of Japanese paperbacks left behind by students, where she found an old Edogawa Rampo collection for four dollars; the light rail rattling past Wentworth Park; the big Moreton Bay figs and corner pubs. And at dusk the fruit bats, thousands of them, streaming over Jubilee Park towards the harbour, black against an orange sky. "In Japan," she says, "this is a yokai story. Here it's Tuesday." She shot it all on the FM2 that first autumn, and the bats never came out.

### What she loved, what she hated, and why

Her first year was one long list of comparisons, kept partly in her journal and partly in the family group chat, and she still uses it. Nearly every opinion she has about Australia was formed in 2018, in the gap between what she expected and what she found.

**Birds.** In Osaka the birds are crows, pigeons and sparrows. On her first morning in Glebe a sulphur-crested cockatoo landed on the balcony rail and screamed at her like a car alarm with feathers, its yellow crest going up as if it was surprised by its own noise. They scream at dawn every day, so the grudge is renewed daily. The ibis, picking through the bins in Victoria Park, Rafa told her were called "bin chickens"; she thought they looked like ancient Egyptian gods fallen on hard times, and adored them slightly against her will. Rainbow lorikeets she loved without reservation ("a fruit parfait that can fly"). Magpies she would learn about in spring.

**Coffee.** At home coffee was sweet and canned from a vending machine, or drip coffee in a quiet old kissaten. In Sydney it was a religion with strict priests. She learned the flat white in her first week and decided it was the best thing Australia had invented, "better than the Opera House, which you cannot drink". But a "large" was a soup bowl: she has ordered a small ever since, and calls the big ones "coffee baths". Walking down the street drinking is mildly rude in Japan, so for months she stood beside the café to finish her cup, until she was late for work three times.

**Portions.** Her first pub burger was the size of a small hat. As a child of kuidaore, Osaka's eat-till-you-drop food culture, she approved, but she was raised in a shop where nothing went in the bin, so she started carrying a little container for leftovers, which Bia laughed at and then copied.

**"No worries."** She heard it a hundred times a day, from baristas, bus drivers, the woman at the bank and the man she accidentally hit with her suitcase on George Street, and decided it was the perfect phrase: polite without being stiff, a bow without bending. She also learned that "How are you going?" is not a question about transport, after answering "by bus" twice to the same bemused barista. And she learned that "How are you?" doesn't want an answer, which she found both relaxing and a little sad.

**Names and manners.** Being called by her first name by her manager, her bank and her doctor felt rude for two weeks, then wonderful, like taking off a tight shoe. She loved "Thanks, driver" when people got off the bus, and strangers chatting in queues (Osaka people chat too, she points out, but not to the bus driver, who is concentrating). She did not love feet on train seats, speakerphone calls in the carriage, or full hot meals eaten on the train. "In Osaka you would be looked at. Here nobody looks. That is also a kind of freedom. I don't want it."

**Trains.** Osaka had ruined her: the Midosuji line every few minutes, trains to the second, marks on the platform where each door would open. Sydney trains came every fifteen minutes on a good day and at weekends often not at all because of a mysterious thing called "trackwork", which replaced them with buses that went the long way. "In Osaka we would rather stand, and arrive," she wrote to Kenta. She also learned to tap off with her Opal card, after paying the maximum fare twice. But the ferries won her over completely. The first time she rode from Circular Quay to Manly, outside in the wind with the Opera House sliding past, she decided no train in Japan could beat a ferry commute, and she has never changed her mind.

**Shops and tipping.** In Osaka the konbini never closes. In Sydney in 2018 many shops shut at half past five, Thursday late-night shopping was treated like a festival, and on Sunday evenings the whole city seemed to have gone to bed without telling her. She missed konbini terribly: egg sandwiches, onigiri, hot cans of corn soup, paying the gas bill at two in the morning. A petrol station with a pie warmer was "not the same, but the pie is good". Tipping, at least, was a relief: at the hotel in Umeda she had watched colleagues chase American guests down corridors to give tips back. The café tip jar, though, "makes everyone feel a little bit guilty and nobody a little bit rich".

**Sun.** Japanese women carry parasols and treat the sun like a debt collector. Rafa laughed at hers until he saw "extreme" on the UV forecast on the evening news. On her first visit to Bondi, in her second week, she put sunscreen everywhere except the tops of her feet, which she forgot, and burnt them so badly she couldn't wear shoes for two days. ("I became the barefoot person. It was my punishment.") She has put sunscreen on her feet ever since, and will remind anyone who listens, and anyone who doesn't.

**Food.** Fruit was cheap and enormous; in Osaka a perfect melon is a gift you take to a hospital, and here you bought a whole rockmelon for a few dollars and nobody bowed. Mangoes in summer made her emotional. Supermarket sushi was "a different food that is also called sushi". She tried Vegemite at Fiona's insistence, too thick, and made a face her father would have recognised: it was Australia's natto, a food that tests whether you belong. (Her father calls natto "Tokyo glue"; she calls Vegemite "Australian glue" and now eats it, thinly, on Sundays.) Tim Tams she loved immediately and dangerously; meat pies with tomato sauce were the one place she let the sauce win.

**Houses.** Australian houses are built as if winter is a rumour. The Glebe terrace had no insulation, single-pane windows, one oil heater the house fought over, and that back door. She had left her winter coat in Osaka because "Australia doesn't have winter"; in July she wore a jumper, her denim jacket and a scarf to bed.

**Barbecues and beaches.** These she adopted completely. Free electric barbecues in public parks, kept clean by the council and shared politely by strangers, were proof that Australia was civilised. Osaka has a sea but no real beach culture; Sydney had beaches you could reach by bus. Her favourite became Bronte, smaller and calmer than Bondi, with its ocean pool cut into the rocks and its grassy picnic slope. She swims between the red and yellow flags, carefully, never far out. "Australia has a lot of dangerous animals, but the most dangerous thing is the water. Nobody tells you this on the postcard."

**Spiders.** In April a huntsman the size of her hand appeared above her light switch at eleven at night. Her scream brought Fiona, just home from a shift, up the stairs with a plastic container and a piece of cardboard; she caught it in one movement and said, "They're harmless. They eat the other ones." Mizuki said, "There are OTHER ones?" She has since made peace with huntsmen ("like the zashiki-warashi, the house spirit: if you have one, the house is lucky"), but she still checks above the light switch.

### The café in Haymarket

Her first job, from early April 2018, was at a small café in Haymarket, a block from Chinatown and Paddy's Markets. It was run by a brisk, kind owner who had been making coffee since before Mizuki was born and who hired her after a ten-minute trial in which she wiped down the counter without being asked. Her hotel years made her fast, calm and polite; her okonomiyaki-shop childhood made her good with regulars. She learned to steam milk to the right shine, to pour a heart that looked like a heart (the rosetta took longer), and to write "lg skim latte xhot" on a cup at seven in the morning without judging anyone.

She disliked customers who ordered while on the phone ("in Japan this is a crime, maybe not in law but in the heart") and the café playlist of acoustic covers of songs she already didn't like. She liked the regulars: the nurse off night shift who ordered a piccolo with her eyes closed, the old man who bought one long black at ten and read the paper for exactly an hour, the builders who called her "love". She learned everyone's order within a fortnight, which in her mother's shop was simply the job and in Sydney seemed to astonish people.

She loved Haymarket itself. Chinatown at lunchtime, with roast ducks hanging in windows; the Friday night market on Dixon Street, smoke and lanterns and skewers; cheap dumplings and cheap pho and a Malaysian laksa that made her nose run in the best way; the noise and the smells and the crowds. It was the closest thing in Sydney to Namba, and she felt at home there faster than anywhere else in the city.

On her lunch breaks she walked to a different ramen shop each day and started the notebook that would follow her for years (see below). Most of those first scores were fives.

The café job ended in October, sooner than she wanted, because of a rule she had read about in Japan and forgotten: on a working holiday visa you could only work for one employer for six months. The owner was genuinely sorry, gave her a card and a jar of the house beans, and said "Come back when you're a resident." Mizuki cried a little on the walk home, which surprised her, because it had only been a café. Then she found casual work for the busy end of the year at a Japanese bakery in the city, boxing strawberry shortcakes and melon bread, which felt, she says, like being seventeen in Tennoji again, with better English.

### Nana, and the curry roux

The Japanese grocery near Town Hall was where she went when she was homesick, which in the first months was most Saturdays. It sold natto in the freezer, Kewpie mayonnaise, furikake, real Japanese rice, konbini-style snacks and curry roux at prices that made her stand in the aisle and do sums. One Saturday in late May she was holding a box of curry roux, saying out loud in Osaka-ben, to nobody, "Eight dollars? Obaachan would come back as a yokai to complain," when a voice beside her said, in Kansai-ben, "Kobe obaachan would come back too. They'd go together."

That was **Nana Yoshida**, from Kobe, a couple of years into Sydney, doing a nail course while working at a nail salon in Chatswood on the North Shore. She was small, round-faced and fearless, with each nail a different colour, and she said yes to everything. Coffee? Yes. Dumplings now? Yes. A beach at six in the morning, a bus to somewhere with a funny name? Yes, yes, yes. Within a month she was the friend Mizuki called when she wanted to do something and didn't want to talk herself out of it.

They had the easy closeness of two Kansai people abroad, speaking Kansai-ben loudly on buses. They argued about Osaka against Kobe (Kobe is "Osaka in a nice shirt"; Osaka is "Kobe with no manners") and about okonomiyaki (Nana's family put konnyaku in it, which Mizuki considers a cry for help). Nana did Mizuki's nails once a month for practice, short and plain for kitchen work, with one tiny fox on her left ring finger.

It was Nana, in 2019, who signed them both up for a K-pop dance class at a studio in the city, without asking. "You'll say no," she said. "So I didn't ask." Mizuki went, was terrible, went again, and kept going.

### Late April: the wallet at Central

The worst thing that happened to her in 2018 happened in her second month, on an ordinary weekday evening in late April, at Central Station. She was going home from the café, tired, with her wallet in the outside pocket of her backpack, the way she had carried it every day of her life in Osaka. A train had been cancelled and the concourse was crowded, people standing in clumps looking up at the boards. Someone bumped her, said sorry and moved off. At the gate she reached back for her Opal card, and the pocket was open and empty.

She stood there holding up the line, feeling the empty pocket as if the wallet might be hiding. The station staff were kind, sympathetic and not at all surprised: report it to the police, cancel the cards, it happens a lot. There was no lost property miracle. The wallet was never returned.

What was in it: about sixty dollars in cash; her Australian bank card, only a month old; her Opal card; her Japanese driving licence; a purikura photo strip of her and Aya from high school, both of them with enormous eyes and cat ears drawn on; and a small paper omamori charm from Shitennoji that her mother had pressed into her hand at the airport. The money she could replace. The bank card took a week, during which Fiona lent her two hundred dollars without being asked and refused to discuss it. The purikura and the omamori she could not replace, and it was those she minded.

She walked home to Glebe that night, forty minutes, because she had no card for the bus. She rang her mother halfway, then hung up before it connected so as not to worry her, and rang Aya instead, who listened and then said, in her driest voice, "Well. You did say you wanted to experience a foreign culture." Mizuki laughed and cried at the same time, a very Osaka combination.

What hurt most was the feeling, for weeks, that the city had been laughing at her. At university she had once left her wallet on the seat of a Keihan train and gone to the station office expecting nothing; it came back two days later from the end of the line, handed in by a stranger, with every yen inside and her student card tucked neatly in the front. That was simply what happened in Japan, and she had never thought of it as special because she had never lived anywhere else. Standing on the Central concourse with an empty pocket, she understood that a whole country quietly handing back what isn't theirs is rare and precious, and that she had grown up inside it like a fish in water.

Not the food, not the temples, not the trains: the wallet coming back. That, she says, was when she understood Japan is special. She is careful to add that Sydney is a very safe city, that one pickpocket is not a country, and that she has met wonderfully honest strangers in Australia too. But ever since she has carried her wallet in a zipped front pocket and her phone in her hand on crowded platforms.

Her mother, when Mizuki finally told her a month later, sent a new omamori from Shitennoji in the next parcel, with a note in her square handwriting: "Keep it INSIDE this time."

### Winter, in a country that said it had none

In late May Fiona took her to the Blue Mountains on a day off: two hours on the train from Central, climbing through bush until the air smelt of eucalyptus. At Echo Point they looked at the Three Sisters standing in a blue haze over a valley that went on for ever (the blue, Fiona explained, is gum-tree oil in the air), and Mizuki was briefly speechless, which Fiona noted as a first. In Leura the old gardens had turned red and yellow, and Mizuki, four autumns a Kyoto student, stood under a maple homesick and happy at once. She has gone back nearly every autumn since: Leura in May is "the only place in New South Wales that looks like Kyoto in November".

In June Sydney held Vivid, the winter light festival, with buildings lit in colours and the Opera House sails covered in moving pictures. She went with Nana and Bia and loved it with her whole Osaka heart: crowds, lights, food stalls, the harbour reflecting everything. "The one time Sydney admits it is a little bit Dotonbori," she says.

Then, early one Monday morning in June, she woke to a message from Kenta: "Earthquake in northern Osaka. We're all fine. Shop fine. A cup fell." It was the earthquake of 18 June 2018, centred north of the city. Abeno was shaken but far enough away; her parents were fine, the shop was fine. Her mother rang and talked about the cup for ten minutes. But Mizuki spent the day at the café with her phone in her apron pocket, checking the news at every break, and for the first time she understood what it would be like to be far away when something happened at home. It was the start of a feeling she'd have again, in different ways, for years.

Winter also brought her first proper Sydney storm, which came in sideways, turned her cheap umbrella inside out on Broadway and carried it away over a bus. She bought a proper raincoat that weekend, navy, hooded, with a pocket big enough for a camera. She still has it, and is irrationally proud of it.

### Luna Park, and the face

She had loved theme parks all her life: Nara Dreamland, Hirakata Park, the Ferris wheel at Expoland, the soaking at USJ. So in September, on a bright spring Saturday, she went with Nana and Bia to Luna Park, the old amusement park on the north shore of the harbour, right under the Harbour Bridge. They took the ferry to Milsons Point, which Mizuki considered the correct way to arrive at anything.

And there it was. The entrance to Luna Park is a giant smiling face: an enormous head with staring eyes, round red cheeks and a wide mouth full of teeth, and you walk into the park through the mouth. Mizuki stopped on the boardwalk and would not go further. To her it was not cheerful. It was a yokai. "It looks like a demon who ate a dentist," she said, "and then smiled about it." Fumiko had told her about creatures that swallow travellers whole, and here was an Australian one, painted in nice colours, with a ticket office. Nana laughed until she had to sit on a bench. Bia took Mizuki's arm and walked her through the mouth, both of them looking at the ground, and Mizuki said "Sumimasen" to the face on the way in, just in case.

Inside, she liked it more than she expected: small and old-fashioned next to USJ, with the shabby charm of a place loved for a long time. She rode the Ferris wheel twice for the view of the harbour, the bridge so close she could see the rivets; it reminded her of the wheel at Expoland, and she was quiet for a minute at the top. She screamed on the Wild Mouse, fell over in the funhouse's rotating barrel, went down the big wooden slides on a sack, and ate fairy floss while Bia danced in a queue.

She took one photo of the face on the way out, on the FM2, in black and white, and it came out terrifying. She keeps it face down in the box. Every time she has been back to Luna Park since, she has walked through the mouth looking at her shoes.

### Spring: magpies, a typhoon and the purple hanami

Spring brought magpies, which sing beautifully most of the year and spend a few weeks every spring swooping on anyone near their nests. In September one came at her out of a tree in Victoria Park, clacking its beak an inch from her ear. She ran screaming across the grass, beret in hand, past students in bicycle helmets with cable ties sticking out of them. She adopted a different defence: under certain trees she walks with her umbrella open, rain or no rain, every September since.

In early September the news from home was worse. Typhoon Jebi struck Kansai on 4 September, the strongest in decades; Kansai International Airport, where her plane had taken off six months before, flooded, and a tanker blown loose hit the bridge to the mainland. She watched the videos and felt sick. Her parents were safe; the wind tore the shop's noren and bent the sign, but nobody they knew was hurt. Her father sent a photo of the sign with one word: "Wind." Her mother said the whole street had cleaned up together and the old lady from the tobacco shop had made rice balls for everyone. Mizuki was proud of them and very far away.

Then the jacarandas came out. In late October and November whole streets turned purple, the flowers falling in carpets that stuck to the bottoms of shoes. Japanese people wait all year for the cherry blossoms, she says, and here was a city doing the same thing in purple, casually, without picnic mats. She named it "the purple hanami" and spent a November weekend walking Glebe and Paddington with colour film she couldn't really afford. It became an annual photo walk.

### Clothes, music, games and the walk home

Her style travelled with her almost unchanged: oversized vintage denim jackets, wide-leg trousers, plain white T-shirts, a beret, and her grandfather Isamu's old Seiko, "the old man's watch". Sydney added UNIQLO basics from the George Street shop, the one place where she knew exactly what everything would look like; op-shop finds in Newtown and Glebe, where Australians gave beautiful old wool jumpers to charity for a few dollars ("the most generous thing about the country, apart from the barbecues"); the navy raincoat; a wide-brimmed sun hat, because a beret is "a winter hat lying about being a summer hat"; and sunscreen on her feet, which she counts as an accessory. Her rule: "No logo bigger than my hand."

Her music in 2018 was home with new things mixed in. TWICE's "What is Love?", out that April, was her lock screen and walking song. Kenshi Yonezu's "Lemon" was everywhere in Japan and she played it on the bus feeling pleasantly homesick. Aimyon's "Marigold", released that August, became her song of the year: soft, sunny and slightly sad, "exactly the late-afternoon light in Glebe". Utada Hikaru's new album Hatsukoi came out in June, and Mizuki, an Utada girl since First Love, listened to it all the way through on the Manly ferry and back. At home there were Rafa's football songs, Bia's forró and Fiona's Scottish indie bands, played low for the day sleeper, who was Fiona.

She went to hitokara, solo karaoke, at a karaoke box in Haymarket on bad days: one hour, one room, nobody watching, Utada and Perfume and her mother's Takarazuka songs at full voice. She thinks it is the best therapy ever invented and Australia's failure to adopt it widely is "a national mistake".

Games were Final Fantasy XIV and almost nothing else, on the laptop from Japan, propped on a cushion on her bed. She kept Mizu Nyan, her Miqo'te White Mage, on her Japanese home world; Sydney is only one or two hours ahead, so Japanese prime time fitted her evenings perfectly. Aya still logged in most nights from Osaka, her Paladin standing in front of the White Mage as she had since 2013. "Honestly," Mizuki says, "I didn't go very far." But the old Osaka raid group drifted, and for a few months she played mostly alone.

She photographed constantly that first year, on film, because Sydney's light deserved film. Film and developing were expensive, so she budgeted like rent: two rolls a week. She shot terraces, ferries, bats, ibis, the barefoot people (from behind, politely), Fiona asleep on the couch in her scrubs, Rafa in barbecue smoke, Bia dancing at the stove. Most of those 2018 negatives were lost years later in a leak in her flat, one of the few things she cannot talk about lightly. A few prints survived in a box: the fence and the gum tree, the Luna Park face, and the four housemates squashed on the front step on the self-timer, everyone squinting.

Films that year were few. Late in the year, in a small city cinema, she saw Kore-eda's Shoplifters, about a poor Tokyo family who aren't quite what they seem: quiet and kind with a twist that breaks your heart. She cried on the walk home because it was Japanese and she was not in Japan.

### First Christmas on Bronte (December 2018)

Christmas in Japan had meant strawberry shortcake, fried chicken on Christmas Eve, illuminations along Midosuji and couples on dates; New Year is the family holiday. An Australian Christmas, in the heat, with grandparents and presents and roast meat, was baffling to her twice over. The Glebe house did it in its own mixed-up way, and it is one of her happiest memories.

They went to Bronte for Christmas Day. Rafa left at six in the morning with a car borrowed from a man on his site, two eskies and a camping chair, and had claimed an electric barbecue in the park above the beach before seven, which Mizuki found deeply impressive and slightly Japanese. By ten the grassy slope was covered in families on rugs and the beach below was full of Santa hats and swimmers. Rafa cooked prawns, picanha and sausages all at once, in his hi-vis shirt, worn "for the occasion". Bia brought the raisin salad; Mizuki ate around the raisins with surgical precision and, when caught, said "I am respecting the raisins from a distance." Fiona arrived at midday straight from a night shift, made everyone pull crackers and wear the paper crowns, read the jokes in a flat Glasgow voice, then slept for two hours under a Norfolk Island pine with her crown on.

Mizuki swam in the ocean pool cut into the rocks, as close to the sea as she could get without having to be brave. The water was cold, green and clear, and waves broke over the edge onto the swimmers. She floated on her back under a blue Christmas sky and laughed out loud at how wrong and how right it was. She reminded everyone about sunscreen on their feet. Rafa got burnt anyway, in a perfect hi-vis shape, and she said nothing, loudly.

In the late afternoon they went home to Glebe, sandy and sleepy, and in the backyard at dusk she brought out the strawberry shortcake she had carried home from the Japanese bakery the night before, on her knees on the bus, like a newborn baby: the one Japanese Christmas thing she would not give up. They ate it while the cicadas screamed and the bats flew over, and Bia said it was the best cake she'd ever had, which Mizuki believes to this day.

Then the video call to Osaka, where Christmas is a working day: her mother at the till, her father at the hotplate, Kenta passing the phone round the regulars, who waved. Kenta asked if Santa wore shorts in Australia. "He wears thongs," she said, "and he goes to the supermarket barefoot." Afterwards she cried a little under the wobbling fan, not from sadness exactly but from the distance, which on holidays is longer. Then she came back down and had more shortcake.

Her verdict, unchanged since: Christmas belongs in summer, because you can go to the beach after lunch and fall asleep under a tree. New Year belongs in winter.

### New Year's Eve at Circular Quay (never again)

New Year's Eve in Japan is quiet and serious and cold: a big clean of the house, toshikoshi soba at night for a long life, the temple bells ringing a hundred and eight times at midnight to drive away a hundred and eight worldly desires, and in the morning the first visit to a shrine in the cold air. She had done it every year of her life.

Everyone told her she had to see the Harbour Bridge fireworks once. Nana said yes before being asked; Rafa and Bia came too; Fiona was on nights and said, "I'll see you all in emergency." They reached Circular Quay at three in the afternoon, which Nana said was late. It was. The best spots had been held since morning by people with rugs, chairs and a level of planning Mizuki recognised from hanami in Osaka, where company juniors guard a spot under the cherry trees all day. They got a patch of pavement near the ferry wharves, with a view of the bridge between a lamp post and a very tall man.

Then they waited nine hours, in heat and then humid dark, in an alcohol-free zone that Rafa took personally. Mizuki needed the toilet, she says, for eight of the nine hours, but the queues were endless and the tall man would have taken her spot. The nine o'clock fireworks for children were lovely. The midnight ones were extraordinary: the bridge pouring fire into the water, the sky white and gold and red, the crowd roaring. Her photo, on her last frame, came out as a beautiful bright smudge.

Then everyone tried to leave at once. They walked home to Glebe in a river of people, an hour, her feet aching and her bladder finally at peace. At two in the morning, turning into their street, her phone rang: midnight in Osaka. Her family, crowded round the phone above the shop with the temple bells on TV, wished her a happy new year with their mouths full of soba. She said "Akemashite omedetou" on a Glebe footpath in shorts, and felt she had celebrated New Year twice and properly neither time.

Her verdict, delivered to the group chat at three in the morning: "Beautiful. Never again." She has kept her word. Every year since she has watched the fireworks on television, or from somewhere quiet with a slice of the sky, with a bowl of toshikoshi soba on her knees. "It's the best view in the world," she says, "from my couch."

### Early 2019: the sign at World Square

By January 2019 her visa had two months left, and she had a decision to make that she had been avoiding since about October. She could go home, as planned. She could do the three months of farm work in the country that would give her a second year, which every backpacker talked about and which, she says, "for a girl from an okonomiyaki shop, sounded like a horror film with strawberries". Or she could find an employer who would sponsor her to stay and work properly, which everyone said was hard.

Late in January, walking back from the bakery through the city, she passed World Square, the big tower-and-shops complex on George Street, and saw a handwritten sign in the window of a small ramen shop: "Staff wanted", in English and in Japanese. The Japanese was in good, firm handwriting. She stood in front of it for a while. Then, being Hiroshi Tanaka's daughter, she came back at three o'clock the next day, the quiet hour, when any sensible owner has time to talk.

The owner was **Mr Kondo** (Kondo Shinji): a solid, cheerful man from Sapporo in his late forties, with a white towel tied round his head, forearms like a sumo wrestler's and a laugh that filled the shop. He had opened it in 2015 after years cooking in Japan and, since 2006, in other people's kitchens in Sydney. (Mizuki, asked to guess his age a few weeks later, said "fifties". He was forty-eight. He has not forgiven her.) She bowed, introduced herself and said, "Four years at Ishida-san's tonkotsu shop near Namba, evenings, during university." He put down his ladle.

They talked for forty minutes and disagreed about everything. He was a Sapporo miso man, rich and buttery, with sweetcorn and a pat of butter melting on top, and called Hakata tonkotsu "pig soup for people in a hurry". She called miso ramen "soup for people who are cold". He thought noodles should be thick and wavy; she thought thin and firm. He thought the customer should be left in peace; she thought the customer should never wait for water. He laughed, refilled her tea, and hired her on the spot. "You argue like an Osaka person," he said. "The customers will like it."

She started in early February 2019. The shop served Mr Kondo's miso, a shoyu, and a tonkotsu he made because Sydney demanded it and didn't really believe in. She spent her first six months helping him improve it, quoting Mr Ishida like scripture: boil the bones longer, much longer; the noodles must be firm enough to fight back; the fat on top matters. Mr Ishida's rules came with her in other ways too. On her second day she rearranged the water jugs so that every table was four steps from one. Mr Kondo watched, said nothing, and the next week bought two more jugs.

She learned him quickly: generous with staff meals, strict about cleaning, terrible at paperwork, good with frightened young workers. He followed the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters with a quiet hopelessness that reminded her of her father and the Tigers. And he hummed when he was worried, tuneless, over the stockpot or the accounts, and the staff learned to read it like a weather forecast. Light humming: a supplier problem. Low, continuous humming: the landlord had rung. Silence and singing: a good day.

Her co-worker was **Lily Park**, Korean-Australian, seventeen and just starting at UTS, who had been working weekend shifts at the shop since 2018 and now fitted them around her lectures: quick, sarcastic, sharp with difficult customers in a way that made them apologise, singing under her breath all shift. On Mizuki's first day Lily saw TWICE's "What is Love?" on her lock screen and said, "Oh no. You're a TWICE person." Lily was a Red Velvet devotee, and their great argument of 2019, Red Velvet's "Bad Boy" against TWICE's "Fancy" (released in April and played in the storeroom until Lily threatened to quit), ran all year, through choreography, vocals and whether a song can be "too cute" (Lily: yes; Mizuki: never, how dare you). They both knew the argument was the fun part.

Lily taught her to say no to a customer the Australian way ("you don't apologise five times, you say 'sorry, we can't do that one' and smile like it was their idea"). Mizuki taught Lily to fold a towel the Japanese way, wipe a counter in straight lines, and bow just enough to make a difficult customer feel important. Lily quickly felt like a younger sister: someone to look after, to tease, and to be secretly impressed by.

The regulars became her city family: the tradie in hi-vis who ordered tonkotsu with extra garlic every Wednesday and called her "boss"; the quiet Japanese businessman who nodded once and left exact change; uni students splitting one plate of gyoza four ways; and an elderly woman who came every Monday for the shoyu and told Mizuki she was too thin. Mizuki remembered every order. In her mother's shop, that was the whole job.

### The paperwork

It was Mr Kondo who brought it up, in March, a few weeks before her visa ran out. He had been humming for two days. Then one afternoon in the quiet hour he sat down opposite her with two cups of tea and a folder and said, without looking at her, "If you want to stay, I can sponsor you. If you want to go home, that's fine too. Think about it."

She thought about it for one night. She rang her parents, which took an hour, most of which was her mother. Her father, at the end, said only, "Is he a good boss?" She said yes. He said, "Then stay." She went back the next day and said yes.

She keeps the details light, partly because the details were boring and partly because they were stressful. There was a migration agent, a great deal of paperwork, documents from Osaka that her mother sent by express post in an envelope covered in her handwriting ("IMPORTANT. DO NOT BEND. THIS IS MY DAUGHTER'S LIFE."), a description of her job in words she would never use about herself, and a long wait. While the application was being considered she stayed on a bridging visa and kept working. For months she checked her email at every break, in the storeroom, among the bags of flour. Mr Kondo, who was paying for most of it, hummed more than usual and never once mentioned the cost.

It came through in the spring of 2019. She opened the email in the storeroom, read it three times, and walked out into the shop, where Mr Kondo was cutting chashu. She bowed to him, very deeply, the forty-five degree bow she had given Mr Hamada when she left the hotel. He went red, waved the knife, said "Yes, yes, back to work," and then hummed something that was almost a tune. Lily cried. The Wednesday tradie, who happened to be in, bought everyone a can of soft drink.

She was staying. She told her mother, who announced it to every regular in the shop for a month ("our Mizuki is staying in Australia, with kangaroos, permanently, maybe"), and cried, and then sent a box with new dashi powder and a winter jumper, because "you will not be on a holiday now, you need a jumper".

### Tsukimi Noodles and Kazu

In mid-2018, playing alone most nights, she had answered a recruiting message in the game for a small Free Company called **Tsukimi Noodles**. It was named after tsukimi udon or soba, the noodle dish with a raw egg cracked on top like a full moon. It was a handful of players: mostly Japanese people living in Australia, a couple in New Zealand, and a few Australians learning Japanese who typed slowly and carefully in hiragana and were treated like precious children. They had a small house in the Lavender Beds, the leafy forest housing district, decorated like a soba shop with a noren curtain over the door and a lot of wooden furniture placed at angles that hurt Mizuki to look at.

The leader was **Kazu** (Kazuhiro), a Japanese dad who lived in Sydney's north-west, in the Hills, with his wife and two small children, the elder of whom, his son, sometimes said "konnichiwa" on voice chat in a tiny voice and then ran away. He played a towering Roegadyn tank, enormous and slow and immovable, and he was in real life, from his voice, exactly the same: calm, patient, funny in a dry way, never in a hurry. He logged off at nine sharp on school nights, because his kids' bedtime stories were his job and he took it more seriously than any raid. "Kazu-san is the only tank in Eorzea," Mizuki says, "who has a curfew."

She became their healer within a week, because they didn't have one and she was, she will tell you, very good. She redecorated the house within a month, because "the lighting was a crime": she moved everything, hung lanterns, put a counter in the front room with bowls on it so it looked like a real noodle shop, and made a little garden out the back with a stone lantern and a bench. Kazu walked his huge Roegadyn in, looked around for a long time, and said in party chat, "We live in a better house now. Thank you, Mizuki-san." She has been in charge of the decorating ever since.

It was her first community in Sydney that spoke her language, and it mattered more than she expected. All day she spoke good English and was always slightly performing; in Tsukimi Noodles she could be lazy and fast and Kansai. They ran dungeons and treasure maps, held a New Year festival in the FC house, and fished together in silence on quiet nights, "the most Japanese thing you can do in a video game". Aya joined some runs as a guest and was declared an honorary member, which she pretended not to care about. By the end of 2019, two early-evening raid nights a week, with Kazu tanking and Mizuki healing and everyone done before his nine o'clock curfew, had become a fixed weekly rhythm. (In 2022, with the kids older, they moved to Tuesday and Thursday at nine, where they still are.)

### The ramen notebook

The notebook began in April 2018, on her lunch breaks from the café, and it is still going. It is a plain A5 notebook from a stationery shop in Osaka that she brought from home, then a second, then a third. Every ramen shop she tried in Sydney got a page, written in Japanese in her small neat hand: the date, the shop (described, never named, even to herself, in case anyone ever read it: "the one with the red lanterns near Haymarket", "the basement one with the queue"), what she ordered, a drawing of the bowl, a score out of ten, and a final line headed "Ishida-san would say:".

The scores in 2018 were mostly fives and sixes. The "Ishida-san would say" lines are her favourite part, and she reads them aloud to people who ask. "Ishida-san would say: this is soup, not broth." "Ishida-san would say: who hurt these noodles?" "Ishida-san would say: why is there corn, is this Sapporo, is this a cry for help?" "Ishida-san would say nothing, and leave money on the counter, and never come back." Once, for a bowl she liked very much in her first winter: "Ishida-san would say: not bad. (This is a seven.)"

Her opinions of Sydney ramen, fixed in those two years, are frequently and freely shared. The broth is often thin or too salty, cooked for four hours instead of eighteen. The noodles are too soft, cooked as if someone is afraid of them. The toppings are piled on like a salad, so you can't find the soup. Sydney people photograph ramen beautifully, which she loves, and then leave half the soup, which she does not, because the soup is the work. And they slurp too shyly, or not at all; in Japan slurping is a compliment to the cook and also cools the noodles, and she began telling customers so at Mr Kondo's, with the result that some regulars now slurp proudly when she's watching.

Nothing in the notebook has ever scored above eight. Not in Sydney, not in Melbourne, not anywhere in Australia. An eight was given exactly twice in these years, both times with an apology to Mr Ishida in the margin. And Mr Kondo's tonkotsu, after six months of their work together, scored a seven and a half. She told him so, standing at the stockpot. He looked at her for a while and said he'd expected a six. She said that was why it was a seven and a half. He hummed, pleased, all afternoon.

### May 2019: a new era, and Melbourne

On 1 May 2019 Japan began a new imperial era, Reiwa, and the old one, Heisei, the era she had been born and grown up in, ended. Her mother sent a photo of the shop's calendar with "Heisei" crossed out and "Reiwa" written above it in marker, and a photo of the television, which had been showing the ceremonies all day. In Sydney it was a Wednesday at the ramen shop. Mr Kondo, who felt it too, made a special bowl for the staff meal, with an egg on top like a full moon, and said "Reiwa, then." Mizuki says it was the first time she felt like an expatriate rather than a traveller: watching her own country turn a page from the other side of the world, on a phone, at lunch.

At the end of May, with her visa safely lodged and a long weekend of her own, she went to **Melbourne** with Nana. It was her first trip in Australia beyond day trips, and she treated it with full Japanese school-trip seriousness: an itinerary in her journal with times, a list of laneways, a list of cafés, a list of ramen shops, and a section headed "Melbourne vs Sydney: questions to answer". Nana, who had not read the itinerary, said yes to all of it.

They flew down on a cheap early flight and shared a small hotel room in the city, where Nana snored gently "like a little scooter". Melbourne was cold, grey and windy, and Mizuki loved it at once because it felt more like a Japanese city than Sydney: a grid of streets, trams, old buildings, people in black coats walking fast, and weather that changed four times a day so everyone carried an umbrella, a jumper and sunglasses at once. "Melbourne people are always prepared," she said. "Like Japanese people. Or like people who have been hurt."

The laneways were better than the photos. Hosier Lane, every wall layered with graffiti and paste-ups like the inside of a comic book, full of tourists photographing each other (she photographed the tourists). Degraves Street, narrow and steaming with little cafés under awnings. Centre Place, so narrow you could touch both sides. She walked them with the FM2 in soft grey light, the best light she'd found in Australia for faces: "Sydney light is a spotlight. Melbourne light is a lamp."

The coffee question she answered scientifically: a flat white in six cafés in two days, Nana finishing the halves. Her verdict still stands: Melbourne coffee is better on average, Sydney coffee is better at its best, and Melbourne people care more and talk about it longer, "which is the most Japanese thing about them".

They rode the free trams in the city centre end to end because they could, admired Flinders Street Station ("a palace for trains, finally a country that respects trains"), and ate hot jam doughnuts in the cold at the Queen Victoria Market with sugar down their coats. Mizuki tried three ramen shops around Chinatown on Little Bourke Street for the notebook: six, six and a half, seven. ("Ishida-san would say: it's cold here, so they've made it rich. Good thinking. Still too soft.") On the last night Nana talked her into a rooftop bar, where one glass of wine made her very pink and very chatty, and she explained the whole plot of Stormblood to a bartender who hadn't asked.

On the last full day they took a coach tour along the **Great Ocean Road**, the coastal road west of Melbourne, a long day there and back. She had seen pictures for years: cliffs, surf, golden limestone stacks in a blue sea. They got fog. It rolled in before they reached the coast and stayed all day, the sea a grey sound somewhere to the left. At Kennett River the guide showed them wild koalas asleep in the forks of gum trees, completely uninterested; Mizuki took eleven photos of the same one. At Apollo Bay they ate fish and chips in drizzle. And at the Twelve Apostles, after three hours on the coach, they walked to the lookout and saw white, absolute fog. Somewhere below, the sea crashed on something. Now and then a pale shape appeared for a few seconds and vanished like a ghost.

The rest of the coach was disappointed. Mizuki was delighted. To her it was the most beautiful thing she had seen in Australia precisely because you couldn't see it, like mist in an old Japanese ink painting, or one of Fumiko's stories, where the thing in the fog is always more frightening and more wonderful than the thing in daylight. She shot a whole roll of fog. Nana said, "You've come all this way to photograph nothing." "Yes. Very good nothing." When the guide admitted there had never really been twelve Apostles anyway, she liked them even more for lying about it. She has never been back to see them in sunshine, and says she doesn't need to.

### Shadowbringers (July 2019)

Shadowbringers, the third expansion of Final Fantasy XIV, opened for early access on 28 June 2019 and launched properly on 2 July. She had talked about nothing else at the shop for a month, to the point that the Wednesday tradie asked if Shadowbringers was her boyfriend. Mr Kondo approved two days off without being asked, "because you'll be useless anyway".

She played it at the Glebe house on her laptop, on her bed, under the wobbling fan, with Tsukimi Noodles on voice chat and Aya in Osaka on the phone propped against the wall. The story took her to the First, a world drowned in endless light, where the sky never went dark and people longed for night; the heroes had to give it back its darkness. She loved the Crystarium, the city of crystal towers and kind refugees; she loved the strange, sad fairy land of Il Mheg; she loved that her character could at last bring back the night sky to a people who had forgotten stars, "which as a photographer, I take personally". And she loved the villain, Emet-Selch, sardonic, tired and grieving, who turned out to have done terrible things for a reason that was almost understandable.

At the end she cried so hard that Fiona, home from a shift, knocked on the door with a cup of tea and asked if someone had died. "Yes," said Mizuki. "A lot of people. Twelve thousand years ago. It's complicated." Fiona gave her the tea anyway and sat on the end of the bed while she finished, without asking another question, which is Fiona's great gift.

Shadowbringers became, and remains, her favourite story in the series. She still goes quiet at the line "Remember us", and says it taught her that a good villain is someone who was sad first. It also fixed the White Mage's place in her heart: the expansion's healing felt, she says, like playing a piano properly for the first time. Tsukimi Noodles celebrated the end of the story with a party in the FC house; Kazu's son, allowed to stay up, said "omedetou" on voice chat and then ran away again.

### The rest of 2019: rhythm, rugby and Tame Impala

By the middle of 2019 her life had a shape, and she likes shapes. Five shifts a week at the ramen shop. Two raid nights, finished by nine. Saturday mornings at Paddy's Markets or the fish market, buying one good thing the way Fumiko taught her; Saturday afternoons with the camera, the beach, or the K-pop dance class with Nana, where she rose from "terrible" to "enthusiastic". Sunday video calls to Osaka, which she got wrong by an hour twice a year because Australia has daylight saving and Japan doesn't. And a monthly train to Crows Nest for proper okonomiyaki flour, aonori, red pickled ginger and katsuobushi, at prices she calls "a hate crime against cabbage".

In August she went to the cherry blossom festival at the Japanese garden in Auburn and was moved to see families doing proper hanami on picnic mats under pink trees, in a park full of every kind of Sydney. She judged the takoyaki as an Osaka person must (too doughy, not enough octopus, a fair try) and told the stallholder so, kindly, in Kansai-ben. He was from Osaka too. They talked for twenty minutes and he gave her extra.

Her music that year kept its Osaka core and grew an Australian edge. TWICE's "Fancy" and then "Feel Special" in September; Official Hige Dandism's "Pretender", which she sang at hitokara with her eyes closed. On Australia Day in January she had sat in the courtyard with the house listening to triple j's Hottest 100, the countdown of the year's favourite songs that half the country hears over a barbecue, baffled and charmed that a nation could care so much about a radio list; she has listened every year since. And she found Tame Impala, whose "Borderline" became "music for walking home in the heat": woozy, warm, a little sad, George Street at seven on a summer evening. She is slightly embarrassed to like an Australian band so much, and mentions it often.

Rafa had left the building sites early that year for a job at a Brazilian steakhouse in the city, carving picanha at the tables on long skewers, which he said was "the same job as the site, but people clap". In June she saw Godzilla: King of the Monsters with him, who wanted explosions, and came out with a verdict that would one day matter: "Too loud, too dark, too many monsters, and where is the sadness? In Japan, Godzilla is sad." Rafa said she was overthinking a film about a lizard. She said that was exactly the problem.

In October the Rugby World Cup was in Japan, and Fiona, who was Scottish and therefore cared about rugby in ways Mizuki didn't understand, insisted they watch Japan play Scotland together on the evening of 13 October, a match that a typhoon had nearly cancelled the day before. Japan won, 28 to 21, and reached the quarter-finals for the first time. Mizuki, who didn't know a single rule, screamed at every Japanese try. Fiona sat with her head in her hands, then got up, made a pot of tea and poured Mizuki the first cup: "Otsukaresama. Your lads earned it." Mizuki says it was the most gracious thing she has ever seen a sports fan do.

Small bad things happened too. A bad flu in August took a week of shifts; Bia brought soup she couldn't taste, and Mr Kondo sent home, with Lily, a container of his miso broth and a note: "Drink. Not tonkotsu. Miso is medicine." She drank it and, for the only time, admitted miso might have a point. She cracked her phone screen on the shop tiles and used it cracked for a year out of stubbornness. And she was swooped by the same magpie in the same tree, a year to the week since the first time, and screamed exactly as loudly.

### The smoke begins (late 2019)

Her second summer in Australia began strangely. Bushfires had been burning in New South Wales since the spring, far away, in the bush up the coast and in the mountains, and at first they were something on the news. Then, in November, the smoke came to the city.

She remembers the first morning: walking to work and noticing the light was wrong, a dull yellow haze instead of Sydney's hard brightness, and a smell like a distant campfire that didn't go away. Over the next weeks there were orange skies and a small red sun at noon you could look straight at. Ash settled on the windowsills in Glebe and the tables outside the shop. Smoke got into her hair and clothes; fire alarms went off in city buildings; some mornings the harbour disappeared; people wore masks in the street months before anyone had heard of the pandemic. Fiona came home talking quietly about people with bad chests, and the Brazilian steakhouse in the city where Rafa now worked kept its doors shut against the smoke on the worst days.

She photographed the red sun once, from Pyrmont Bridge, and then put the camera away, because it felt wrong to make it beautiful. She doesn't joke about that summer, which ran on into 2020 as the Black Summer, and she doesn't say much. Her one quiet line is that it was the first time Australia felt like home and not a holiday, because she was worried for it the way she had been for Osaka in the typhoon. She stood outside the shop one Saturday with a donation tin, the way she had stood outside Tennoji station in 2011.

There was one lighter memory from those weeks. On 14 December 2019 the new light rail opened along George Street, running right past World Square, the long-awaited line that had torn up the city's main street for years. On her next day off she rode it from Circular Quay all the way to the end of the line and back again, sitting by the window, watching the city go past through the haze. "Finally," she wrote to Kenta, "a Sydney train that comes when it says." Kenta replied, as he always does, without words: a photo of the Midosuji line timetable.

Her second Christmas was at Bronte again, quieter, under a strange pinkish sky, with a strawberry shortcake and the same four people, though none of them knew it would be the last Christmas they'd all have in that house. She had arrived, almost two years before, with one suitcase, a plan to go home in a year and a fox for luck. She ended 2019 with a work visa, a job, a boss who hummed, a younger sister who argued about K-pop, a Free Company of noodle-named strangers, a Glasgow nurse who brought tea at the right moment, a Kobe girl who said yes to everything, a ramen notebook full of sevens, and a sky full of smoke she was worried about. "I came for a working holiday," she says. "Somewhere in there, the holiday finished, and I didn't notice."

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes the hard Sydney light** and still photographs "nothing at all" in it (the fence and gum tree from the bus); **Melbourne light for faces** ("a lamp, not a spotlight").
- **Hates bare feet in shops**: it breaks the shoes-off rule she grew up with, and named Australia "barefoot country".
- **Loves lorikeets and, secretly, ibis**; hates dawn cockatoos (her first morning); fears spring magpies, umbrella open, after two Septembers of swooping.
- **Small flat white only** ("coffee baths" otherwise); Melbourne coffee better on average, Sydney's better at its best.
- **Likes "no worries", "thanks, driver" and first names** (a tight shoe coming off); **dislikes** feet on train seats, speakerphone calls and hot meals on trains.
- **Hates Sydney train frequency and trackwork** (Osaka ruined her); loves ferries since her first Manly crossing, and the George Street light rail since its first week.
- **Misses konbini**: egg sandwiches, hot corn soup, shops that never close.
- **Sunscreen on her feet, always**, since the Bondi burn. **Loves park barbecues and Bronte's ocean pool**; swims between the flags.
- **Wallet in a zipped front pocket** since Central Station, and a new pride in a Japan where lost wallets come back.
- **Christmas in summer, yes; New Year in summer, no.** Never again at Circular Quay; soba on the couch instead. Strawberry shortcake every Christmas; raisins in salad, never.
- **Vegemite is "Australian glue"**, the local natto, eaten thinly; Tim Tams dangerously; tomato sauce only on meat pies.
- **Hates cold, uninsulated houses** (the Glebe terrace in July).
- **Respects huntsmen** as house spirits, from a distance.
