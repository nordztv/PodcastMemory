## Chapter 3: Pocket monsters and pretty soldiers (1996 to mid-1999)

These are the years when Mizuki stopped being the little girl on the stool by the till and became a schoolgirl with opinions: about Pokémon, about Final Fantasy, about which Sailor Senshi was best (Mercury, obviously), about school liver stew and about the correct way to dance to SPEED. She went from six to nine. She got a Game Boy, a red randoseru, a Tamagotchi that died, a first trip to a theme park, a first trip to the sea and, in the last summer of the chapter, a broken arm. Almost everything she still loves about games, cartoons and summer starts somewhere in these three and a bit years, and so does one of her firmest beliefs: that the best things in life usually arrive with something slightly sad tucked inside them.

### Spring 1996: a Game Boy next to the shortcake

Pocket Monsters Red and Green came out in Japan on 27 February 1996. Mizuki had first heard about it at the counter, from a salaryman regular who had a nephew, and then from a boy at kindergarten whose big brother had a magazine. By March she had a full sales pitch, delivered separately to each adult in her life.

To her mother she argued education: "You have to read everything, all the words, it's like a book." Yumiko, wiping down a table, said it was too expensive and that books were cheaper and also books. To her father she argued nothing at all; she simply stood next to the hotplate after closing while he scraped it down and said "Pocket Monsters" once, quietly, every few minutes, like a temple bell. Hiroshi said nothing back, which in Hiroshi's language meant he was thinking about it. To Fumiko she argued fairness: Daisuke had a Super Famicom, other children had Game Boys, and she, Mizuki, had a stool. Fumiko said, "Ara, poor thing," in a tone that suggested she did not think Mizuki was a poor thing at all.

On Wednesday 10 April 1996, her sixth birthday, the shop was closed (Wednesday was always the day off) and the family had dinner upstairs at the low table: chirashi sushi that Fumiko made with shredded egg on top like yellow confetti, and a small strawberry shortcake from the cake shop by the station with six candles. Next to her slice was a flat parcel wrapped in the cake shop's paper because nobody had bought proper wrapping. Inside was a grey Game Boy and a copy of Pocket Monsters Green.

Hiroshi claimed afterwards that he'd picked up whichever box was nearest the till at the shop in Abeno, and that he hadn't known there were two versions. Nobody believed him. Yumiko later admitted that he'd asked a regular's teenage son which one a girl would like, and the boy had said Green because it had the cuter monsters, which was not true, but it was a good answer. Mizuki decided it was fate. She was not wrong, as it turned out, because Green had Rokon.

She chose Bulbasaur (Fushigidane) as her starter because it was green like the cartridge, and named her rival Kenta, because the rival was smug and her real brother, then two, had just eaten her pudding. She gave her own character the name MIZU, the four-letter limit being, she felt, a personal insult. That first evening she got as far as Viridian Forest and then fell asleep with the Game Boy on her chest; Yumiko took it off her, turned it off without saving, and the next morning Mizuki discovered she was back in her bedroom in Pallet Town with no Pokémon at all. It was the first great tragedy of her gaming life. She has saved compulsively ever since. In FFXIV she still sometimes types a little "/save" joke into chat out of muscle memory from 1996, and nobody in her Free Company understands it.

The real home of the Game Boy was under the shop counter. Mizuki sat on an upturned beer crate in the narrow space by her mother's legs, the screen tilted to catch the light from the doorway, the hotplate hissing above her and sauce smell drifting down. The Game Boy's screen had no light of its own, so playing in the evening meant constant small negotiations with the shop's fluorescent tubes. Regulars leaned over the counter to ask how many badges she had. Yumiko answered for her: "Four. She's stuck on the psychic one. Don't help her, she has to learn." (She was stuck at Sabrina's gym in Yamabuki City for about a month. She solved it in the end by trading a Kadabra with a boy at kindergarten, which made it an Alakazam, which Yumiko considered cheating and Mizuki considered business.)

The batteries were AAs, four of them, and they became a currency in the household. Hiroshi kept spare packs in the shop's drawer for the radio. Mizuki learned to ask for them politely, then to ask for them on days when her father was in a good mood (a Tigers win), then simply to take them, which ended with a family rule that every battery had to be "signed out" on the back of an order pad. The pad is long gone. The phrase "sign out a battery" survives in the family as a joke about Mizuki taking things.

### Rokon, and the foxes of Inari

Rokon, the little six-tailed fire fox called Vulpix in English, appeared only in Green. Mizuki caught her first one in the grass on the route outside Tamamushi City (Celadon to Westerners) after about forty minutes of trying, with three Poké Balls left. She named it nothing, because she liked "Rokon" too much to change it, and kept it in her team long after it was any use in a fight, carrying it all the way to the Elite Four at level thirty-something, where it was promptly flattened. She did not mind. "It was there for moral support."

When she showed Fumiko, her grandmother put on her reading glasses, looked at the tiny grey sprite for a long time and then said that foxes were the messengers of Inari, the god of rice and good harvests, and that a fox with many tails was very old and very wise. Every hundred years, a fox grows a new tail. At nine tails, it is almost a god. "So my Rokon is a baby god," Mizuki said. "A baby fox," said Fumiko. "Gods come later."

From then on every Inari shrine they passed, with its red torii gates and its pairs of stone foxes wearing little red bibs, got a bow from Mizuki "for Rokon's relatives". There was a small one tucked between houses on the way to Fumiko's, barely bigger than a phone box, and she bowed to it for years. Fumiko told her the foxes liked fried tofu (aburaage), which is why kitsune udon has a sheet of sweet fried tofu on top, and from then on kitsune udon was Rokon's dish, ordered with great ceremony at any noodle stand.

The connection between Fumiko's yokai foxes and a Game Boy monster is the whole reason Vulpix is still Mizuki's favourite Pokémon, thirty years later. It wasn't the strongest or the rarest. It was the one that her grandmother had already told her about before it existed. There is a Vulpix keyring on her keys in Sydney, and a plush on her shelf in Pyrmont, slightly bald on one ear.

### The last year of kindergarten, and the end of Sailor Moon

From April 1996 Mizuki was in her final year of kindergarten, the oldest class, the "big ones" who got to feed the rabbits and lead the songs. She took both duties very seriously. She was also, by her teacher's account, "a natural organiser of other children", which meant she ran the sandpit like a construction company and assigned roles in every game of make-believe. The make-believe was almost always Sailor Moon.

Sailor Moon had come into her life on Saturday evenings when she was four or five and never really left. By the time she was six, the series had reached its last season, Sailor Stars, and she owned (in descending order of importance): a Sailor Mercury transformation pen from Fumiko, an Ami Mizuno pencil case, a Sailor Moon lunch box, two Sailor Moon T-shirts (one too small, still worn), a pair of pink plastic Sailor Moon sandals for summer and a sticker album with the stickers arranged not by character but by how good their hair looked.

Ami was the one. Sailor Mercury: the clever, shy girl with short blue hair and a little computer, who read on the walk to school and blushed when anyone praised her. Mizuki was a very good reader for her age and went pink whenever customers called her "kashikoi" (clever) in front of everyone, so she felt Ami had been drawn specifically for her. It helped that Ami's surname began with "Mizu", like hers. "Water girls," she explained to Kenta. "We are calm and intelligent. You are a fire type. You have no self-control." Kenta, aged two and a half, agreed by trying to eat the pencil case.

In kindergarten games she insisted on being Mercury, which caused problems only when another girl also wanted to be Mercury. This happened once, in the autumn of 1996, and was resolved, after tears on both sides, by the teacher declaring that there could be two Mercurys "because there's a lot of water in the world". Mizuki accepted this ruling with dignity and privately considered herself the original.

She practised "Mercury Power, Make Up!" in the long mirror on the shop's back wall after closing, twirling with the pen raised, while Hiroshi, scraping down the hotplate, provided the sound effects without looking up: a flat, perfectly timed "shararara" at the right moment. It was one of the few times her father performed, and he only did it facing the other way. But the line she actually used in daily life was Usagi's: "In the name of the moon, I'll punish you!" aimed at Kenta, with the full pose, two fingers in a V across her face, every time he stole a snack. It is still her favourite line in the world, and the one she uses most on air.

For one month in 1996 she demanded that her mother do her hair in Usagi's odango, two buns on top with long tails hanging down. Yumiko did it before kindergarten every morning, at speed, with a mouth full of hair pins, and by lunchtime the buns had usually slid to the sides of her head, so the effect was less Sailor Moon and more "a girl who's been in a strong wind". She gave it up when a regular said she looked like a pair of steamed buns. She did not speak to that regular for a week, a punishment he did not notice.

Sailor Stars ended on 8 February 1997, two months before she started elementary school. She watched the last episode upstairs, sitting very close to the screen, and at the end of it she went downstairs, stood in the shop doorway in her socks and announced to the room that it was over. A table of office workers applauded her, not entirely sure why. She considered it a serious scheduling failure on the part of the universe that Sailor Moon should end at the exact moment she was about to need it most. She kept the Mercury pen in her desk drawer for years after the batteries died, and she still owns it; it lives in a box in her parents' cupboard with her randoseru and her first FFX strategy guide.

What she took from Sailor Moon, beyond a catchphrase, was an idea about girls: that you could be cute and clever and brave at once, and cry and still win, and that your friends were the actual superpower. It also gave her a fondness for transformation sequences that she has never grown out of. She will defend, to anyone, that a good outfit change is a plot point.

### Tamagotchi, and other things that died in 1997

Bandai's Tamagotchi went on sale in Japan in November 1996 and by early 1997 was the most wanted object in the country, sold out everywhere, with queues outside toy shops before they opened and stories on the news about adults fighting over them. Mizuki wanted one with the whole of her being. Her campaign this time was long and relentless, and it was Chiyo, her father's mother, who finally ended it. Chiyo did not believe in queues, but she knew a woman who ran a toy shop near Tennoji, and in February 1997 a white Tamagotchi in its egg-shaped case appeared in Mizuki's hand with the words, "That was not cheap. Look after it."

She looked after it beautifully for twelve days. She fed it, played the little guessing game with it, cleaned up its pixelated poo with the seriousness of a nurse and set an alarm in her head for every beep. Then came the problem: she was about to start school, and school banned them. In her first week of first grade, in April, she left it with Fumiko during the day with long, careful instructions written on the back of a calendar page. Fumiko fed it twice, put it in a drawer so the beeping wouldn't disturb her afternoon nap, and forgot it. It died on a Thursday.

Mizuki came home from school, opened the drawer, saw the little ghost on the screen and cried so hard that Fumiko cried too. Then Fumiko lit a stick of incense at the family altar, put the Tamagotchi in front of Shigeru's photograph for a minute, and said a short, completely serious prayer for it. That stopped Mizuki crying, mostly because it was so strange. She restarted it, of course, but she never felt the same about the second one, and it was lost in the cushions by summer.

It's one of her small, well-used disappointments and she plays it for laughs now: "My first pet died because my grandmother was busy. It was 1997. We all lost someone." But it's also the beginning of something real. She doesn't like looking after things she can't actually save. She has never owned a plant that needs daily attention (the balcony herbs in Pyrmont are allowed because they forgive her), and she has always said she'll get a cat "when I'm a person with a schedule", which so far she isn't.

### Spring holidays 1997: Final Fantasy VII, from behind Daisuke

Final Fantasy VII came out on the PlayStation in Japan on 31 January 1997. Her second cousin Daisuke, whom everyone simply called her cousin, was eleven and back in Kobe in a repaired flat, and he had a PlayStation. Over the spring holidays at the end of March, the week before she started school, he came to stay at Fumiko's for a few days with the console in his backpack and the three discs in a padded envelope, and he played for most of every day.

Daisuke was twelve by then in manner if not in years: taller, quieter, a little embarrassed to be visiting his younger relatives, but not too embarrassed to plug his PlayStation into Fumiko's old television and bring the whole Midgar slums into her tatami room. Mizuki, nearly seven, sat on the floor behind him for every minute of it. She was not allowed to touch the controller. Daisuke made this clear on the first morning with the air of a man protecting a national treasure. So she became the narrator instead, reading the text boxes aloud, faster than he could, in a variety of voices, and offering advice nobody asked for. ("Go that way." "That's a wall." "Go that way anyway.") It was during these days that Daisuke started calling her "the audience". He still does, at forty-one, at every family New Year.

She didn't understand most of the story. She understood the important parts: that Cloud was cool but confused, that Barret was loud like her uncle who lived in Sakai, that Tifa was kind, that the flower girl in the church was the nicest person in the game, and that the music was better than any music she had ever heard on a television. She liked the chocobos enormously and demanded to see them as often as possible. She liked the pink-and-purple ghost hotel at the Gold Saucer. She did not like the bits where people got stabbed, and covered her eyes, then spread her fingers, then covered them again. This is the origin of her famous "through my fingers" method for watching anything violent.

When Aerith died at the end of the first disc, on the second-to-last day of the holiday, Mizuki cried so hard that Fumiko came in from the kitchen with her hands still wet. Daisuke said, "It's only a game," and then went very quiet himself and didn't play anything for an hour. Mizuki has never forgiven Sephiroth, and has never fully forgiven Daisuke for "only a game". It's an argument they still have, affectionately, every few years. He has conceded nothing; she has conceded nothing; Kenta, who wasn't there, has taken Daisuke's side out of principle.

Daisuke had to go back to Kobe before he finished, and took the PlayStation with him. For a few months Mizuki genuinely didn't know how the story ended. He came back in the summer to finish it, on a sticky afternoon during Obon week, with Fumiko's electric fan turning from side to side, and Mizuki watched the ending with her knees pulled up to her chin. She remembers the lifestream rising and the last shot of Midgar overgrown, and that Fumiko, passing through, said, "Ah, the city became a forest," as if that was a perfectly normal thing to happen to a city.

That spring is when Mizuki decided Final Fantasy was "the only game series that matters". At seven it was a feeling, not an argument. She has spent nearly thirty years collecting the evidence. She did not own a Final Fantasy game until 2002, which she describes as "five years of suffering", and which is why every Final Fantasy story she tells about her childhood begins with the back of a boy's head.

### April 1997: a red randoseru

The Japanese school year begins in April, so Mizuki, born on 10 April, entered first grade at her local public elementary school in Abeno in April 1997, a few days before her seventh birthday. Being born in early April made her one of the very oldest in her year, a fact she still raises whenever anyone calls her childish ("I was the oldest in my class. I have always been the senior person in the room.").

Her randoseru, the stiff leather backpack every Japanese elementary schoolchild carries for six years, was red, as almost every girl's was then (boys had black; anything else was a statement). Fumiko had saved for it for a year, and it had come in a big box the previous autumn, which was the custom, so the child could spend months admiring it. Mizuki had worn it around the flat upstairs empty, then full of stuffed toys, then with Kenta inside it (briefly, before Yumiko intervened). On the first morning it was so big against her small back that from behind she looked like a randoseru with legs. Like every first grader she had a yellow safety cover over the flap, so drivers would notice the new children, and a yellow cap for walking to school.

The entrance ceremony was in the school gym, with red-and-white bunting on the walls, the parents in dark suits and best clothes and the new first graders in their smartest outfits. Mizuki wore a navy pinafore dress with a white collar and new white tights that itched like a punishment. Hiroshi closed the shop at lunch for the first time in anyone's memory to be there. For the traditional photo at the school gate under the cherry blossom, she made him move her twice so the sun wasn't in her eyes, explaining that the light was wrong. He moved her, said nothing, and took it anyway. The photo is still on the wall upstairs. She is squinting.

Her first-grade teacher was Ms Ueno, a young woman in her second year of teaching with a ponytail, a whistle she never used and an extraordinary ability to make thirty-two seven-year-olds go quiet by simply standing still. Mizuki adored her. In the second week of term she raised her hand to ask a question and accidentally called Ms Ueno "Okaasan" (Mum), which is one of the most humiliating things a Japanese child can do, and which she did loudly. The class laughed for what felt like an hour. Ms Ueno said, "Then do your homework like a good daughter," which made them laugh more but somehow made it all right. Mizuki went pink to the ears and then laughed too, which she counts as her first lesson in surviving embarrassment: laugh before they finish laughing.

The walk to school was about ten minutes, in a group with the other children from the surrounding streets (the "tokohan", the walking group), led by a sixth-grade boy with a flag who took the job like a general. The route went past the Inari shrine, a tofu shop, a tiny bicycle repair place with an old man permanently asleep in it, and a cigarette machine she was convinced was haunted because it hummed. Volunteers in yellow armbands stood at the crossings. Every morning at the corner where the group gathered, Yumiko, in her apron, would come out of the shop and say "itterasshai" (go and come back), and every afternoon when Mizuki came back in through the noren she said, instead of the usual "okaeri", "You're late." She said it even when Mizuki was early. It was how her mother said welcome home, and still is.

School in the late 1990s ran on Saturdays too, but only some of them: from 1995 the second and fourth Saturdays of each month were days off, and the others were half-days of lessons that finished before lunch. Mizuki liked the half-days best of all. She came home at noon, ate noodles upstairs in front of Yoshimoto Shinkigeki, the slapstick comedy from Namba, and then went to Fumiko's.

Lessons were in Japanese, maths, life studies (a subject that covered everything from growing plants to visiting the fire station), music, art and PE. She was quickly good at Japanese, average at maths, good at art, enthusiastic about music and very bad at PE. She learned her first eighty kanji in first grade, practising them in big squared notebooks, and found that she loved the satisfying order of the strokes. Her handwriting was neat, which surprised everyone who knew her.

### Kyushoku, cleaning and the shape of the day

School gave her habits she has kept all her life.

The first was cleaning. Japanese schools don't have cleaners for the classrooms; the children do it. Every afternoon (soji time) her group pushed the desks to the back of the room, swept, then raced each other across the wooden floor on hands and knees with damp cloths, bottoms in the air, from one wall to the other. Mizuki loved it with a passion that puzzled her mother, who could not get her to tidy her own room. "It's different," Mizuki said. "At school there are teams." She was especially good at the window ledges. To this day she can't leave a workplace without wiping something, and at the ramen shop in Sydney she's the one who finds the sticky patch under the condiment tray: "In Japan, seven-year-olds clean better than this."

The second was kyushoku, school lunch. Children on duty that week wore white smocks and white caps (and gauze masks) and carried the big metal pots and trays from the school kitchen to the classroom, ladling out rice, soup, a main dish and a small bottle of milk to each desk while everyone else waited, before the whole class put their hands together and said "itadakimasu" in one voice. Mizuki loved being on serving duty and was strict about portions. She once told a boy that his helping of curry was "the right amount for a person, not for a bear", which he did not appreciate.

Her favourites were curry rice (Wednesdays, if they were lucky), and age-pan, a soft bread roll deep-fried and rolled in sugar and kinako (roasted soybean flour) that her whole generation goes misty-eyed about. Also good: kare-udon, the occasional fruit jelly, and fried chicken that was always slightly too small. Her nemesis was a vegetable stew that came with cubes of liver, perhaps once a month. In 1990s Japanese schools you had to finish your plate, so on liver days she was always the last at the table, chewing very slowly with her eyes shut while the cleaning group swept around her chair and Ms Ueno sat opposite with infinite patience. She has not eaten liver voluntarily since: "No. I paid for that in 1998."

The little glass bottles of school milk, 200 millilitres each, came with a cardboard cap you pried off with a pin, and she drank hers holding her breath every day for six years. It's why she still won't drink plain milk, though milky drinks are "completely different, Scott, don't be silly". The milk caps themselves, though, were treasure: kids collected and flipped them in a game called "menko" with bottle caps, and Mizuki had a tin of forty.

The third habit was indoor shoes. Everyone changed at the shoe lockers into white canvas slip-ons (uwabaki) with their name written on the toe, and every Friday the uwabaki went home in a drawstring bag to be scrubbed over the weekend. Hers came back clean only because Fumiko scrubbed them on her back step with an old toothbrush, singing. Mizuki still takes her shoes off the moment she's through a door, Sydney or not, and feels a small pang of wrongness in any house where people don't.

There was also the morning "chorei" assembly, the class duty board with the names on little magnetic tags, the class pet (a goldfish tank in first grade, a pair of stag beetles in second), and the "renrakucho", the daily notebook that went between teacher and parents. Ms Ueno wrote in hers that Mizuki was "cheerful, kind to younger children and tells very long stories at the wrong moment". Yumiko wrote back: "We know. We are working on the endings."

### Tuesdays at half past six

The Pokémon anime began on 1 April 1997, the same week she started school, on TV Tokyo's network (TV Osaka in Kansai) on Tuesday evenings at half past six. That was precisely the start of the shop's dinner rush, so she watched it on the little television high in the corner above the two tables, sitting at the end of the counter with her homework open and not being done. On Tuesdays at 6.29, even when the Tigers were on, Hiroshi changed the channel without being asked and without comment, and changed it back at seven. It's one of her favourite examples of how her father loved her: silently, on schedule.

One old regular, a retired postman who came in for a beer and a modan-yaki (okonomiyaki with yakisoba noodles folded in) every Tuesday without fail, would ask her, very seriously, to explain who everyone was. She did, in detail. He asked the same questions every week and she answered them every week with enormous patience, which she now suspects was the whole point. "Who's the boy with the hat?" "Satoshi. Ash. He's not very good yet." "And the yellow one?" "Pikachu. It doesn't like the ball." "Very sensible." He never learned anything, and she understood years later that he hadn't been trying to.

She liked Misty (Kasumi) best of the humans, partly because she was a water trainer like a water girl should be, and partly because she shouted at Ash, which Mizuki recognised as a tsukkomi. She liked Team Rocket's motto better than anything else in the show, learned it by heart in a week and performed both parts with Kenta, who was meant to be Meowth but kept forgetting his line. She did not like Ash's Charmander being left in the rain, and she did not watch that episode again for twenty years.

That autumn the playground ran on Pokémon. The trading card game had come out in October 1996 and by 1997 the cards were everywhere: kids traded them in the corner of the yard by the climbing frame, a black market with its own exchange rates. Mizuki was a cautious trader with one great regret. In the winter of 1997 she swapped a shiny Lizardon (Charizard) card to a boy from the class next door for three cards she can't now remember and a packet of gummy sweets. She has thought about that card more often than she will admit. "I sold my birthright for gummies. It was a bad market."

### November 1997: seven, in red

In November she was seven, which meant a second Shichi-go-san at Sumiyoshi Taisha, the big one for girls. This time the kimono was a proper red one, her first with a stiff obi sash tied by an expert (Fumiko, who had a friend in the kimono trade, and opinions), and she was given another long paper bag of chitose-ame, the red-and-white thousand-year candy, with cranes and turtles printed on the bag and the bag almost as long as her arm. She remembered the first Shichi-go-san, when Fumiko had stopped her eating a sweet in full kimono, and she was older and cleverer now. While the adults were lining up for the photo under the gingko trees, she quietly ate the candy. All of it. Hiroshi, behind the FM2, looked up from the viewfinder at the empty bag, and then at her red, sticky, entirely innocent face, and took the picture anyway. It is the only Shichi-go-san photo in the family album in which the girl is holding a flat bag and has pink lips. Yumiko says she looks "like a fox who has had a very good day". Mizuki considers this the highest compliment ever paid to her.

Kenta, who was four and not due for his own until the next year, was allowed to clap at the hall, and did it before anyone else, and then again.

### 16 December 1997: the Porygon night

On Tuesday 16 December 1997, she was in her seat at the end of the counter for the episode about Porygon, the computer Pokémon. It was a cold night, the shop full of steam and workmen in jackets, and the Christmas decorations Yumiko put up every year (a small plastic tree by the till, tinsel on the menu board) were already blinking. Near the end of the episode came a burst of rapid red and blue flashing on the screen. Yumiko, seeing the light strobing across the faces at the tables from the far side of the shop, said "Akan," reached up on her toes and switched the television off at the set.

Mizuki protested loudly. She was perfectly fine. She had been about to find out what happened. Several customers took her side. Yumiko did not turn it back on, and said only that it was "too flashy" and was hurting her eyes from across the room.

Then the evening news came on (Yumiko switched it back on for the news) and reported that children all over Japan had felt unwell during a cartoon, that hundreds had been taken to hospital with dizziness and fits, and that it was the same programme. By the next morning it was the biggest story in the country. At school the following day every classroom was talking about it; a few children had felt sick, nobody in Mizuki's class had been seriously ill, and Ms Ueno spent part of the first lesson explaining what had happened and telling them to sit far from the screen in a bright room, which became a slogan. The show went off the air for four months, until April 1998, while the whole country argued about it.

Mizuki was mainly furious about missing the ending, and then about the four-month gap, which she took personally, like Sailor Stars all over again. But the family story changed over the years. Yumiko tells it now as the night she saved her daughter. Hiroshi tells it, in one sentence, as the night his wife unplugged a television "like a ninja". Kenta, who was four and asleep upstairs, tells it as if he was there. Mizuki tells it as the night she was robbed of an ending, and then adds, more quietly, that her mother watched the room, not the screen, and that this is what mothers who run shops do. She still turns away from strobe lights at concerts, and a club in Sydney with a heavy light show is, for her, "a Porygon situation".

### Sports day, the zoo and the first summer homework

The autumn undokai, sports day, was one of the biggest family events of the year, a Sunday in early October. Families staked out the edges of the playground with blue plastic sheets from early morning, grandparents brought folding stools, and fathers with video cameras the size of loaves stood in a row along the rope. The school was split into red and white teams in reversible caps. There were relays, tug of war, a giant ball pushed by teams, a beanbag-throwing contest into baskets on tall poles, and a group dance for each year.

Mizuki was clever, shy in front of crowds and slow. In first grade she came last in the fifty-metre run by a distance her father described, lovingly, as "very relaxed". She had been looking at the crowd for her family. She found them, waved, and only then remembered she was meant to be running. Hiroshi closed the shop for lunch again and stood at the rope with his Nikon, and the photo he took is of Mizuki, last, waving, delighted. It's one of her favourite pictures of herself, because it's so honest.

What she loved was the group dance (first grade did a dance with pom-poms to a children's song, which she practised every night in the shop mirror) and, above all, the lunch. Fumiko made a three-tier lacquered bento: rolled omelette, karaage chicken, octopus-shaped little sausages, inari sushi in their sweet tofu pockets (for Rokon), onigiri with salmon and with kombu, and peeled mikan and apple cut into rabbits. The family ate it on the blue sheet with the other families, trading pieces back and forth with neighbours. Mizuki decided a sports day was ninety per cent sitting and eating, which suited her, and she is still the person who brings too much food to any picnic.

The first school excursion was to Tennoji Zoo, ten minutes from home, where she had been dozens of times with Fumiko. She spent the day showing her classmates the way to everything before the teacher could explain, which Ms Ueno described in the renrakucho as "very helpful, for a while". The elephant, she informed everyone, was called Haruko and was very old. (She thought this was secret knowledge; it was on a sign.)

The first summer holidays, from about 20 July to the end of August 1997, came with a mountain of homework: drill books, a picture diary to be filled in every day, a "free research" project, a reading list and, for first graders, a pot of morning glories (asagao) brought home from school, to be watered and drawn every day. Hers survived being forgotten for four days during Obon; she did not tell Ms Ueno. Every morning at 6.30 she went to radio exercises (rajio taiso) in the park in her pyjamas-but-not-quite, for the stamp card, mainly because Fumiko bought her a cold barley tea from the vending machine afterwards. Like every Japanese child in history she wrote most of her picture diary on 30 and 31 August, inventing the weather, which in 1997 was apparently "sunny" every day. Her free research project, the first of many about food, was "Which okonomiyaki sauce is best?", which she tested on regulars with toothpicks. Her father's won, eight votes to two. The two dissenters were both Kenta.

From those summers she kept a love of morning glories ("the most hopeful flower, because it opens fresh every morning") and a deep distrust of far-off deadlines: "A deadline in six weeks is a lie."

### Clothes: hand-me-downs, a Mezzo Piano dress and leopard print

Clothes in these years were mostly practical, because a child in a shop family gets sauce on everything. Mizuki wore hand-me-downs from an older girl two doors down: soft, faded T-shirts, corduroy skirts, a quilted winter coat in a shade of pink that she described even then as "tired". On school days it was a T-shirt, a skirt or shorts, knee socks, the yellow cap and the randoseru; in summer, sandals and a towel round her neck for the sweat; in winter, a padded jacket and a knitted scarf from Fumiko that was too long and nearly got caught in her bicycle wheel more than once (a warning sign, in retrospect).

Then there was the Sailor Moon wardrobe, which she wore until it disintegrated or until Yumiko secretly threw it out, and the one special dress. For her seventh birthday and her first school photos, Chiyo bought her a dress from Mezzo Piano, the very sweet, very expensive girls' brand of the 1990s, all frills and ribbon. It was pale pink with a white collar and a print of tiny red strawberries all over, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever owned. It came out for special days only: New Year visits, a wedding in Sakai, the photo studio, and in 1999, Takarazuka. Chiyo mentioned the price every time she saw her in it. "Looks good. Seven thousand yen." Mizuki thought this was part of the compliment.

Her real fashion education, though, came from the counter. The leopard-print auntie, the true Osaka obachan with purple-tinted hair and a handbag full of sweets, was still a regular, and Mizuki, now old enough to really look, studied her: leopard print with gold, a tiger appliqué jumper in winter, three patterns at once, the confidence of a woman who has decided the world can cope. Mizuki asked her, at eight, how she chose her clothes. The auntie said, "I put on what makes me happy, then I put on one more thing." It's still the best fashion advice Mizuki has ever heard, though her own rule became the opposite in practice ("No logo bigger than my hand"). She took the spirit, not the leopard.

### Music: SPEED in the shop mirror

The shop radio played enka for the older customers and whatever was on FM for everyone else, and Hiroshi had a small stack of Southern All Stars tapes he played on Wednesday mornings when he cleaned the vents, singing along in a low mumble that sounded like a man complaining. Mizuki knew every word of "Itoshi no Ellie" by eight without ever being taught it. Yumiko, meanwhile, sang everything, loudly, especially at the regular karaoke nights at a snack bar near the station where she was famous; she sang Takarazuka songs in the bath and enka while counting the till.

But Mizuki's first music of her own was SPEED. The four girls from Okinawa debuted in August 1996 with "Body & Soul", and they were barely older than teenagers, two of them only twelve or so. To a six-year-old they were proof that girls could be on television being amazing. "Go! Go! Heaven" came in the summer of 1997, and then, in October 1997, "White Love", the winter ballad that everyone in Japan seemed to sing that Christmas. Mizuki and a girl from her class named Mio, who lived on the next street and was her main playground ally in first and second grade, learned the dances off the TV music shows, Hey! Hey! Hey! Music Champ and Music Station, and performed them in the shop mirror after closing, with Kenta made to stand at the back as "the fourth member" (he was barely four and refused to dance; he was allowed to clap).

Mio was the better dancer; Mizuki was the better singer and the one who remembered all the words. They argued over who got to be Hiroko, the main singer, and settled it by taking turns by the day of the week. It was Mizuki's first experience of a dance group, and she later says it's the direct line to her K-pop dance classes with Nana in Sydney: "Same mirror energy. Different decade. Worse knees."

She also had a soft spot for "my graduation", SPEED's spring 1998 song about saying goodbye, which she found almost unbearably sad at seven, though she had never graduated from anything except kindergarten. It was the first song she remembers liking because it made her feel sad. That was a new and interesting thing to like, and it would become a pattern.

Other music filtered in: Anpanman songs for Kenta, the Sailor Moon theme "Moonlight Densetsu" (still the song that makes her stand up a little straighter), the Pokémon anime's opening "Mezase Pokémon Master", which she could sing faster than the TV, and the Ghibli songs Fumiko liked, especially "Kimi wo Nosete" from Laputa, which Fumiko hummed while folding origami.

By the spring of 1999 the mirror routines were starting to fade. Mio had moved to a different class, SPEED were older and singing about things Mizuki didn't yet care about, and in March Yumiko came home with a CD a customer had recommended, put it in the stereo upstairs and never really took it out again. That CD was *First Love*, and it belongs to the summer of the cast. But SPEED came first, and Mizuki is loyal to firsts. If "White Love" comes on in a konbini in December, wherever she is, she still does the little hand movement from the chorus, very small, by her hip, so nobody will notice.

### Jiichan's stool, Chiyo's abacus

Her father's parents were part of the furniture of the shop in these years, in the most literal way. Isamu had opened the place in 1964, worked beside Hiroshi from the wedding on, and handed him the hotplate fully around 1994. Now he came in every morning at ten (Wednesdays excepted), and often again in the afternoon, to "supervise" from seat one, the stool at the end of the counter, with a cup of tea and the sports paper, calling every child who came through the noren "shachō", company president, and asking for their order as if they were about to sign a contract. He was loud where Hiroshi was quiet, quick to laugh, quick to lose his temper for exactly one minute and then sorry. Mizuki, at seven and eight, sat on his knee after school while he read her the Tigers results like bedtime stories, with voices for the pitchers.

They were not good stories. The Hanshin Tigers finished last in the Central League in 1998, and then kept finishing last for years. Isamu read the scores anyway, with great drama and a gold tooth, and ended each one the same way: "The Tigers lose so we can enjoy the beer." Hiroshi, at the hotplate, said "mada mada" (not yet, not yet) under his breath, which he had inherited from his father along with the shop. Mizuki learned "Rokko Oroshi", the Tigers' song, before her times tables, and learned the deeper Osaka lesson with it: you support the team that loses, because anyone can support the Giants. She still finds people who only back winners slightly suspicious.

Isamu also took her, now and then, to Shinsekai for kushikatsu, and taught her the rule of the shared sauce tray with a finger raised: "Once. If you dip twice, the police come." She believed this until she was about eleven.

Chiyo was the other end of the family's temperament: small, upright and precise, with a purse she never let go of. She had kept the shop's books in pencil for thirty years, totting them up on an abacus, and had never forgiven the calculator for replacing it. She had handed the daily figures to Yumiko a few years before, but every Sunday afternoon she still came in to check the week's takings. In 1997, when Mizuki started school, Chiyo decided the girl should learn the soroban properly, and on those Sunday afternoons the two of them sat at the end of the counter while the beads clacked through the takings. Mizuki was not good at it; she talked through the sums. But she loved the sound, like rain on a tin roof, and she learned the most important thing Chiyo had to teach: "Yen is yen. Count it twice." Chiyo also asked the price of every present Mizuki ever received, including from Fumiko, which created a certain atmosphere between the two grandmothers that everyone else in the family enjoyed enormously.

### Saturdays with Fumiko: Kuromon and the 21st

Fumiko's Kuromon Saturdays went on through elementary school, fitted round the half-days: grilled scallop standing up in the arcade, fish judged by their eyes, and Fumiko's theory that Kyoto people would rather be hungry and look elegant. "That's why Kyoto food is so small." Mizuki repeated this at school as a scientific fact.

On the 21st of each month there was the flea market at Shitennoji, the great old temple a few streets from the shop, with its long walls and its stone torii and its stalls of old kimono, chipped bowls, tools and tin toys. Mizuki was given one hundred yen to spend, which bought almost nothing, and she spent most of each visit deciding. In the autumn of 1998, after three months of looking, she bought a small ceramic fox with one ear chipped, for Rokon. The stallholder, an old man in a cardigan, knocked twenty yen off because of the ear. "So it's a bargain fox," Fumiko said. It still sits on her parents' shelf upstairs. Her love of markets, and of second-hand things that have already had a life, starts on those mornings.

### Spring 1998: Nara Dreamland

Her first theme park was Nara Dreamland, on a Wednesday in the spring holidays of 1998, a few weeks before she turned eight. Nara Dreamland had opened in 1961, built by a Japanese businessman who had visited Disneyland in California and come home wanting one, and it showed: there was a castle copied from Sleeping Beauty's, pink and blue and pointed, at the end of a main street of little shops. By 1998 the paint was tired and the crowds had thinned, which Mizuki didn't notice at all.

The family went on the Kintetsu train to Nara and then by bus, with Yumiko's thermos of barley tea, a bag of onigiri ("park food is a robbery") and Kenta, four, who fell asleep on the bus and woke up furious that he'd missed the arrival. Hiroshi carried the Nikon and took very few pictures, because film was money, and the best of them is of Mizuki standing in front of the castle with her arms out as if she owned it.

She went on the carousel twice and the teacups once (Kenta was sick, gently, on Hiroshi's shoe; Hiroshi wiped it with a towel he had brought for exactly this reason). She did not go on the big roller coaster. She stood at the bottom and watched it for a long time, listening to the screams, and then announced that she was "saving it for when I'm taller", which was the start of a long and honourable career of saving roller coasters for later. In the afternoon she bought a castle keyring with her own coins, ate soft-serve vanilla that dripped on her one good cardigan, and insisted on waving goodbye to the castle from the bus window until it was out of sight.

She loved Nara Dreamland the way you love something when you have nothing to compare it with. She went back once more, with Daisuke's family, and never again, and when she read in 2006 that it had closed, she felt as if a friend's house had burned down quietly while she wasn't looking. It's why she still feels protective of old, slightly shabby amusement parks, and why, at every theme park since, she has looked first for the castle.

### Summer 1998: festivals, Obon and the first Shirahama

Osaka's summer began at the end of June with the Aizen Matsuri at Shoman-in temple near Tennoji, the first festival of the year and the first time each summer that girls wore yukata. Yumiko dressed her in cotton (navy with goldfish one year, white with morning glories the next), and by 1998 Mizuki had settled the festival rules she still follows: one takoyaki, one shaved ice (always melon syrup, "which tastes of nothing but bright green"), one go at goldfish scooping, and no candied apple, because they look beautiful and are impossible to eat. In late July there were the Tenjin Matsuri fireworks, watched from a crowded bridge on Hiroshi's shoulders, his hands round her ankles, both of them sweating.

In mid-August the shop closed for Obon. The family tidied the grave, Fumiko made a little horse out of a cucumber and a cow out of an aubergine for her altar (the horse so the spirits could hurry home, the cow so they could go back slowly), and Mizuki danced bon odori in the park round a tower hung with lanterns. Fumiko told her the dead came home at Obon to see how everyone was behaving, and Mizuki took this so seriously that for the whole of Obon 1998 she did not hit Kenta once. Kenta noticed, and exploited it.

Straight after Obon came something new. Hiroshi had announced it in July at the staff dinner, in one sentence: "Three days. Shirahama. August." It became the shop's only holiday every year until 2008, and it was always the same, which was the point.

They took the Kuroshio limited express from Tennoji, two and a half hours south past rice fields, then plum orchards, then sudden flashes of blue. Mizuki fought Kenta for the window on the sea side, the right-hand side going south, and won, and spent the trip with her forehead on the glass. They stayed at a small family inn with thin futons, a big shared bath and a landlady who called Kenta "little general". Shirarahama beach was whiter than anything she had imagined sand could be. It was the first time she had swum in the sea, in a pink swimsuit and a rubber ring shaped like a duck, and she was amazed that it was salty all the way out, as if she'd assumed it would stop somewhere. Hiroshi stood waist-deep, arms folded, not swimming, watching both children with the attention he usually gave a hotplate. Before he went in, every morning, he stopped at the water's edge and gave the sea a short bow, the way the fishermen he knew from the rocks in Wakayama did. "It's bigger than us," was his whole explanation. Mizuki copied him, and has bowed to the sea before every swim since, at Shirahama, in Okinawa, and now at Bronte and Coogee, where nobody has ever asked her why.

They watched the sun go down through the hole in Engetsu island, a small rock arch offshore. They looked over the cliffs at Sandanbeki, which frightened her in a good way. And they spent a whole day at Adventure World, the animal park, where the pandas were asleep. (They were asleep in every year she ever went. She has a whole set of photos of sleeping pandas, and considers them some of her most honest work.)

The holiday had its bad moment too. On the second afternoon a wave took one of her new beach sandals, bought for the trip, and she watched it bob out of reach while she stood in the shallows holding the other one. She cried the way you cry when something is gone and it's partly your fault. Yumiko said the sandal had gone to Okinawa for a holiday of its own, and Kenta asked every evening whether it had arrived yet. The next morning Hiroshi produced a cheap pair from a beach shop without a word, a size too big. She wore them for two summers.

In the evening at the inn there was sashimi so fresh that Hiroshi, who rarely praised anything, put down his chopsticks and said, "This is correct." Mizuki didn't know yet that she would spend the rest of her life using that word for food. She just knew it was the most he had said all day.

### The audience, again: Ocarina, cards and a cinema in July

Daisuke kept coming to Fumiko's in the school holidays, older every time, and from the winter of 1998 he brought a Nintendo 64 and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, which came out on 21 November that year. Mizuki, eight, sat behind him exactly as she had for Final Fantasy VII. The controller rule had not changed. "The audience is here," he said when she arrived, and she took her place on the tatami. She liked the horse and the ocarina songs, and hummed Saria's Song until everyone begged her to stop. She did not like the skeletons, and went back to the through-my-fingers method. She also noticed, loudly, that the princess was "not very busy" for someone whose name was on the box.

Kenta, four and five in these years, was turning into a person with opinions of his own. He played the sprite to her girl in Seiken Densetsu 2 on Daisuke's old Super Famicom, which meant a running battle over the healing magic ("Heal me! Heal ME! Why are you casting on the rabbit?"). He learned to win at Super Mario Kart, mostly because Mizuki made it very clear that crying was not allowed. Isamu gave him his first Mini 4WD racing car, and he spent hours building it on the floor of the room they shared, with the concentration of a surgeon. They were already the shape they would always be: she talked, he fixed things.

The Pokémon craze, meanwhile, kept growing. The anime came back in April 1998 after the Porygon break, and Mizuki forgave it immediately. She ate Pokémon bread for the sticker inside, and kept the stickers in the same album as the Sailor Moon ones, arranged, as before, by hair. The first Pokémon film, *Mewtwo Strikes Back*, opened on 18 July 1998, and Yumiko took both children on a Wednesday in the summer holidays. When Ash was turned to stone and Pikachu kept trying to wake him, Mizuki cried so hard her nose ran on Yumiko's sleeve, then argued for a week that this was "not the same as Aerith, because it's got a happy bit". It was her first cry at a cinema, and not her last.

### Spring 1999: third grade, a new bike and a goldfish called Tanaka

Ms Ueno stayed with Mizuki's class for second grade, as teachers often did, and the farewell at the end of it in March 1999 was Mizuki's first proper goodbye to a grown-up who wasn't family. On the class's farewell card Mizuki wrote her message in her neatest kanji and added, in small letters at the edge, "Sorry about Okaasan." Ms Ueno laughed and cried at once.

In April 1999 the classes were reshuffled for third grade, and Mio, her SPEED partner, went into the class next door. They still walked to school together, but by the summer they were friendly rather than friends. Nobody did anything wrong; it was her first friendship ended by timetable, and she minded more than she said.

For her ninth birthday, on Saturday 10 April, Isamu and Chiyo gave her a new bicycle: a proper one, silver and pale blue, with a basket on the front and a bell she rang at every cat. She had learned to ride the summer before on Fumiko's old one, in the back alley behind the shop, with Hiroshi running alongside holding the seat and then, at some point she never noticed, not holding it. Chiyo told her the price of the new one twice. Mizuki rode it to Fumiko's, to the shrine with the stone foxes and round and round the temple streets until it was dark, and felt, for the first time, that the neighbourhood belonged to her.

At the end of June the Aizen Matsuri came round again, and Kenta, five, made his first serious attempt at goldfish scooping. Mizuki had never caught one; her paper scoops always broke at the first touch. Kenta took his time, chose a small orange fish near the edge, and lifted it out on the very last corner of the paper. He held the bag up to the lanterns and announced its name: Tanaka. Nobody has ever discovered why. "It's a Tanaka," he said, which was not an explanation. Tanaka lived in a glass bowl on the shelf in the children's room, then in a proper tank by the shop door, where regulars greeted it by name. It lived for six years, which Mizuki pointed out was considerably longer than her Tamagotchi.

### July 1999: the Shitennoji wall

Summer holidays began around 20 July. On a hot afternoon in the first week, Mizuki rode her new bike towards Fumiko's the long way, along the outer wall of Shitennoji, because the road there was straight and shaded and she liked to go fast. A cat sat on top of the wall. She rang her bell at it, turned her head to see if it had noticed, and her front wheel went into the edge of the gutter. The bike stopped. Mizuki didn't.

She put her left hand out to save herself, the way everyone does, and landed on it. She remembers the sound more than the pain at first, and then the pain, and then a woman with a shopping bag kneeling next to her on the hot road saying "daijōbu, daijōbu" over and over. Someone ran to the shop. Yumiko arrived still in her apron, at a speed nobody had seen before, and took one look at the arm and said, "Right," which was the most frightening thing she could have said, because she didn't make a joke.

At the clinic near Tennoji the doctor said it was a clean break in the forearm and that she had been lucky. Mizuki did not feel lucky. She felt like she had ruined the summer, which, in a nine-year-old's terms, she had. The arm went into a plaster cast from her knuckles almost to her elbow, and the doctor said the word every child dreads in July: no swimming. Shirahama was three weeks away. She cried properly then, not about the arm but about the sea.

Hiroshi came to the clinic after closing the shop, which meant he had closed it early, which had happened perhaps four times in her life. He looked at the cast, then at her, and said only, "Does it hurt?" She said yes. He nodded, as if that was the right answer, and carried her home on his back although there was nothing wrong with her legs. Fumiko was waiting at the shop with a peeled peach cut into rabbit shapes, the summer version of the apple rabbits, and the first thing she did was draw a small kappa on the cast in felt pen "to keep the water spirits friendly".

The bike, its handlebar bent, was leaned against the back wall, and nobody mentioned it. Mizuki was right-handed, so she could still write her picture diary, which she resented. Yumiko, who knew something about consolation, made a promise that night at the low table: when the cast was off, the two of them would go to the Grand Theatre at Takarazuka, just the girls. Mizuki didn't really know what that meant yet. She only knew that her mother had never promised her anything in that voice before.

The rest of that summer, the cast, the sea she couldn't swim in, and the CD that played upstairs every afternoon belong to the next part of her story. But the fall itself she keeps in this one, as the end of being small. "I was going fast," she says, "past a temple, looking at a cat. Honestly, it's a very Osaka way to break your arm."

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:**
  - **Vulpix (Rokon)**: only in Green, and Fumiko had already told her about foxes; her favourite Pokémon ever since, with a keyring and a slightly bald plush to prove it.
  - **Inari shrines and kitsune udon**: a bow "for Rokon's relatives", and fried tofu for the foxes.
  - **Final Fantasy, "the only game series that matters"**: VII from behind Daisuke's head; the chocobos, the music, the city turning into a forest.
  - **Sailor Mercury, transformation sequences and "In the name of the moon"**: cute, clever and brave at once; a good outfit change is a plot point.
  - **Misty and Team Rocket's motto**: a water girl, and a tsukkomi.
  - **School cleaning time and wiping things**: "at school there are teams".
  - **Age-pan and curry rice**: the kyushoku she still goes misty about.
  - **Sports day lunches**: ninety per cent sitting and eating; she still brings too much food.
  - **Morning glories**: "the most hopeful flower", from the first summer homework.
  - **SPEED and "White Love"**: her first music of her own, and a mirror to dance in.
  - **Sad songs**: "my graduation" taught her to like music that hurts a little.
  - **Theme park castles**: Nara Dreamland's was the first, and she still looks for the castle first.
  - **Shirahama, the sea, and the word "correct"**: the first beach, sleeping pandas, Hiroshi's praise for the sashimi.
  - **Festivals by her own rules**: one takoyaki, one melon shaved ice, one goldfish attempt.
  - **Flea markets and things with a past**: the bargain fox from Shitennoji.
  - **The Tigers, because they lose**: Jiichan's bedtime scores and "mada mada".
  - **The sound of an abacus**: like rain on a tin roof, from Chiyo.

  - **Liver**: "I paid for that in 1998."
