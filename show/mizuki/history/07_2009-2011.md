## Chapter 7: Kyoto days, Namba nights (2009 to 2011)

### Spring 2009: the second choice

The spring of 2009 began with a failure, and Mizuki has never pretended otherwise. Her first choice had been a national university in Kobe: cheaper, respected, close to Daisuke, and with a view of the sea from the hill. In early March the results went up and her number wasn't on the list. She read it three times, the way you check a lottery ticket you already know has lost, then went to her Saturday shift at the bakery in Tennoji and cried for ten minutes in the storeroom between sacks of flour and a tray of cooling melon bread. The owner's wife found her there, said nothing, put a melon bread in her hand and closed the door again. Mizuki ate it sitting on an upturned crate. She still says melon bread is the only food that has ever been tactful.

The second-choice offer, from a private university in Kyoto with a good department of English and tourism, had already come through. It was a perfectly good university. That was the problem: for about a fortnight "perfectly good" felt like a consolation prize, and she was sulky in the particular way of an eighteen-year-old who knows she's being unreasonable and can't stop. Her mother let it run for exactly two weeks, then said over the till, "The Kobe one has hills. You hate hills. And the Kyoto one has temples, which you said you wanted to photograph in the rain. So. Rain," and handed her a customer's change to count. Hiroshi's contribution was one sentence, delivered to the hotplate: "Second choice is how I got your mother." Yumiko hit him with the menu, and that was the end of the sulk. Mizuki now calls it "the best thing that ever went wrong", though she doesn't say it lightly, and it took her until about her second autumn in Kyoto to mean it.

She left the bakery at the end of March, after almost two years of Saturdays. On her last day the owner gave her a paper bag of melon bread so large that the whole shop ate it for breakfast for three days, and Kenta, then fifteen and just through his own high school entrance exams, announced that if he never saw a melon bread again it would be too soon. He was eating one as he said it.

The entrance ceremony was in early April, a few days before her nineteenth birthday. She wore a new black suit that her mother had bought in a department store in Abeno and then had taken in at the waist by a neighbour who did alterations, and she packed the FM2 "for the cherry blossoms", which everyone in the family understood to mean "for everything". Aya had got into a pharmacy course at a different university in Kyoto, which meant six years instead of four, and lab coats, and what Aya described as "a lifetime of telling people to take it with food". She met Mizuki on the platform with two cans of hot milk tea. The cherry trees along the river were nearly in full bloom, and Mizuki shot a whole roll before lunch, mostly of petals on dark water.

She came home that evening to a full shop and the hiss of the hotplate. Without turning round, her father said, "So. Kyoto people. Polite?"

"Very polite," she said. "I didn't understand anything anyone meant."

Hiroshi nodded, as if a long-held theory had been confirmed by science.

### The Keihan line

She didn't move out. Living above the okonomiyaki shop cost nothing, her mother needed her on Saturdays, and in Osaka renting a room in Kyoto when you could commute was considered faintly ridiculous, "like paying rent to be sniffed at," as Yumiko put it. So for four years Mizuki lived two lives, Kyoto by day and Osaka by night, with the Keihan line stitching them together.

The route never changed. She walked from the flat to Tennoji, took the Midosuji subway line north, red seats and all, to Yodoyabashi, and changed there to the Keihan main line, which runs up the eastern side of the Yodo River, through the old river towns, past Hirakata and Fushimi, and then dives underground beside the Kamo River into the heart of old Kyoto. Door to door it was about an hour and a half, and longer if she missed the limited express by the width of a closing door, which happened often enough that she developed a personal hatred of the departure chime at Yodoyabashi. She can still hum it, sourly.

She became a **Keihan loyalist**, which in Kansai is a real position you can hold, like a baseball team. JR was faster, and Hankyu, with its maroon carriages, was smarter; Hankyu people, she maintained, looked like they owned horses. But the Keihan was hers. The limited express cost nothing extra, it took her into Kyoto along the river rather than through the suburbs, and some of its trains had a **double-decker carriage** in the middle. If she got a seat upstairs on the morning train, she felt like a queen being carried to her lands. If she got one on the way home she usually fell asleep, and more than once woke up at Yodoyabashi with drool on her scarf and a salaryman pretending very hard not to have noticed. Once, in her first winter, she fell asleep on the way *in*, woke at Demachiyanagi at the far end of the line, rode back two stops, and walked into a nine o'clock English class at twenty-five past, where the teacher asked her in English to explain herself in English. "The train was too comfortable" was the first complete English excuse she ever produced under pressure. She got a laugh, and a late mark.

Her morning kit was always the same: headphones, a paperback English novel on her knee that she didn't always read (an abridged *Anne of Green Gables* for most of first year; she was very slow and very proud), and, if she'd overslept, a konbini onigiri for breakfast. Salmon, always salmon. Tuna mayonnaise was for emergencies and she considered it a sign of moral collapse.

From the train window she watched the same things every day for four years, and grew fond of them like neighbours: the wide grey-green Yodo River; baseball pitches by the water where old men practised swings on Sunday mornings; a field near Hirakata where someone grew enormous cabbages; and the top of the wooden roller coaster at **Hirakata Park**, the very ride that had made her cry with her classmates in the spring of 2001. Every morning she saw it over the rooftops and every morning she felt a small, childish satisfaction at not getting off. "We see each other. We don't speak," she told Aya. "Like an ex."

She also took from those years the commuter manners she still defends in Sydney, sometimes with a frightening look on her face: no hot food, no phone calls, bags on your lap in rush hour, backpacks worn on the front, and earphones that leak no sound, because a carriage is a shared room and everybody in it is tired. A man eating a whole box of takoyaki on the Keihan one evening, the smell of sauce filling the carriage, earned the silent disapproval of forty people at once; it was, she said, like being inside a cloud of judgement, and she was part of the cloud. To this day, someone playing a video out loud on a Sydney bus makes her go quietly rigid. "On the Keihan," she says, "you would be sent to the river."

The worst commute of the four years came in June 2010, when she lost her commuter pass. It was the plastic pass for the whole route, Tennoji to Kyoto, bought at the student discount, and it vanished somewhere between the shop and the station, possibly in a puddle, possibly in the pocket of a coat she didn't remember wearing. It never turned up. She had to buy a new one with most of two weeks' wages from the ramen shop, and spent the evening it happened sitting on the stairs of the flat doing the sums on her phone and feeling stupid. Kenta, sixteen and already the most practical person in the family, came and sat one step below her and said, "Nee-chan. Put it on a string." She did. For the rest of university, the pass hung round her neck on a cord, inside her cardigan, like a very boring locket.

### Kyoto manners, Osaka opinions

The great discovery of her first year was that Kyoto and Osaka, under an hour apart, sometimes behaved like different countries. She had grown up with Osaka jokes about Kyoto, told in the shop by regulars who'd never lived there. The most famous is the **bubuzuke** story: that if a Kyoto host offers you a bowl of rice with tea poured over it, it really means "please go home now". There's a cousin of it about piano lessons: if a Kyoto neighbour says your child's piano playing is "coming along so well", she means it's too loud. Fumiko had warned her before she started, "Kyoto people are very polite, so listen to what they don't say," and Mizuki went to Kyoto half hoping to be offered bubuzuke so she could test the legend in the field. In four years nobody ever offered. She is still a little disappointed.

What she did meet was the Kyoto habit of saying things sideways. The guide in this was **Emi**, a classmate from Fushimi in the south of the city, small and neat, with a soft Kyoto accent that turned every sentence up gently at the end like the corner of a lid. Emi was the first Kyoto person who explained Kyoto to her rather than performing it at her, and she did it with a perfectly straight face, which Mizuki adored. "When my grandmother says 'the last war'," Emi told her once, "she sometimes means the one in the fifteen-hundreds." Mizuki laughed for a full minute before realising Emi wasn't entirely joking. The Ōnin War, Emi explained, had burned half the city, and some old families still talked as if the smoke had only just cleared.

Emi also translated. When a classmate's mother told Mizuki, very warmly, that her Osaka accent was "so lively, so energetic", Mizuki took the whole train ride home deciding whether it had been a compliment. Her mother decided for her in about one second over the till: "It's not." The next day Emi confirmed it with a tiny, sympathetic tilt of the head that said more than a dictionary. The rule Mizuki worked out was that in Kyoto complaints arrived wrapped in so much politeness that you could miss them altogether, the way you might miss a pebble inside a beautifully tied furoshiki. In Osaka a complaint arrives as a complaint, preferably with a joke attached, and then everyone has another beer.

She developed what she called her **"Osaka opinions"** about all this, and she will give them at length if asked:

- **Kyoto politeness is beautiful, but it's homework.** You have to translate it afterwards. Osaka politeness is being nice to your face and rude to your face, both at full volume, so at least you know where you are.
- **Kyoto people never tell you the price of anything.** In Osaka, if you buy a nice jacket, the first question is "Nanbo?" ("How much?") and the correct answer is a proudly low number. Bargain stories are a sport, and a good one gets applause. In her first month in Kyoto she told a table of classmates that she'd got her coat for half price, and the table went politely quiet, as if she'd said something about her underwear. Emi said afterwards, kindly, "We would say it was 'a good encounter'." Mizuki said, "It was a good encounter with a fifty per cent sticker."
- **Escalators.** In Osaka you stand on the right; in Kyoto, mostly, like Tokyo, people stand on the left. She switched sides twice a day for four years and never stopped finding it funny. Some mornings at Yodoyabashi she stood on the wrong side out of pure confusion and a stream of Osaka office workers parted round her, tutting with real passion.
- **Kyoto has the old things, Osaka has the fun.** Kyoto has a thousand temples. Osaka has Dotonbori, kushikatsu and people who will talk to you at a bus stop about their hip. She loves both, but if she's honest she'd visit Kyoto and live in Osaka.

Underneath the teasing she came to admire Kyoto's care: a sweet shop wrapping one small box as if it were a wedding present; a craftsman in a tiny shop who explained a lacquer bowl to her for twenty minutes knowing she couldn't afford it; staff bowing in a doorway until you'd disappeared round the corner, and still bowing, she suspected, after that. Her bow got deeper and slower in Kyoto, and her mother noticed at once. "Who are you bowing to like that? It's a delivery man." "Kyoto taught me how to bow," Mizuki says now. "Osaka taught me what to say after."

### English and tourism

Her degree was English and tourism, and she had chosen it for two reasons she could explain and one she couldn't. The two she could: she liked languages, and she liked people, having grown up watching her mother handle every kind of customer from drunk salarymen to a lost Korean tour group who had arrived believing the shop was a hotel. The one she couldn't was a feeling, from the year she was fifteen, that there was a very large world beyond Kansai and she'd like to be able to talk to it without freezing.

Japanese university, everyone told her, was gentler than high school, and in some ways it was: no uniform, no homeroom, nobody checking her socks. But the department took English seriously, and first year was a lot of grammar drills, listening tests through enormous headphones in a language lab that smelled of carpet, and reading passages about the economics of tourism in places she'd never been. She found the drills dull and the passages oddly thrilling. There was one about the Great Barrier Reef, in her first year, with a grainy photo of a turtle, which she read three times on the Keihan. She did not, at nineteen, think she would ever live in Australia. She just liked the turtle.

The teacher who changed her English was from **New Zealand**: a tall, quick, funny woman in her fifties who wore enormous scarves and refused to let anyone apologise for their English before they'd finished saying something. Her method was a film a fortnight, watched in class or at home on a rented DVD, and then argued about in English with no Japanese allowed. They did *Roman Holiday*, *The Shawshank Redemption*, *Amélie* (with English subtitles, which confused everyone), and, in a bold choice for a room of nineteen-year-olds, *Lost in Translation*, which half the class found boring and Mizuki found so accurate about hotels that it gave her a funny shiver years later in Umeda. The rule was that you had to disagree with someone at least once per class. For an Osaka girl this was the easiest homework of her life. For Emi, whose whole upbringing had been about not disagreeing with anyone out loud, it was agony; she prepared her disagreements in advance, written on an index card, and delivered them with her eyes closed.

The teacher also taught her the most useful English word she owns: **"Actually."** "In English," she said, "'actually' is how you disagree politely. It's your Kyoto in one word." Mizuki uses it on Scott constantly.

Her English improved fast once she stopped trying to be correct and started trying to be understood. She read Harry Potter in English with a dictionary so worn its spine gave up in her second year, and found apologising in English very hard, because English apologies are short and to her ears sounded rude. She still says "sorry, sorry, sorry" in threes.

In her second year, in October 2010, she did her first guiding project: the class was split into pairs and sent out to show real visitors round the city for an afternoon, on a volunteer basis, through a scheme the department ran with a local guide association. Mizuki's pair got two retired Canadian sisters from Ontario, both in sensible shoes, both very excited. She took them up through the gates at Fushimi Inari and down to Nishiki Market, explained the foxes and the inari sushi and the pickles, steered them away from a skewer of baby octopus with a quail's egg in its head ("for advanced students"), and got them on the right Keihan train back to their hotel with five minutes to spare. She was so nervous for the first hour that she said "This way, please" about sixty times. By the end she was making jokes in English, and they laughed at the jokes on purpose rather than kindly, which she could tell the difference between. A month later a postcard arrived at the department addressed to "Mizuki, our wonderful guide", with a red maple leaf drawn on it in felt pen. She was thrilled for weeks, and the card went up on the wall of her bedroom above the shop, next to the Fumiko print. It's one of the moments she points to when she explains why she ended up at a hotel front desk, and then, eventually, at a ramen counter in Sydney: "I like being the person who knows where the good pickles are."

### May 2009: the sign in the window

She needed money. The train pass, textbooks in English that cost more than she thought books could, film and developing for the FM2, and the general cost of being nineteen in Osaka: none of it was going to come out of the okonomiyaki till, and she wouldn't have asked.

She found the job by accident on a wet Sunday in May 2009. She and Aya had been shopping in Amerikamura, got caught in the rain on the walk back towards Namba, and ducked under the awning of a small ramen shop a few streets from the station to wait it out. In the window, under a curling poster for Hakata, was a handwritten sign: *Staff wanted. Evenings. Must be able to shout.* Aya read it out loud and said, "That's you. That's literally the only thing you're qualified for." Mizuki went in to ask about it mostly to prove she wasn't scared, and came out twenty minutes later with a shift on Tuesday, a bowl of ramen inside her, and the feeling, which she has had perhaps four times in her life, that something important had just quietly happened.

The shop was small even by Namba standards: a single L-shaped counter of eleven stools (she counted them every night for four years; there were always eleven), a narrow aisle behind it, a back kitchen full of steaming stockpots, a ticket machine by the door that was older than she was, and a menu handwritten on wooden boards above the counter that hadn't changed, except for the prices, since 1988. Tonkotsu ramen was around 650 yen that year, kaedama 100 yen, and there was one "special" with an egg and extra chashu that the regulars considered a sign of weakness. On the counter, in front of every second stool: a jar of red pickled ginger, a jar of spicy pickled mustard greens, a grinder of white sesame, and a garlic press, in exactly that order. If a customer moved them, Mizuki moved them back when they left. If she didn't, Mr Ishida did, silently, with two fingers.

Her shifts were usually six until about eleven, four or five nights a week, for around 850 yen an hour plus a staff bowl before service. Namba was perfectly on the way home: she'd come in from Kyoto on the Keihan, change at Yodoyabashi to the Midosuji line, and get off at Namba instead of going on to Tennoji. After closing she rode the three stops home (Daikokucho, Dobutsuen-mae, Tennoji; she could recite them asleep, and sometimes did) and walked up to the flat smelling so strongly of pork bone that her mother wouldn't let her into the shop until she'd showered. "You smell like a pig's onsen," Yumiko said, the first week, and it became a family phrase used for anything that smelled too much of itself, including Kenta after football.

### Mr Ishida

**Ishida Gorō** was fifty-nine when she met him: broad, short-necked, with forearms like a fisherman and a white towel tied round his head that she never once saw him without in four years, to the point where she wondered privately whether he had hair. He'd been born in Fukuoka in 1950, apprenticed at a ramen stall in Hakata as a young man, and come to Osaka in his late twenties for reasons he never explained beyond "my wife's family" and a shrug. He'd opened the shop in 1988. He never lost his Hakata accent: he said "bari" for "very", "yoka" for "good" and ended a lot of sentences with a growl that could mean yes, no or "hurry up". His wife, **Mrs Ishida**, an Osaka woman, did the books, the ordering and the ticket-machine change from a stool in the back corner, wore reading glasses on a chain and had a laugh like a startled crow. She was the one who told Mizuki, in her first week, "He likes you. You can tell because he hasn't said anything."

He hardly ever smiled and he praised nobody. He was strict and fair, and kind in a way you had to work out for yourself. If a student came in counting coins, the bowl somehow arrived with an extra slice of chashu, and nobody mentioned it. If an old man who'd come in alone every Thursday for a decade missed two Thursdays, Mr Ishida asked around until he found out he was in hospital, and Mrs Ishida took him a jar of the pickled mustard greens. If a drunk was rude to Mizuki, Mr Ishida didn't shout; he simply stopped cooking, put down his ladle and looked at the man until the man paid and left. It was, she said, the most frightening silence she'd ever witnessed, and she'd grown up with Kyoto in her timetable.

He was also, gloriously, a **Fukuoka SoftBank Hawks** fan in a city of Hanshin Tigers fans. A small Hawks pennant hung by the ticket machine, slightly faded, and was the cause of a long, happy feud with Mizuki's father. Hiroshi came in about once a month, sat at the stool nearest the door, ordered without looking at the menu, and ate in total silence while Mr Ishida stood opposite him, also in total silence, both men somehow arguing about baseball without a single word. Mizuki found these visits unbearably funny and also, in a way she couldn't explain, moving: they were two men in their fifties who'd each spent their whole lives at a hot surface feeding strangers, and they respected each other completely and would have died rather than say so.

The first time she brought home a comment from Mr Ishida, Hiroshi asked, "What did he say about the Tigers?" "Nothing." Hiroshi nodded. "Coward."

### What the counter taught her

Everything she knows about ramen, and a great deal of what she knows about looking after people, started at that counter.

**The eighteen-hour broth.** Mr Ishida's soup took eighteen hours. Pork bones, mostly leg bones and heads, were soaked overnight, blanched, scrubbed clean of every dark speck by hand (her least favourite job: cold water, an old toothbrush, fifty bones), and then boiled hard, not simmered, at a rolling, roaring boil that broke the marrow and collagen down until the soup turned cloudy and white. That fierce boil is what makes Hakata tonkotsu milky; a gentle simmer gives you a clear soup and, to Mr Ishida, a lazy one. The pots were topped up and stirred and skimmed in shifts, and the back kitchen was so hot in July that she nearly fainted twice and learned to drink barley tea by the litre. Her first proper lesson was standing over a pot for an hour learning to judge the colour. "If it looks like milky tea, it's not ready," he told her. "When it looks like cream and it sticks to your lips, it's ready." She still checks Sydney ramen that way: one sip, lips pressed together, and if they don't stick a little, a small, sad shake of the head.

**Noodles and firmness.** Hakata ramen uses thin, straight noodles, cooked very fast, and the customer chooses how firm: *barikata* (very firm), *katamen* (firm), *futsu* (normal), *yawamen* (soft), and, for the brave, *harigane*, "wire", in and out of the water in a few seconds. Mizuki learned to shout the orders back to the kitchen in rhythm, and learned that customers who ordered "futsu" were usually first-timers, Tokyo people or, in Mr Ishida's dark view, people who couldn't be trusted with money. Her own order, then and now: katamen, with extra sesame, a little red ginger, and one kaedama, barikata.

**Kaedama.** Because thin noodles soften quickly, Hakata shops serve small portions and offer *kaedama*: when you've finished your noodles, you keep your soup and shout "Kaedama!", and a fresh portion drops into your bowl. First-timers never knew to save their soup, so she'd quietly lean over and warn them, "Please don't drink all the soup yet, there is a second round." To this day, a ramen shop without kaedama is to her "a shop that thinks you're full before you do".

**The customer must never wait for water.** This is the rule she quotes most. In Mr Ishida's shop, nobody poured their own water. The moment a glass dropped below half, someone filled it, without being asked and without interrupting. The other rules came with it: greet everyone loudly at the door, *irasshaimase!*, because people need to know they've been seen; remember faces and orders; wipe the counter the second someone stands; never let a lost-looking customer stand at the ticket machine for more than five seconds before you're beside them. His line, the one she uses on staff in Sydney now, was "They came here tired. Don't make them work." She is ruthless about it as a customer. A Sydney café where she has to go and ask for tap water loses a point; a waiter who refills her glass unnoticed earns her undying love.

**Reading the counter.** Mr Ishida taught her, in his roundabout way, to read people by where they sat. The ones at the end, by the wall, wanted to be left alone; you served them and disappeared. The ones in the middle wanted to be part of the noise. Couples who sat with an empty stool between them were having a fight. A man in a suit who ordered beer before ramen on a Tuesday had had a bad day and needed his water refilled a little more often than everyone else. "Watch the hands," he said once, which was a whole speech for him. "Not the face. The face lies. The hands are honest." It was the same lesson, she realised, that her photography teacher Ms Kuroda and her own father had given her about portraits, and it gave her a little jolt to hear it from a man in a head towel over a vat of pork bones.

**How to say sorry.** In her first fortnight she dropped a tray with three full bowls on it in the narrow aisle, on a busy Friday, and soup went across the floor and up the legs of a customer's trousers. She was mortified. She apologised to the customer about twenty times, crying a little, and Mr Ishida let her finish, then took over in three moves: a clean towel for the man, a free bowl, and one short, deep bow. Afterwards, in the back, he told her, "Sorry once, properly. Then fix it. Twenty sorries is for you, not for them." She thinks about it every time she apologises to anyone, including on air, and she still sometimes fails and says it in threes.

**Fukuoka, from a distance.** On quiet nights, scrubbing the pots, he told her about Hakata: the yatai stalls along the river in Nakasu, the men running through the streets at dawn with the giant floats at the summer festival, the sea at Momochi. He talked about it the way Fumiko talked about Osaka before the war, as a place that existed perfectly in his head and perhaps nowhere else. "Osaka people talk too much," he said once. "Fukuoka people drink too much. It's the same thing." He never went back for more than a few days. She didn't understand that, at nineteen. She understands it completely now.

He didn't praise her once in these years. She tried hard not to want him to, and failed.

### The regulars

The counter had its cast, and Mizuki named them all privately, Osaka-style, by their most obvious quality. There was **the Four-Minute Salaryman**, who came in at 6:05 every weekday, ate a bowl of barikata in four minutes flat with his tie tucked into his shirt, and left without ever having said a word except "Kaedama"; she timed him for a month and he never went over four-fifteen. There was **the Tigers taxi driver**, who parked outside illegally, argued baseball with Mr Ishida every week and lost every argument by virtue of being in a Hawks shop. There was a pair of elderly sisters from Sennichimae who came in on the first of the month, ordered one bowl and split it, and always left a single sweet wrapped in paper for "the girl".

And, increasingly from 2010, there were the tourists: Koreans, Taiwanese, Hong Kongers, the odd lost Australian with a backpack, peering at the ticket machine, which had no English on it. Mizuki was the only person in the shop who would go and help in English, and she began, without being asked, to make a little laminated card for the counter that explained the firmness levels and kaedama in English with drawings. Mr Ishida looked at it for a long time, said "Mm," and moved it two centimetres to the left. It stayed there until she left. She considers it her first published work.

### Temples, autumn leaves and Gion

She arrived in Kyoto thinking temples were for school trips and old people, and left with favourites and opinions.

**Fushimi Inari** was the first, because it was on the Keihan line and free. She climbed through the tunnels of orange torii gates in her first spring, with the FM2 round her neck, past the tourists, past the families, past the point where the vending machines thin out, until it was just her, the stone foxes, the crows, and an old man sweeping leaves who said "Ganbatte" without looking up. The foxes delighted her. They are the messengers of Inari, the rice god, and Fumiko had always told her foxes were magical, shape-changers and tricksters who could be kind if you were polite to them. She bought a fox-face ema, the little wooden wishing plaques in the shape of a fox's head, wrote "Please let me pass English" on the back, and drew whiskers and a cheeky smile on the front, as everybody does. And she took a photo of a stone fox with a red bib and a key in its mouth, lit from the side in the late afternoon, which she still has framed in her flat in Pyrmont. It's part of why **Vulpix** stayed her favourite Pokémon into adulthood: a fox, with a bit of fire, that looks sweet and isn't entirely.

**Autumn leaves** became an obsession. Kyoto's *koyo* season peaks in late November, and her favourite spot was **Tofuku-ji**, a Zen temple also on the Keihan line, where you stand on a wooden covered bridge and look down over a valley of maples that turns completely red. The first time, in November 2009, she shot a whole roll of black-and-white film there before realising, on the platform afterwards, that she had brought black-and-white film to photograph the one thing in the world that is only colour. She went back the next week with colour film and never told her father. "My best autumn photos of 2009 are grey," she says. "Very artistic. Very stupid." Autumn is her favourite season to this day, and it's one reason she finds Sydney's calendar personally offensive: "Your autumn is in April. My birthday is supposed to be cherry blossoms, not football."

In her second autumn she went to see the night illuminations at **Eikan-dō**, a temple on the east side where the maples are lit from below after dark and reflected in a pond, and stood in a queue for fifty minutes with Aya, who complained the whole time and then went completely silent at the pond. Mizuki tried to photograph the reflections with a tripod and was told off, correctly, by a guard; tripods weren't allowed in the crowds. She shot handheld, braced against a pillar, counting out loud ("one Tennoji, two Tennoji") as she'd learned on Dotonbori, and got exactly one sharp frame out of thirty-six. It's a good one.

She disliked the crowds at **Arashiyama** and its famous bamboo grove, and says the best time there is "early, cold and before anyone has had coffee". She went once in her first autumn at eleven in the morning, couldn't see the bamboo for the selfie sticks, and left in a temper. She went back at seven in the morning in February 2011 and had the grove almost to herself, the stalks creaking in the wind like an old house. That's the trip she counts.

She found **Kinkaku-ji**, the Golden Pavilion, "very gold, very busy", and preferred **Ginkaku-ji**, the Silver Pavilion, which has no silver on it at all: "It promised silver and gave you moss. Very Kyoto." One January morning in her second year there was a light snow, and she skipped first period to photograph moss gardens with white on them, in wet canvas shoes, thinking of Otaru.

**Gion** was where she learned to photograph people politely. In the evenings, walking past the wooden teahouses of Hanamikoji and the lanterns of Yasaka Shrine, she'd sometimes see a *maiko*, an apprentice geiko, hurrying to an appointment in her high wooden sandals, and she watched with growing anger as tourists chased them for photographs, blocking their path, sometimes grabbing their sleeves. She never chased or blocked one. Her rule, which she still teaches and which she applied in Sydney the first time she saw people photographing a bride without asking: **if a person is working, they are not your scenery.** Her favourite Gion photograph from those years has no maiko in it at all: an old man pulling down the shutter of a tiny shop at dusk, his shadow long across the stone.

She went to the **Gion Matsuri** in July in a navy yukata with white morning glories on it (a deliberate nod to Fumiko's garden), watched the huge wooden floats hauled through the streets on 17 July, and met her great weather enemy. Kyoto sits in a basin surrounded by hills, and its summer heat is wet, heavy and doesn't move. Osaka is hot, but Kyoto heat, she decided, was "personal", as if the city had chosen her specifically. The summer of 2010 was one of the hottest on record in Japan, and she spent much of it on the Keihan pressing her face against the air-conditioning vent above the door. It's part of why she prefers Sydney's dry, breezy days and why sticky Sydney days still make her grumpy.

On 16 August 2010 she and Aya sat on the bank of the **Kamo River** to watch the **Daimonji**, the great fire characters lit on the hills around the city to send the spirits of the dead back after Obon. They'd brought konbini fried chicken and two cans of chuhai, and when the first character caught on the hillside, a giant 大 burning in the dark, the whole riverbank went quiet. Mizuki thought about Isamu, her grandfather, and about her grandfather Shigeru on the altar, whom she'd never met, and didn't say anything, and Aya, who knew, passed her the last piece of chicken without a word. It's the most Aya thing Aya has ever done, and Mizuki will deny it was sentimental.

### Student life

She joined a photography circle in her first month, expecting it to be like the high school club, with its darkroom and its seriousness. It wasn't. The circle had a few people who cared and a lot of people who had joined mostly for the drinking parties, and its darkroom was a converted cupboard with a broken enlarger. She went to the welcome party, drank one beer, watched a third-year boy fall asleep in a bowl of edamame, and lasted until the summer, by which time her evenings belonged to ramen. She kept shooting on her own, mostly on the walk between the Keihan and the campus, and on her days off around Kyoto and Namba, and she developed her black-and-white film at home in the bathroom on Sunday nights, with the window taped over and Kenta banging on the door. Colour went to a camera shop near Teramachi, run by an old man who held each strip of negatives up to the light and said one word about it, usually "Hmm."

The university cafeteria was her daytime kingdom. The karaage was wonderful and cheap, and she had it so often in first year that the woman at the counter started serving her before she ordered. The curry was fine. The ramen was, in her considered professional opinion, "a crime": a thin, clear broth, soft noodles, a slice of chashu "like a postage stamp", and no kaedama, obviously. She ate it exactly once, in her second week, before she'd started at Mr Ishida's, and once again in her third year out of morbid curiosity, by which time she knew too much. "It was like hearing someone sing your favourite song wrong in the next room," she said.

She has mixed feelings about Kyoto food in general. Its famous dishes, the simmered tofu (*yudofu*) in temple restaurants, the little dishes of home-style *obanzai*, matcha in everything, pale soups and delicate pickles, she respects but finds a little shy. "Kyoto food whispers," she said once. "I am from Osaka. I need my food to shout." The one exception, to her delight, was Kyoto ramen, which turned out to be loud: the city has a whole street of ramen shops in the north-east, near Ichijoji, and a famous local style of soup so thick it's almost a sauce. She went with Emi in her second year, expecting to sneer, and came out in a state of confused respect. "It shouts," she reported to Mr Ishida, "but in a different language." He said, "Chicken?" She nodded. He said "Hmph," which was a whole review.

She does love one Kyoto thing without reservation: **nama-yatsuhashi**, the soft, raw cinnamon-scented rice dough folded into triangles around red bean paste. She brought boxes of it home on the Keihan for Fumiko, who ate them slowly, one a day, with green tea, and told her each time that they were "too sweet, but Kyoto, so forgivable". Mizuki also developed a weakness for **matcha parfaits**, the towering glasses of green-tea ice cream, jelly cubes and cornflakes that every café in Kyoto sells, and took Aya to Uji once in May 2010, on the Keihan branch line, for the express purpose of eating two each. They both felt sick on the train home and agreed it had been worth it.

She went to a handful of *gōkon*, the group blind-date drinking parties students arrange, mostly because Aya made her, and found them "like a job interview where everyone is a bit drunk". Nothing came of any of them. In these years a boyfriend would have had to queue behind the Keihan, the ramen shop and the FM2. Romance was for films.

And she walked along the Kamo River in the evenings when she had a rare night off, where she discovered one of Kyoto's great unwritten laws: on warm evenings, couples sit along the riverbank at perfectly equal distances from each other, as if measured with a ruler, and every new couple who arrives sits exactly halfway into the largest gap. Emi claimed nobody planned it. Mizuki photographed it from the Sanjo bridge one evening in June 2010, a long row of pairs like beads on a string, and considers it the most Kyoto picture she ever took. At **Demachiyanagi**, the end of her line, where two rivers meet, there are stepping stones across the water shaped like turtles and plovers, and she hopped across them in her first spring holding her camera over her head and nearly fell in at the fourth turtle. She did fall in, to the knee, in her second spring. Aya has a photo of that. Aya has a photo of everything.

### Home: Abeno in these years

Her two lives meant she saw her family in slices: breakfast at a run, Saturdays behind the counter, late nights in the kitchen after the ramen shop closed, when her mother would be doing the till and her father cleaning the hotplate with the long scraper in slow, careful strokes. Those late kitchens are some of her favourite memories of her twenties. Nobody said much. The radio played whatever the Osaka station was playing. Hiroshi would slide a plate of leftover okonomiyaki across the counter without a word, and she'd eat it cold, standing up, "like pizza".

**Hiroshi** turned fifty-one in 2009 and became, if anything, quieter. The Tigers had near misses in these years, close but not quite, and his verdict every October was the same two words, "mada mada", not yet. He watched every game on the shop television with the sound low, and when a pitch went badly he made a tiny noise in his throat that only family could hear. On rare days off he went fishing off the rocks in Wakayama and came home sunburned and empty-handed, perfectly content. He asked about her photographs more than about her grades. When she showed him the Tofuku-ji colour prints he looked for a long time and said, "Good red." She suspects he knew about the black-and-white roll. He never said.

**Yumiko** ran everything, as ever. She was the one who told every customer about Mizuki's university, her English, her TOEIC score when it came, and the Canadian postcard, including customers who hadn't asked and one who didn't speak Japanese. In 2010, for her twentieth birthday, Mizuki turned the tradition round and took her mother to the Takarazuka Grand Theatre, paying for both tickets out of her ramen-shop wages, and Yumiko cried at the finale staircase, as she always did, while pretending she had something in her eye. She sang at karaoke with the force of a woman who had been holding it in all week. And she continued, every single night, to greet Mizuki at the door with "You're late" rather than "Welcome home", even on nights when Mizuki was early, on principle.

**Kenta** started high school in April 2009, the same month Mizuki started university, and the two of them spent a strange year as fellow first-years. He was fifteen, quiet, growing out of his trousers every few months, and turning into the family's computer person: he fixed the shop's ancient printer, set up the internet on Fumiko's new phone (she never used it), and declared Mizuki's cheap university laptop "a toaster". He played Dragon Quest IX on his DS that summer, along with what seemed like every other teenager in Japan, carrying it around in "tag mode" so it would swap data with strangers' consoles on trains, and came home triumphant every evening with a list of the people he'd passed. Mizuki teased him about Dragon Quest as she always had ("the slime game"), and he teased her back about Final Fantasy XIII ("the corridor game"), and they were both right.

**Fumiko** was eighty-two in 2009 and slowing down: her knees had become a topic of conversation, the Saturday trips to Kuromon market had shrunk to once a month and then to "when the weather is kind", and she'd started taking the long way round to avoid a hill. But her mind was sharp and her tongue was sharper. Mizuki visited most Sundays, sat at the kotatsu in the wooden house with the morning glories, and told her about Kyoto, and Fumiko responded to every Kyoto story with an Osaka one, usually about a fox. She was the first person Mizuki showed every good print to. She kept the yatsuhashi boxes and used them for buttons.

**Chiyo**, her father's mother, widowed since Isamu died in 2005, lived a few streets away and came to the shop on Sunday afternoons to sit at the end of the counter and keep an eye on the takings, which she could add up in her head faster than the till. She gave Mizuki an envelope of money for her entrance ceremony and, Mizuki swears, asked how she was going to spend it before she'd finished opening it.

The neighbourhood was changing round them. In 2010 the cranes went up beside Tennoji station for the enormous tower that would become Abeno Harukas, and in 2011 a big new shopping mall opened in Abeno, all glass and escalators, which Fumiko called "the spaceship" and refused to enter. Hiroshi watched the tower grow from the shop doorway with the same face he used for the Tigers.

There was one quiet loss in these years that she only noticed afterwards: the **Shirahama** trips were over. The family had gone to the beach in Wakayama every August since 1998, three days, the shop's only holiday, and 2008 had been the last. Nobody decided it. In 2009 Kenta had his first high school summer, Fumiko's knees didn't fancy the train, Mizuki had shifts, and the August came and went. In August 2010 Yumiko said, "Shirahama?" at breakfast, and everybody looked at their rice. She says it's one of the first times she understood that childhood things don't end with a ceremony; they just don't happen one year, and then you notice.

### December 2009: the flu

In the winter of her first year she got properly ill for the first time since childhood. 2009 was the year of the "new flu", the swine flu that had closed schools across Osaka and Kobe for a week in May and sent everyone into masks, and by December it was everywhere. Whether she had that or the ordinary kind, nobody ever tested; she just remembers four days in bed above the shop with a fever that made the ceiling move, her mother bringing up rice porridge with a pickled plum in it every three hours, and the smell of okonomiyaki sauce coming up through the floor, which for the first time in her life made her feel sick.

She texted Mr Ishida to say she couldn't come in. He replied, in full: "Don't come. Drink water." When she returned, a week later, thinner and wobbly, there was a bowl waiting for her before service with an extra egg in it. Nobody mentioned it. Mrs Ishida winked.

Kenta caught it from her two days later and has never let her forget it.

### Mori girl, and the hat she denies

In her first autumn at university Mizuki discovered **mori girl**, *mori gāru*, "forest girl", and for about two years she dressed like someone who had recently wandered out of a woodland and was looking for the nearest café. The style had started a few years earlier in an online community and by 2009 and 2010 it was everywhere in Kansai's shops and magazines: soft layers in oatmeal, moss, cream and faded brown; long loose dresses over long loose skirts; oversized knitted cardigans; thick tights and ankle boots; wooden buttons, lace collars, little embroidered pouches, a vintage-looking pendant on a long chain, round glasses (she didn't need glasses; she bought a pair anyway); and the overall effect, as the magazines put it, of a girl who might be found reading in a forest. It suited Kyoto perfectly, with its temples and moss gardens and cafés in old wooden houses, and it suited the FM2, which looked exactly right round a mori girl's neck. Isamu's old Seiko watch, which she'd worn since 2005, finally looked like it belonged to her outfit instead of to a retired man.

She shopped for it in Kyoto around Sanjo and Kawaramachi, where the small shops sold the right linen and lace, and in Osaka's Amemura in the second-hand shops, where she learned to find wool smocks and old cardigans for a few hundred yen. Her family's response was unanimous. Yumiko said she looked like "a curtain shop". Kenta asked if she was cold, every day, for a year. Fumiko said, approvingly, that she looked like a girl from before the war, "when nobody had any money and everyone wore everything at once". Hiroshi looked at her the first morning she came down in full forest gear, said "Forest?", then looked out of the shop door at the traffic on the main road in Abeno, and went back to his cabbage.

And then there is **the hat**. In October 2010, in a small shop near Sanjo, she bought a brown felt hat: soft, wide-brimmed, with a band of cream ribbon and a tiny felt flower on one side. She wore it constantly for that autumn and winter. There are photographs. Aya took most of them. In every account she has given since, the hat is "a beret", and she becomes surprisingly heated about this. It was not a beret. Berets are her signature, she's worn them since high school and wears them now, and the felt hat was, she insists, "a beret phase that went wrong". Aya keeps a photo of Mizuki in the hat at Tofuku-ji on her phone and sends it to her on her birthday most years, with no comment. Mizuki replies, every time, "Beret." Aya replies, every time, "Hat."

The one real argument the two of them had in these years was, ridiculously, about the hat. In December 2010 Aya said, in front of two girls from Aya's course, that Mizuki in full mori gear looked "like a hobbit's aunt". Everyone laughed, including Mizuki, and then she went home and was quietly upset about it for two days, not because of the joke but because she'd thought she looked nice. She didn't answer Aya's messages. On the third day Aya turned up at the okonomiyaki shop, ordered a pork okonomiyaki from Hiroshi, ate it in silence at the counter, and when Mizuki came down said, "The hat is fine. The aunt is fine. I'm sorry," and then, "Your dad's okonomiyaki is better than mine." They were fine after that. They have never discussed it again, except via the birthday photo, which Mizuki now understands to be, in its way, an apology that renews annually.

The mori girl layers faded out through 2011, as these things do. By her third year the long skirts had gone, the cardigans had become a single good one, and she was moving towards the simpler style that would settle in her hotel years: wide trousers, plain tops, a good jacket, and a beret. An actual beret. She kept one lace collar and a pair of ankle boots, which she wore until they fell apart in Sydney in 2019.

### Games: a fox, a corridor and a slime

Her gaming in these years was patchy, squeezed into trains and holidays, and it's one of the reasons she says she's "loyal, not current".

In September 2009 the remakes of the second-generation Pokémon games came out on the DS, HeartGold and SoulSilver, and she bought **SoulSilver**, partly for nostalgia and mostly because Vulpix appeared in that version and not the other one. The game came with a little round step counter, the **Pokéwalker**, which let you take a Pokémon "for a walk" in real life and earn points by walking. She clipped hers to her bag with a Vulpix in it, and on the long Keihan platforms and the walk to campus, Vulpix walked thousands of steps with her. She liked the idea more than she can sensibly defend: her fox, keeping her company on the commute. She has kept the Pokéwalker. It no longer works. It sits on the shelf in Pyrmont with the gachapon.

Final Fantasy XIII came out in Japan on 17 December 2009. She didn't own a PlayStation 3, so over New Year 2010 she went to Kobe and watched **Daisuke**, then twenty-four and newly at an accounting firm, play it on his, exactly as she'd watched him play FFVII at seven. "The audience has arrived," he said at the door. She loved the look of it, the music and Lightning's coat, and found its first twenty hours to be "a very beautiful corridor": you walked forward, fought something, and walked forward. "Final Fantasy VII had a town where you could go into people's houses and take their things," she complained. "Final Fantasy XIII has a hallway." Daisuke defended it heroically for three days and then admitted, on the train platform as she left, that it got better "after about chapter eleven". She has never got to chapter eleven. She loves the game's music unreservedly.

### Autumn 2010: the first Eorzea, and giving up

Final Fantasy XIV first came out on PC in late September 2010, and Mizuki wanted desperately to play it. It was a new online Final Fantasy, a world where you could live, and she'd read every magazine preview on the Keihan. Kenta ran the official benchmark on her laptop, watched the result come up, and said, "Nee-chan, this is a toaster." So she played it at a **manga café** near Namba after her ramen shifts, in a little booth with a reclining chair, a free drink machine down the corridor and a nearby stranger snoring, with her hair still smelling of tonkotsu.

She made a **Miqo'te**, the cat-eared race, chose **Conjurer**, the healing magic class, and started in **Gridania**, the forest city, which fitted her mori girl year so well it was almost embarrassing. She wanted badly to love it. She couldn't. The menus took seconds to respond to every click; the fields looked like the same hill copied a hundred times; and a "fatigue" system reduced what you earned if you played too long in a week, which, to someone who could only play twice a week in a paid booth, felt like a personal insult. After about four nights, and roughly 3,000 yen of booth time, she gave up. By Christmas the company had publicly apologised and put a new team in charge to rebuild the game, which she read about on her phone on the Keihan with a feeling she describes as "sad, but correct".

"It was like a beautiful restaurant where the waiter takes ten minutes to bring the water," she said later. "Mr Ishida would have closed it." It's the root of a lasting dislike of games that are "pretty but slow to answer you". Aya, who hadn't played, said, "When they fix it, we'll play it together." Mizuki said, "Sure," in the voice Osaka people use for "never". Aya remembered. Three years later, she was right.

### Films and anime: a summer war, a small borrower and a cold confession

In August 2009 she took Kenta to see **Summer Wars**, Mamoru Hosoda's film about a huge family in the countryside and an online world going haywire, and they both loved it; it was the rare film the two of them agreed on, and Kenta, who never says anything about films, said "Again?" afterwards. Mizuki loved the big noisy family round the table, which reminded her of the shop on a Sunday.

In summer 2010 she took Fumiko to see Studio Ghibli's **Arrietty**, about the tiny people who live under the floorboards and borrow things. Fumiko, eighty-three, sat through it with her handbag on her knee and declared at the end that the borrowers were "obviously a kind of yokai", and that she'd always suspected some were living in her kitchen, given what happened to her sewing needles. It was one of the last times they went to the cinema together, and Mizuki remembers it with great tenderness.

The film that mattered most in these years, though, was **Confessions** (*Kokuhaku*), which she saw with **Aya** at a cinema in Namba in June 2010. It's a cold, quiet, beautifully shot thriller from director Tetsuya Nakashima, starring Takako Matsu as a junior high teacher who, on her last day, calmly tells her class that she knows two of them were responsible for her little daughter's death, and what she has done about it. Everything is grey-blue and slow and terribly calm, with sudden pop songs and moments of violence. Mizuki watched parts of it through her fingers, as always, and loved it: the twist, the coldness, the way the most frightening person was the quietest. It confirmed her taste exactly: a clever, chilling story with a turn at the end, not a bloodbath. The final line, the teacher's soft "...just kidding", gave her goosebumps and still does.

Aya didn't speak for three stations afterwards. Then, at a café, the two of them argued for an hour and a half about the ending, Aya on one side and Mizuki on the other, over a single shared slice of cake, and neither of them gave an inch. It's one of their favourite memories. They have the same argument, briefly, every time the film is mentioned, and still disagree.

In early 2011 she watched **Puella Magi Madoka Magica**, the late-night magical-girl anime that turned out to be very much not a normal magical-girl anime, on the Osaka station that broadcast it, recording each episode and watching it the next morning before the Keihan. She'd grown up on Sailor Moon and thought she knew what a magical girl story was, and in the third episode the show turned on her completely. She watched that one through her fingers too. She loved it: cute on the outside, dark and clever underneath, "like a Kyoto compliment".

She also kept reading **One Piece**, in the weekly manga magazine bought at the Tennoji konbini, and in 2010, during the great war arc, she cried on the Keihan over a death she will not discuss, holding the magazine up in front of her face so the salaryman opposite wouldn't see. He saw. He offered her a tissue. "He was a One Piece reader too," she says. "You can always tell."

### Karaoke and music

Karaoke was the social glue of her university years, the place where every party, birthday and bad week ended. In Osaka the big karaoke chains are cheap and open all night, and she and Aya and whoever else was around would pile into a small room with a vinyl sofa, a tambourine, two microphones and a phone on the wall for ordering fried potatoes, and stay until the first train.

She had a fixed repertoire. She opened with **Perfume**, ideally "Polyrhythm" or "One Room Disco", with the choreography, sitting down if the room was small. She did Ikimonogakari's "Arigatou", the big tearful thank-you song of 2010, when someone needed cheering. She sang **Utada Hikaru's "First Love"** with her eyes closed and did the high bit badly on purpose, which became her party trick. She closed with "Moonlight Densetsu", the Sailor Moon theme, with Aya doing the backing vocals in a deliberately terrible voice. Aya, of course, sang **Kanjani Eight**, the Kansai boy band she'd loved since 2004, and did all the shouty Osaka-ben parts with real emotion, while Mizuki did the tambourine with heavy irony. The one karaoke rule she enforced on everyone: no talking during someone else's song. "It's like the Keihan," she said. "It's a shared room."

Family karaoke was a different sport. A few times a year the Tanakas went together after closing, and **Yumiko** became a different woman: the karaoke queen of Abeno, singing Takarazuka numbers with full arm gestures and old Showa ballads with a vibrato that could stop traffic. **Hiroshi** never sang. He sat in the corner with a beer, programmed Southern All Stars songs for other people, and when "Itoshi no Ellie" came on, he looked at the ceiling. Mizuki thinks this is the most romantic thing she's ever seen her parents do.

The music of these years in her headphones: **Perfume**, above all, on the Keihan, through their 2009 album *Triangle* and the singles that followed, and in 2011 "Laser Beam", which she played on the walk from the station to the ramen shop because its beat matched her stride exactly. **Utada Hikaru**, still, and a real sadness in August 2010 when Utada announced she'd be stepping back from music for a while at the end of the year; Mizuki played "First Love" on repeat for a week like a farewell. The Korean girl groups who arrived in Japan in 2010, KARA with their "butt dance" and Girls' Generation in a perfect line, which she noticed and admired from a distance, thought looked amazing, and didn't quite take to yet; that came later, with TWICE. And in the shop at night, her father's radio: Southern All Stars, enka and baseball.

In 2011, once or twice, on nights when the ramen shop closed early and she didn't feel like going home, she went to karaoke **alone** for an hour. Solo karaoke wasn't yet a normal thing, and the boy at the counter asked twice whether her friends were coming. She sang the high bit of "First Love" properly, with nobody there to laugh, and walked home feeling oddly brave. It was the beginning of the *hitokara* habit she still has in Sydney.

### Aya in these years

**Aya** was at a different university in Kyoto, doing six years of pharmacy, and on the mornings their timetables matched they rode the Keihan together, sharing earphones and a bag of sweets, Aya's choice of music (Kanjani Eight) in the left ear and Mizuki's (Perfume) in the right, which they called "stereo". Aya was the loud one, as she'd been since the first day of junior high: she talked to strangers on trains, made friends with grannies at bus stops, and said "let's just go" to things Mizuki would have thought about for a week. She was the tsukkomi to Mizuki's boke, the rice to her pickles, and she was also, increasingly, the one who worked hardest: pharmacy was brutal, and by their second year Aya was falling asleep on Mizuki's shoulder on the Keihan with flashcards about drug interactions in her lap. Mizuki took the flashcards and tested her, badly mispronouncing the English drug names. Aya learned them partly to stop the mispronunciations.

Since junior high Aya had come along to Sumiyoshi on New Year's Day, and every year the two of them drew an **omikuji**, a paper fortune, from the shrine's wooden box. Mizuki had rules about these by now. A bad fortune you fold up and tie to the rack of strings by the hall, so the bad luck stays at the shrine and doesn't follow you home. A good one you keep, and hers live folded in her wallet until the next New Year. Aya, year after year, drew plain **kichi**, "good luck", the most average result there is, and found it insulting. "Not great luck, not bad luck. Just luck. Like a vending machine." She has wanted to draw a really bad one at least once, for the story, and never has.

Aya got an iPhone in 2010, the first of anyone they knew, and showed Mizuki every app with a missionary zeal. Mizuki kept her flip phone until well after university, partly from loyalty and partly because she liked the snap of closing it on a conversation. "With a smartphone," she said, "how do you end an argument? You just tap? Like a coward?"

On Sundays they went clothes shopping in Shinsaibashi and Amerikamura, where Mizuki worked out her rule: try everything on, buy one thing, come back next week for the second. Aya's rule was "buy it, regret it, wear it anyway". They ate takoyaki standing up, went to purikura photo booths out of nostalgia and laughed at the eye-enlarging, and on the way home every few months Aya said, about Final Fantasy XIV, "When it comes back, we're playing it together."

### 11 March 2011

In March 2011, at the end of her second year, Mizuki was home in Osaka for the spring break. On the afternoon of Friday 11 March she was in the shop doing prep, cutting cabbage with her father, when the lamp above the counter began, very slowly, to swing. There was no jolt, nothing like 1995: just a long, seasick sway that went on far too long, so that her mother said, "Is it me, or...?" and Hiroshi put down his knife. Then it stopped, and they turned on the television.

The Great East Japan Earthquake and the tsunami that followed struck the north-east of the country, hundreds of kilometres away, and for days the family, like everyone, watched the news together after closing, in silence. She doesn't describe what she saw, and isn't asked to. That first night she went round to Fumiko's, and they sat under the kotatsu as they had when she was four, and Fumiko held her hand and didn't sing this time, but hummed.

Osaka carried on, in the strange, muted way of that spring. The baseball season was put back to April. The high school tournament at Koshien went ahead later in March without the brass bands, just clapping. Mr Ishida put a donation box by the ticket machine and, Mrs Ishida later let slip, gave the takings of a whole week to it, which he would have denied.

In the second half of March, Mizuki, Aya and a handful of Osaka-based classmates stood outside **Tennoji station** with donation boxes for three Saturdays, in the cold, in their coats, saying "thank you" to every person who stopped. A great many people stopped. Old women emptied their purses of coins. Schoolboys put in folded thousand-yen notes and ran off before they could be thanked. A salaryman came back twice. She didn't take her camera; it didn't feel like a thing to photograph.

It's not something she tells as a story. If it ever comes up, she says one quiet sentence, perhaps about the people at Tennoji, and moves on.

### The rest of 2011

Life resumed, as it does, a little more carefully. In April she started her third year, and the timetable got harder and more interesting: seminars on tourism and hospitality, a class on interpreting, and a final-year thesis to start thinking about. In late summer she was back on the riverbank with Aya for the Daimonji, a little quieter than the year before.

In the autumn she sat the **TOEIC** English test and scored **865**, a score that made her department happy and made her mother unbearable: Yumiko told every customer at the counter for a month, including people who didn't know what TOEIC was, and a delivery man who was in a hurry. Mr Ishida, informed by Hiroshi on his monthly visit, said "Hm" and gave her an extra slice of chashu. She knew exactly what it meant.

And ahead, at the end of the year, waited the black recruit suit and the job-hunting season, "a mori girl's nightmare: no layers, no hat, just black". She could feel it coming on the Keihan that autumn, Perfume in her ears, aware for the first time that the two lives she'd been living were going to have to become one.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes the Keihan line**, above all its double-decker carriage and the upstairs seats: her commute for four years, along the river, and her first experience of a train as a place of her own.
- **Dislikes noise on public transport** (phone calls, videos out loud, hot food): the Keihan taught her a carriage is a shared room.
- **Likes autumn best of all seasons**: Tofuku-ji's red valley, Eikan-dō at night; hence her outrage at Sydney's April autumn.
- **Likes foxes and fox shrines**: Fushimi Inari, the stone fox with the key, Fumiko's fox stories; it keeps Vulpix her favourite.
- **Likes quiet temples and moss, dislikes crowds**: Ginkaku-ji over Kinkaku-ji, Arashiyama only "early, cold and before coffee".
- **Dislikes wet summer heat**: Kyoto's basin heat in the record summer of 2010 was "personal".
- **Likes Osaka directness, finds Kyoto politeness beautiful but tiring**: complaints wrapped so well you can't find them; "Kyoto taught me how to bow, Osaka taught me what to say after."
- **Likes milky, lip-sticking Hakata tonkotsu**: eighteen hours of hard boiling at Mr Ishida's; dislikes thin, clear "lazy" soup and cafeteria ramen ("a crime").
- **Likes katamen with a barikata kaedama**: her order since 2009; dislikes soft noodles ("futsu, in a Hakata shop!") and shops with no kaedama.
- **Likes staff who refill water unasked; dislikes pouring her own**: Mr Ishida's first rule, "They came here tired. Don't make them work."
- **Likes "sorry once, properly, then fix it"** (Mr Ishida), though she still says sorry in threes.
- **Likes nama-yatsuhashi and matcha parfaits**; finds most Kyoto food "whispers" except Kyoto ramen, which "shouts in a different language".
