## Chapter 1: Before her, and the first three years (1964 to 1993)

### 1964: Isamu lights the hotplate

The shop is older than Mizuki's parents' marriage and only six years younger than her father. It opened in the autumn of 1964, the year of the Tokyo Olympics, the year the first bullet trains began running between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka, and the year the whole country seemed to stand up straight and decide it was modern now.

Isamu Tanaka was thirty-two. He had been born in Osaka in 1932 and was a boy of twelve in 1945, when much of the city burned. He almost never spoke about that year. If anyone asked, he said, "I was hungry," and changed the subject to cabbage, and the family learned not to ask. What he did talk about was the little street stalls after the war that sold "issen yoshoku", one-sen Western food: a thin wheat batter cooked on a griddle with a few spring onions and whatever else there was, folded and brushed with sauce. It was cheap, hot and filling, and to a hungry boy it smelled like the future. Okonomiyaki, "grilled as you like it", grew out of those stalls, and Isamu grew up alongside it.

Through his late teens and twenties he worked wherever there was a hotplate, washing dishes and then cooking at a busy okonomiyaki place in the south of the city with a queue of factory workers at midnight. An old cook there taught him the rules he would teach his son, and his son would one day try to teach Mizuki: shred the cabbage fine but not too fine; don't overmix the batter or it turns to glue; never press an okonomiyaki down with the spatula, because "you're squeezing the air out of somebody's dinner"; and turn it once, with confidence, as if you've never failed in your life.

By 1964 he had married Chiyo, had a six-year-old son called Hiroshi, and had saved, borrowed and argued his way to a narrow shopfront on a back street in Abeno, a few minutes' walk from Tennoji station. It had been a tobacconist's. It had two rooms upstairs, steep stairs and a drain that made a noise like a frog. Isamu put in a long black iron hotplate, a plain wooden counter with eight stools and two little tables by the window, and hung an indigo noren over the door with "okonomiyaki" written across it in white. A small wooden sign by the door said, simply, "Tanaka". He never gave the place a grander name. "If the food is good, people remember the street," he said. "If it's bad, a nice name won't save you."

The shop opened in early October 1964, a few days before the Olympic opening ceremony. Isamu always said he timed it on purpose, so every customer would be in a good mood. Chiyo always said the builder was late. Both stories were told at every family gathering for forty years, and both were probably true.

That October a second-hand television on a shelf in the corner, slightly green, showed the Games all day. The great night came on 23 October, when the Japanese women's volleyball team beat the Soviet Union for gold. Most of that team played for a textile company's side from Kaizuka, just south of Osaka, and the papers called them the "Oriental Witches". The shop was so full that two customers ate standing in the doorway, and when the final point went in Isamu banged two spatulas together like cymbals and gave everyone a free beer, which Chiyo then quietly added to the next week's bills. "That," Mizuki says, "is my whole family in one evening."

To mark the opening Isamu bought himself a plain steel Seiko with a cream face. Seiko was the official timekeeper of the Tokyo Games, and he liked the idea that the company timing the fastest men in the world was now timing his okonomiyaki. He wore it every day for forty-one years, thirty of them at the hotplate, wiping batter off the glass with his thumb. It is the watch Mizuki wears now, her "old man's watch", and when she glances at it in Sydney she is, in a way, checking the time in 1964.

### Isamu and Chiyo

Isamu was loud. This surprises people who know Hiroshi, but Mizuki's father got his hands from Isamu and his silence from somewhere else entirely. Isamu talked to the customers, the cabbage, the radio, the television and the pigeons on the step. He sang while he cooked, mostly old Osaka comic songs with the words wrong, and he had a big laugh that went up at the end like a question. Behind the hotplate he stood like a conductor, and when he flipped two okonomiyaki at once, one with each pair of spatulas, regulars clapped.

He had sayings. "Cabbage is the honest part" (you could tell everything about a cook from his cabbage). "A customer who complains is a customer who came back." And "mada mada", "not yet, not yet", which he said about the Tigers, about his son's flipping and, later, about Mizuki's handwriting, and which Hiroshi inherited whole. He was generous to a fault: children got extra katsuobushi "for the dancing", and anyone who looked as if they'd had a bad day got their beer topped up.

Chiyo made the generosity possible. She was born in 1935 in Kishiwada, the tough little castle town south of Osaka famous for its danjiri festival, when huge carved wooden floats are hauled at a run through the streets every September and swung around corners by shouting teams of men. Chiyo said anyone who had grown up watching a danjiri take a corner was not frightened of anything, including Isamu. She was small, neat and sharp-eyed, and could add up a table's bill in her head faster than anyone could write it down. She kept the accounts in pencil in a cloth-bound ledger, in tiny upright handwriting, and knew what a cabbage had cost in every month since 1964. Every morning she shredded the cabbage, a mountain of it, with a long knife and a rhythm like a train. She ran the till for twenty-three years and never once came up short.

Where Isamu gave away beer, Chiyo kept a note of it. Where Isamu said "pay me next week", Chiyo made sure next week came. The regulars were a little afraid of her and liked her more for it. When the oil shock hit in the autumn of 1973 and the great toilet paper panic began (it started, as Osaka people don't love to admit, at a supermarket in Senri New Town on the city's northern edge), Chiyo didn't queue. She had bought two years' supply the previous spring because it was on special. Isamu told this story for the rest of his life as proof he had married a prophet.

He called her "the Minister of Finance". She called him "that man", in the tone Osaka wives use for husbands they wouldn't swap. In 2026 Chiyo is in her nineties, in a care home in Abeno, a little forgetful about what day it is and still entirely capable of telling Mizuki her flight home cost too much.

### Abeno and Tennoji in those days

Abeno sits on the old raised ground of the Uemachi plateau in the south of central Osaka, and it has always been a crossroads. In the 1960s Tennoji station and, beside it, the Kintetsu company's Abenobashi terminal sent trains everywhere: the loop line and the lines south to Wakayama and east to Nara, the subway's Midosuji line north to Namba and Umeda, and, rattling down the middle of the road, the little Hankai trams to Sumiyoshi and Sakai. Above the Abenobashi station stood the Kintetsu department store, where Abeno mothers bought school uniforms, New Year gifts and good towels.

North of the station lay Tennoji Park with its pond, zoo and art museum, and just beyond it Shinsekai, the "New World", built for an exhibition in 1903 and going cheerfully downhill ever since, with its tower, Tsutenkaku, rebuilt in 1956, its kushikatsu counters, shogi parlours and old men in caps with opinions about everything. East was Shitennoji, the great temple founded by Prince Shotoku in 593, with its flea market on the 21st of every month. South, down the tram line, was Sumiyoshi Taisha, the city's grandest shrine.

The streets around the shop were a warren of two-storey wooden houses, tiny bars, a rice shop, a public bath, a tatami maker, a tofu shop that opened before dawn and a little corner shrine with a stone fox in a red bib. Some of the old tangle south of the station was later cleared for a huge redevelopment, and Isamu watched whole blocks vanish and complained about it at the hotplate: "They're building a city for people who don't eat." The shop's own street survived, still so narrow that two bicycles passing have to negotiate.

The customers were the neighbourhood: workers from small factories, tram crews coming off shift, department-store assistants, students, taxi drivers, families on Sundays. Some of the children who sat on the stools in 1964 brought their own children in 1990 and their grandchildren after that. That sense of three generations eating on the same eight stools is a large part of what Mizuki means when she says "home".

### The shop grows up

The 1960s and 70s were good years for a small Osaka business. People were earning a bit more each year, and an okonomiyaki and a beer after work was a small luxury they allowed themselves. Negiyaki, the spring-onion version with no cabbage, went on the menu in the late 1960s at a regular's request and never came off. Yakisoba arrived. A squid version appeared, and then the mixed one, with pork, squid and prawn, which Isamu called "the deluxe" with a straight face although it cost very little more.

In 1970 the world came to Osaka. The World Exposition opened in the Senri hills in March, under Taro Okamoto's Tower of the Sun. Isamu took Chiyo and twelve-year-old Hiroshi on a Wednesday, the shop's day off, and they queued for hours to see the moon rock in the American pavilion. Hiroshi, tired, hungry and twelve, said afterwards that it looked like a rock, and Isamu laughed about it for a decade. What Hiroshi actually remembered, he told Mizuki years later, was the Tower of the Sun's faces looking out over the crowds. Even then he noticed the small things.

The shop also got its sauce. Isamu bought a sweet-sour sauce from a Kansai maker in big tins and "corrected" it in a battered aluminium pot at the back. Nobody outside the family knows what goes in, and Mizuki, who knows, won't tell even Scott. "That's not a secret," she says. "That's an inheritance."

The mayonnaise squiggle came later and caused the first great family argument. Isamu thought it was a fad; Hiroshi, by then in his twenties and working full-time, thought customers wanted it. For about a month in the early 1980s the shop served mayonnaise only on request, with Isamu sighing every time he squeezed the bottle. Then Chiyo looked at the ledger, saw that the mayonnaise customers came back more often, and the matter was settled by the Minister of Finance. Isamu called it "Hiroshi's white paint" for the rest of his life, and put it on everything.

### Hiroshi: the quiet boy at the hotplate

Hiroshi Tanaka was born on 3 August 1958 in Abeno, Isamu and Chiyo's only child, and grew up in the two rooms above the shop, falling asleep to the same hiss of batter his daughter would. He was a quiet boy. School reports said "conscientious", "careful" and "could speak up more". He liked trains, baseball cards, fishing with his father off the breakwaters on rare free days, and drawing, carefully, things he had looked at for a long time: a bicycle, a bowl, a cat on a wall. At about ten Isamu stood him on a beer crate at the end of the hotplate to make yakisoba for the staff lunch. By fifteen he was flipping okonomiyaki for customers on busy nights, and after high school he worked at the shop full-time. When Mizuki asked once if he'd wanted to be anything else, he thought for a long time and said, "A Tigers player." Then, after another pause: "But they had enough bad ones."

Isamu supported the Nankai Hawks, the Pacific League team whose ground was in Namba, but Hiroshi was a Hanshin Tigers boy from the start, like most of Kansai. The Tigers play at Koshien, the old ivy-covered stadium between Osaka and Kobe, and as a teenager Hiroshi sat in the right-field stands, where the noisiest fans are, and discovered that a quiet person can shout very loudly if fifty thousand other people are doing it.

In 1982, aged twenty-four, when Isamu at last began paying him a proper wage, Hiroshi took his first full pay packet to a camera shop in Namba and bought a Nikon FM2, new that year: a sturdy, all-mechanical camera in silver and black that works with no battery at all. It cost him most of the month. Chiyo was appalled; Isamu was secretly impressed. Hiroshi photographed everything he loved, slowly: the noren at dusk, his father's hands on the spatulas, regulars at the counter, Koshien, the fishing rocks in Wakayama, tram lines shining in the rain. He had his rolls printed at the camera shop and kept the prints in shoeboxes, each dated in pencil on the back. Those shoeboxes are, to Mizuki, the most valuable things in the building. They are where she first saw Isamu young, Chiyo laughing, and her own mother at twenty-four looking straight at the lens as if she had a few things to say about it.

The shop's radio-cassette player gave Hiroshi his music: Isamu's old enka, slow and heartbroken, all harbours and sake, and then, from about twenty, his own great love, Southern All Stars, the shaggy, funny, sentimental band from the beach at Chigasaki, who sounded to a young Osaka cook like summer itself. Ask him which is better, enka or Southern, and he says, "Yes."

### Yumiko: the tram driver's daughter

Yumiko Morita was born on 21 January 1961, also in Abeno, about ten minutes' walk from the shop, in the old wooden two-room house that would become Mizuki's second home. She was the only daughter of Fumiko and Shigeru Morita, a late and very welcome baby; Fumiko was thirty-three and had almost given up hope.

Shigeru, born in 1924, drove trams on the Hankai lines, the little streetcars that run from Tennoji-ekimae in Abeno south through Sumiyoshi into Sakai. For most of his years the trams belonged to the Nankai railway; in 1980 they were handed to a company of their own, and Shigeru retired around then. He was a tall, gentle man with big ears and a cap worn at a slight tilt, and according to Fumiko he whistled at every stop: a short, cheerful two-note whistle, as if telling the stop he'd arrived. Passengers who rode with him every day whistled back. On Sunday mornings little Yumiko rode in the front of his tram on a cushion by the door, watching the rails, and she has loved trams ever since. If a Hankai tram goes by, she stops talking for a second, which in Yumiko is a remarkable event.

Fumiko, born in 1927, kept the house, sewed for neighbours, grew morning glories up strings in the tiny yard and told stories, handing out yokai tales and old Osaka gossip a little at a time, like sweets. Yumiko loved her mother dearly and argued with her constantly, which in that family was the same thing.

Yumiko was not a quiet child. She talked early and fast, answered the teacher's question before it was finished and corrected it if it was wrong. Watching the Namba comedy shows on Saturday afternoons she decided that in any double act she would be the tsukkomi, the one who corrects the fool. "Somebody," she said, "has to keep order." At about fourteen she discovered the Takarazuka Revue, the all-women musical theatre company in the hills towards Kobe, where women play all the parts, including the dashing men. *The Rose of Versailles* had opened there in 1974 and was a sensation; a school friend's aunt had a spare ticket, and Yumiko came home changed. She pinned photos of the top stars inside her desk and sang the songs in the bath. That love never faded, and it's why she would take Mizuki to her first show at the Grand Theatre in 1999.

After high school she got a job at the Kintetsu department store at Abenobashi, soon on the gift counter, wrapping New Year and midsummer presents with that fast, crisp department-store fold and talking customers up from the cheap towels to the good ones. She learned there what she would bring to the shop: remember a customer's face, then their name, then their children's names, and they're yours for life. On paydays the staff went to karaoke, still a machine in the corner of a snack bar with the lyrics in a printed book, and Yumiko turned out to be very good: loud, unembarrassed, with a vibrato she could switch on like a light. Enka for the older men, Takarazuka for herself, pop hits for everyone else. Her title of "karaoke queen" starts there.

### 1985: the Tigers year, and a burnt okonomiyaki

Hiroshi and Yumiko had grown up ten minutes apart and must have passed each other a thousand times, but the family version of how they met begins in the spring of 1985. Yumiko, twenty-four, came into the shop one evening with three women from the department store and sat at the counter. Hiroshi, twenty-six, was at the hotplate. According to Yumiko, he looked at her once, looked at the hotplate, looked at her again, and then, for the first and only time in his professional life, burnt an okonomiyaki. Black on one side. He turned it over, looked at it, and slid it in front of her.

"Is this a joke?" she said.

"It's well done," said Hiroshi.

That was the first exchange of their manzai act. The regulars laughed, and so did Yumiko. Isamu made his son do another, which was perfect. She came back the next week with her friends, and then on her own. She said later that what she liked about him was that "he said three words an hour, and two of them were funny".

It helped that 1985 was the Tigers year. The Hanshin Tigers, who hadn't won a pennant since 1964, suddenly could not stop hitting home runs. In April, Randy Bass, Masayuki Kakefu and Akinobu Okada hit three in a row against the Giants at Koshien, a moment every Tigers fan of that generation can describe, and from then on Kansai went slowly mad. Yumiko didn't care about baseball, but she learned the players' names to tease Hiroshi, and by summer she knew the batting order better than he did. He found this annoying and, he admitted much later, it was the moment he knew.

On 16 October the Tigers clinched the Central League pennant. That night thousands of fans gathered at the Ebisu bridge on the Dotonbori canal and jumped into the filthy water, and somebody threw in the statue of Colonel Sanders from a nearby fried chicken shop, because fans said it looked like Bass. The Tigers won the Japan Series in early November, their first national championship, and then, as every Osaka child is taught, the curse began: the Colonel stayed at the bottom of the canal, and so, for most of two decades, did the Tigers.

Hiroshi was in Dotonbori that night with two friends and Yumiko. He swears he didn't touch the Colonel and didn't jump. Yumiko says he wanted to do both and she held his collar. There's a photograph from the FM2: neon, a crowd of soaked men in Tigers caps, and in the corner Yumiko, laughing, looking straight at the lens.

Their first proper date, on a Wednesday, was to the Expo Commemorative Park in Suita, the old 1970 Expo site, because Hiroshi wanted to show her the Tower of the Sun and tell her about the moon rock. She said it was the strangest first date of her life and that she'd enjoyed it enormously. He took one photograph of her under the tower. She told him to take another because her eyes were closed. He said, "That's the one."

### 8 November 1987

They married on Sunday 8 November 1987, in a Shinto ceremony at a wedding hall in Abeno, with the reception in its banquet room. Yumiko was twenty-six and Hiroshi twenty-nine. The photographs show Yumiko in a white wataboshi hood and kimono for the ceremony and then, for the reception, a pink Western gown with very large sleeves, because it was 1987 and sleeves were large, and a perm she now calls "a decision I made with my whole heart". Hiroshi wore a black morning suit and the expression of a man being photographed against his will. Isamu cried during the sake-sharing ceremony and denied it at the reception. Chiyo had negotiated the price of the reception down by an amount she would never disclose. Shigeru, sixty-three and proud, whistled his two-note whistle when the couple came in, and the whole room laughed.

Hiroshi's speech, which tradition says should thank everyone at length, was one sentence: "Thank you for coming, please eat a lot." The guests, being Osaka people, applauded wildly. Hiroshi's cousin, who had moved to Kobe for work, came with his wife and their two-year-old son Daisuke, who spent the reception under a table eating prawn crackers and who, seven years later, would carry a Super Famicom into Fumiko's house. At the after-party in a karaoke bar near Tennoji, Yumiko sang four songs, two of them twice, and Hiroshi, under extreme pressure, sang half a Southern All Stars song and handed the microphone to his father, who sang an old song with the wrong words.

The shop could spare them for three days, so instead of the fashionable honeymoon abroad they went to the hot springs at Arima, in the hills behind Kobe, ate too much, and Hiroshi went fishing on the way home.

Then came the rearrangement. Isamu and Chiyo moved into a small house three streets away, near the Hankai tram line, where Isamu took up bonsai so he would have something at home to argue with. The newlyweds moved in upstairs. Hiroshi, who had been his father's hired hand since high school, now stood beside him at the hotplate as a partner. Yumiko left the department store to run the front of the shop: the till, the drinks, the orders and the regulars. This was not entirely smooth. The till had been Chiyo's for twenty-three years, and in early 1988 Chiyo came in "just to help" most evenings and stood very close to it. There was a week in which the two women communicated about the change float entirely through Hiroshi, who did not enjoy it. Then Yumiko proposed a treaty: she would run the till, Chiyo would go on keeping the books in pencil, and the two of them would go through the figures together, line by line, over tea. Chiyo agreed. In the early 1990s she handed the daily figures over to Yumiko altogether, and from then on she came in every Sunday afternoon, in the quiet between lunch and dinner, to check the takings. The Sunday ledger meeting went on for years after Isamu died in 2005, and Yumiko says she learned more about money there than in her whole life before. Mizuki, later allowed to sit in with a colouring book, says it was where she first understood that her mother and grandmother were "two generals who'd decided to stop fighting and conquer something else instead".

### 1988: Shigeru

In the cold part of 1988 Shigeru Morita died, aged sixty-four, quietly and suddenly: he went to bed one night and did not wake up. Yumiko took it very hard. She had been married barely a year, and the shop couldn't close for long, so she worked the front through the weeks afterwards with a brightness the regulars saw straight through. Being Osaka people, they said very little and ordered a great deal, which was their way of helping. A few older ones who had ridden his tram whistled the two-note whistle to her softly when they came in. She said it undid her every time and got her through.

Fumiko, at sixty, did not move. She put Shigeru's photograph on the family altar, in his tram cap at its tilt, and talked to him every morning as she lit the incense, telling him the weather and the price of fish. She said she wasn't lonely, because he'd never been a great talker anyway and now at least he couldn't interrupt. Yumiko began calling in every day, and the short walk between the shop and the old house, past the tofu shop and the stone fox, became a path worn by the family's feet. Two years later it would be the most important road in a small girl's world.

Mizuki never met her grandfather. She knows him from the altar photograph, a few prints in her father's shoeboxes (Shigeru at the wedding, cheeks round, mid-whistle), his tram cap on its hook in Fumiko's house, and the stories. Whenever she rides a tram, in Melbourne or on the Hankai line on trips home, she whistles two notes, very quietly, at the first stop.

The same year the Hawks were sold and announced their move to Fukuoka. Isamu, who had lost his team and his son's father-in-law in one year, declared himself "retired from baseball" and was watching the Tigers with Hiroshi within a month.

### Osaka at the top of the bubble

The years between the wedding and Mizuki's birth were the height of the bubble economy. Land and share prices rose and rose; companies were flush; people talked about property the way they talked about the weather. In Osaka the bubble showed up as more company groups ordering a second round, more expensive bags on the coat hooks and cranes going up around Tennoji. On Friday and Saturday nights the shop was always full, Isamu and Hiroshi side by side at the hotplate, the old man talking and the young one silent, Yumiko running the floor.

On 7 January 1989 the Showa Emperor died and the era became Heisei. The television ran solemn programmes for days, the shop kept its radio low, and Isamu, who had been a boy in the war, sat on his stool that Wednesday and was quiet for a long afternoon. In June, Misora Hibari, the great singer of the postwar years and an idol to his generation, died, and for weeks the shop's cassette player played her last big song, "Kawa no Nagare no Yō ni" ("Like the Flow of the River"), until Yumiko threatened the cassette.

On 29 December 1989 the Nikkei share index hit its all-time peak. Nobody in the shop noticed. Within weeks the market began to fall, and over the next few years the bubble came quietly apart. But by then the Tanakas had something much more important to think about.

### 10 April 1990: a full moon over Abeno

Mizuki Tanaka was born in the evening of Tuesday 10 April 1990, in a small maternity clinic in Abeno, at the tail end of the cherry blossom. Yumiko had worked the lunch service the day before against everyone's advice, and claimed she went into labour because a customer asked for extra beni shoga and the sight of it upset her. Mizuki weighed just over three kilograms and, according to the nurse, "complained immediately". Yumiko said she was clearly an Osaka girl.

That night the moon was full. Hiroshi, who had spent the evening pacing the corridor and then the street outside, came back in and said, to everyone's surprise, a whole sentence: "The moon is out. It's full." They had argued about names for months. They named her Mizuki, written with the characters for "beautiful" and "moon". Yumiko's line, delivered at every birthday since, is that "the cherry blossoms waited for her, then fell off in relief, and the moon came out to have a look". It is also, Mizuki says, why "in the name of the moon, I'll punish you!" was always going to be her line. "I didn't choose Sailor Moon. Sailor Moon was assigned to me."

Three more pieces of birth legend are told at every opportunity. Hiroshi, after a long silence holding his daughter, asked the nurse whether babies could eat okonomiyaki yet (he says it was a joke; Yumiko says it was not). Isamu came straight from the hotplate in his white coat with a towel round his head, and a nurse tried to hand him a tray to take to the kitchen. And Chiyo brought a gift envelope and a bag of mikan, and asked how much the private room was.

Fumiko came last and stayed longest. She looked at the baby for a long time and said, "She has Shigeru's ears." Everyone agreed this was a compliment, though not everyone was sure.

### Satogaeri: the first month at Fumiko's

A new Japanese mother traditionally goes home to her own mother for the first weeks after a birth, a custom called satogaeri, "returning to the village". Yumiko's village was around the corner. So Mizuki spent the first month of her life in the old wooden house, among the morning glory seedlings and Shigeru's photograph. Fumiko made rice porridge and miso soup heavy with seaweed, supposedly good for nursing mothers, and wouldn't let Yumiko lift anything heavier than a teacup. Hiroshi ran over between services, stood in the doorway smelling of sauce, looked at his daughter for exactly as long as he had, and ran back.

Fumiko sang to the baby constantly in a small, slightly cracked voice: "Yūyake Koyake", about the evening bell and the crows going home, and a lullaby from her own childhood. Yumiko said the baby always slept better at Fumiko's than at home, and was offended by this for years. You can see the shape of something in that month: the old house as the place of calm, Fumiko as the one never in a hurry, singing as the thing that makes the dark all right. Under the kotatsu in the dark on 17 January 1995, the songs would be the same ones.

When the month was up Yumiko carried her home round the corner and up the steep stairs, and Mizuki slept her first night above the shop, to spatulas, batter and regulars laughing through the floor. She has needed noise to sleep ever since. "Quiet rooms," she says, "are suspicious. Something is planning."

### The first year: rituals

Japanese babies get a year of little ceremonies, and Mizuki had them all, fitted around an okonomiyaki shop.

**Omiyamairi**, the first shrine visit at about a month, was at Sumiyoshi Taisha on a Wednesday in May. They took the Hankai tram from Tennoji-ekimae, Shigeru's old line, with Fumiko holding the baby on the front seat by the door where Yumiko had sat as a girl. By custom the father's mother carries the baby at the shrine, so Chiyo did, in her best kimono. The baby screamed at the drum, and Isamu said proudly she had "good lungs for the shop". Hiroshi used a whole roll of film, including a shot of the steep red arched bridge that Mizuki would, a few years later, be terrified of.

**Okuizome**, the "first eating" at about a hundred days, came in July: a tray of celebration food, red-and-white rice, a whole sea bream and soup, offered to the baby in pretend, and a small stone touched to her gums for strong teeth. Isamu held the stone and made a speech about teeth in the food business. On the edge of the tray, specially made by Hiroshi, sat one tiny square of okonomiyaki that nobody let her eat. In every photograph from that day she is staring at it.

That summer, on another Wednesday, they went to the International Garden and Greenery Exposition, Expo '90, at Tsurumi Ryokuchi park in the east of the city (it ran from April to the end of September). Mizuki, four months old, slept through most of it in a sling on Hiroshi's chest in a white sunhat. Yumiko said it was flowers and queues; Hiroshi said it was a nice place for a walk; Fumiko said it was too hot, and it was: Osaka in August, thick as soup. It was the first of many summers in which Mizuki would be too hot in Osaka, and the start of her lifelong grudge against humidity.

On 20 July 1990 the Osaka Aquarium Kaiyukan had opened at Tempozan on the waterfront, with an enormous central tank and a whale shark. The family went the next spring, when Mizuki was nearly one and could point. She pointed at the whale shark all the way down the long spiralling walk around the tank, shouting one syllable at it, and refused to be carried away. Twenty-two years later, in front of the giant tank at Churaumi in Okinawa with Aya, she felt exactly the same thing, and her mother said on the phone, "You were like that when you were one."

**Hatsu-zekku**, a girl's first Doll Festival, was on 3 March 1991. The mother's side traditionally gives the hina dolls, and Fumiko gave a small glass case with just the emperor and empress in silk robes, because the rooms upstairs had no space for a seven-tier set. Fumiko said a pair was better anyway, "like a good manzai act". Yumiko put them away the moment the festival was over, because leaving them out is said to make a daughter marry late. Mizuki notes that her mother now leaves them up a little longer and claims she forgets.

### The first birthday: the spatula

Her first birthday, 10 April 1991, had two Kansai traditions. For **isshō mochi**, a one-year-old carries a big round rice cake of about two kilograms tied in a cloth on their back; "isshō" means both a measure of rice and a whole lifetime, so it's a lifetime of food on your shoulders. Mizuki took three proud steps across the shop floor, sat down hard, considered the situation and crawled off at speed towards the till, mochi and all. Isamu said this meant she'd be independent and good with money. Chiyo said it meant she wanted the money.

For **erabitori**, "choosing", a few objects are laid out and the one the baby grabs foretells her future. On the counter went a pencil, a hundred-yen coin, Chiyo's little abacus, a soft ball and, because Isamu insisted, a small spatula from the hotplate. Mizuki grabbed the spatula with both hands. Isamu shouted. Hiroshi said nothing but, according to Yumiko, made his first okonomiyaki that evening with "a very particular face". Then she dropped the spatula and grabbed the coin, and Chiyo nodded as if this confirmed everything.

Mizuki, now a shift lead at a Sydney ramen shop quietly saving for a market stall of her own where she could make Tanaka-style okonomiyaki for strangers, thinks the meaning was obvious all along. "Spatula first, then the money. That's the correct business plan."

Her first word, at around the same time, is also disputed. Yumiko heard "baa" and understood it as "obaachan", Fumiko, and was mildly hurt it wasn't "mama". Hiroshi, standing at the hotplate holding a bottle for a customer, maintains she was pointing at it and the word was "beer". The regulars were consulted. The regulars voted for beer.

### The shop and its regulars

By the time Mizuki could sit up, the shop had the shape she'd know all her childhood. Lunch from about half past eleven to two, then five until late, six days a week, closed Wednesdays. Eight stools, two tables, the long black hotplate (which Hiroshi seasoned with oil every night "the way other men polish cars"), the indigo noren, a beckoning cat in the window, the lucky bamboo from the Toka Ebisu festival above the till, a calendar from the rice shop and the television on its bracket in the corner, sound low. Wooden menu boards: pork, squid, mixed, negiyaki, modern-yaki (with yakisoba noodles inside, a concession to young people), yakisoba, beer, sake, oolong tea and orange juice for children. A plain pork butatama cost about six hundred yen. The prices were in Isamu's brush hand, and when they went up Hiroshi painted the new numbers over the old ones, slightly less well.

Isamu, nearing sixty, still worked most days, but the balance was shifting: Hiroshi did most of the cooking now and Isamu most of the talking, standing at the end with the customers, making the yakisoba and handing out opinions about the Tigers. Around 1994 he would hand the hotplate over to his son completely. Chiyo came in mornings for the cabbage and to keep the books. Yumiko ran the floor.

The regulars were Mizuki's first world. The taxi driver who always ordered negiyaki, always said there was too much spring onion and ordered it again the next week, for about twenty years; from when she was two he called her "the manager". The leopard-print auntie, a true Osaka obachan with purple-tinted hair, gold earrings, a different animal on her blouse from the one on her trousers and a handbag full of wrapped sweets for every child in range. Yumiko's old department-store friends, who came on payday and spoiled the baby. A group of salarymen from an insurance office near the station who came every Friday and sang "Rokko Oroshi", the Tigers' anthem, if the Tigers won, and more sadly if they lost.

And old Mr Hirano, who had sat on the first stool from the left every Saturday lunchtime since the week the shop opened. He was a retired Hankai tram conductor who had worked the line in Shigeru's years, and he called Yumiko "Morita's girl" to the end. He wore his hat indoors, ordered the plain pork with no mayonnaise "in protest", drank exactly one bottle of beer and read the racing pages. When Mizuki was big enough for the stool beside him he gave her a pencil and let her circle horses whose names she liked. He never bet on any of them, which, she says, was wise. He was the first to tell her about Shigeru's whistle, and he whistled it for her, badly, so she'd know how it went.

As a baby she slept in a basket on a high shelf by the till, away from the heat, where Yumiko could reach her. By about eighteen months she had her own stool at the very end of the counter, cushion on top and a rail behind so she couldn't fall, and that was her post. Her self-appointed job was to shout "irasshaimase!" at everyone who came through the noren, a beat too late and much too loudly. Newcomers jumped; regulars bowed to her. By three she'd picked up "ookini", the old Osaka thank-you hardly anyone her age used, from Mr Hirano and the older customers, and said it so gravely that grown men laughed into their beer.

A shop full of adults taught her things early. A counter is a small stage. People relax when you make them laugh. A grumpy customer is usually just hungry. Say the name of someone's child back to them and their whole face changes. And when the shop is busy you are not the main event, so the way to get a busy adult's attention is to be useful or funny, ideally both. She has been trying to be both ever since.

Upstairs were two small tatami rooms: one with the low table that became a kotatsu in winter, the television, a bookcase and the altar shelf; the other where they slept on futons folded away every morning. The family ate at about three in the afternoon, between services, downstairs at the counter with the noren taken in: rice, miso soup, pickles and whichever okonomiyaki had come out misshapen. From her very first solid food Mizuki preferred the broken ones. She still says they taste better, "because they've been through something".

### Fumiko's house

Because the shop was busiest in the evenings, from the time she could walk Mizuki spent a large part of her life at Fumiko's: two or three evenings a week, many Wednesdays and most Saturday mornings. It was ten minutes at a toddler's pace, past the tofu shop, the rice shop, a bicycle repairman who waved a spanner at her, and the corner shrine with the stone fox, which she was taught to bow to and bowed to so deeply she sometimes fell over.

The house had sliding doors that stuck in wet weather, a yard of morning glories, a kotatsu, the altar with Shigeru's photograph and his tram cap on a hook, and a smell of incense, green tea, dried shiitake and mothballs. Mizuki loved it all except the mothballs, which she hated from the start: "It smells like a ghost's wardrobe."

Fumiko was the only adult in her life who was never in a hurry. In a family that ran at restaurant speed, her house was where time slowed down. She let Mizuki help with things that took a long time and didn't matter: shelling broad beans, sorting buttons from a tin, watering the morning glories one cup at a time. At two and three origami mostly meant Mizuki crumpling coloured paper with great concentration while Fumiko smoothed it out and folded a crane from it as if by magic. The proper lessons, and the crane in the dark, came later.

At two and three Mizuki was too young for the truly scary stories, so Fumiko told the gentle ones: the tanuki drumming on his tummy in the moonlight, the kappa who loves cucumbers, the foxes who grow an extra tail every hundred years. And there was one she could point to. A short walk away in Abeno is Abe no Seimei Shrine, said to stand at the birthplace of Abe no Seimei, the famous court sorcerer of the Heian period, and an old legend says his mother was a white fox called Kuzunoha who had taken the form of a woman. Fumiko told it often: how the fox mother loved her little boy, and how, when she was found out and had to go back to the forest, she left him a poem. Mizuki didn't understand the poem but understood perfectly that a fox could be a mother and a mother could be a fox, and that this was very sad and also wonderful. It is, she thinks, the first story she ever loved, and why foxes, and later one small six-tailed fire fox on a Game Boy screen, have always felt like family.

Saturdays with Fumiko meant outings: the subway to Namba and the walk to Kuromon market, where a fishmonger handed her a grilled scallop on a stick and she decided, at about two and a half, that seafood was the finest thing in existence; and the Shitennoji flea market on the 21st, where a stallholder gave her a cracked teacup with a rabbit on it because she wouldn't stop looking at it.

### Toddler at the counter, 1991 to 1993

She walked at about thirteen months, along the bottom of the counter, holding on to the stools. After that she went everywhere, and one evening, nearly two, almost to the hotplate. She had seen her father touch it a thousand times. She reached up towards the edge, and Hiroshi, without turning round, caught her wrist an inch from the iron. The heat alone raised a tiny blister on one fingertip, and she cried as if the world had ended. Yumiko carried her upstairs, shaking. Hiroshi came up after the service and sat with her a long time, holding the blistered finger, saying nothing. She has treated hotplates with enormous respect ever since and is the person at any Sydney barbecue who will tell you, unprompted, exactly how far away to stand.

She talked early and a lot, in the soft, singsong dialect around her: "akan" for "no good" before she said "no", "honma?" for "really?", "meccha" for "very", a little "nen" at the end of sentences. She learned the old merchants' hello, "Mokari makka?" ("Making any money?") and its answer, "Bochi bochi denna" ("Oh, so-so"), and said the second to customers at two with a grave shake of the head, as if business genuinely worried her, which brought the house down every time.

More than the words, she picked up the rhythm. Her parents were a double act whether they meant to be or not: Hiroshi the deadpan boke saying absurd things in a flat voice ("The cabbage is looking at me"), Yumiko the tsukkomi catching every one, often with a rolled-up menu to the back of his head: "Nandeyanen!" ("What are you on about!"). By three Mizuki had the tsukkomi chop, the flick of the back of the hand, and used it on her father, the customers, the beckoning cat and Mr Hirano, who pretended to fall off his stool. She learned the most Osaka lesson of all, that a story needs an ochi, a punchline. If she ran in with a long tale about a pigeon, Yumiko waited until the end and asked, "And the ochi?" Mizuki would think hard, then do a pratfall. That counted.

The days had a pattern: mornings in the shop while Chiyo shredded cabbage (Mizuki "helped" by carrying outer leaves to the bin one at a time, which took all morning), lunch service on her stool, a nap upstairs, the three o'clock family meal, evening service partly on her stool and partly at Fumiko's. On Wednesdays, outings. Tennoji Zoo, where she loved the penguins, was suspicious of the elephants and, at two, burst into tears at the gorilla, who had looked at her "on purpose". The park pond, where Isamu taught her to clap so the carp came. Shinsekai, where Hiroshi lifted her up to rub the soles of Billiken's feet at Tsutenkaku, the grinning, pointy-headed god of things as they ought to be; she did it with both hands and such energy that the attendant laughed.

The year turned on festivals. Hatsumode at Sumiyoshi Taisha every New Year, on the Hankai tram, with Hiroshi carrying her over the steep red drum bridge on his shoulders while she clung to his head. Toka Ebisu at Imamiya Ebisu Shrine in January, where Yumiko bought the new lucky bamboo for above the till. Setsubun in February, Hiroshi in a cardboard demon mask falling over convincingly under a hail of roasted soybeans, and then the Osaka custom of eating a whole fat sushi roll in silence facing the year's lucky direction, which Mizuki, at three, could not manage for more than four seconds without giggling. And Obon in August at Fumiko's, with the small welcome fire at the door, a cucumber horse and an aubergine cow on chopstick legs, and Fumiko talking to Shigeru's photograph as if he'd just popped in. Mizuki was too young to understand it, but she absorbed the idea that the dead aren't far away, only quiet.

### Clothes, toys and what she ate

As a baby and toddler she wore mostly hand-me-downs, above all from an older girl two doors down, topped up by Yumiko's old colleagues' children, with someone else's name crossed out on every label. Yumiko's view was that babies grow out of clothes in a fortnight so clothes should be free. There was a yellow raincoat with a duck on it that she refused to take off in summer, and red shoes that squeaked with every step, which Hiroshi called "the alarm system". The one real dress, a frilly Mezzo Piano one from Chiyo, was still years away. For her third birthday, in April 1993, she got a new pair of red shoes, which squeaked even louder, and the photograph of her in them, scowling under a cherry tree because she'd been told to stand still, still hangs upstairs.

Her real fashion education came from the leopard-print auntie. Three patterns at once, gold at the ears and neck, a purple rinse, fierce lipstick and no apology: to toddler Mizuki she was the most glamorous person alive. At two Mizuki tried to put on all her clothes at once in tribute and came downstairs in a sunhat, the duck raincoat, a skirt over trousers and one sock on her hand. "What are you?" said her mother. "Auntie," she said. The auntie, at the counter, laughed until she cried and gave her an entire bag of sweets. "Osaka obachan fashion is not a mistake," Mizuki says now. "It's a decision." Yumiko, meanwhile, wore a perm, an apron, and on Wednesdays a jacket with shoulder pads big enough "to land a helicopter on", as Hiroshi once said and was hit with a menu. Hiroshi wore a white cook's coat and a twisted towel round his head six days a week, and a Tigers cap on the seventh.

Her toys were mostly not toys: spare chopsticks, empty sauce bottles, the order pad, plastic food Chiyo found at the flea market, and the beckoning cat (not allowed). She had a soft Anpanman doll from the negiyaki taxi driver that went everywhere with her, and a maneki-neko money box from Chiyo for the one-yen coins customers gave her, which Chiyo inspected on Sunday afternoons.

Her first solid food was rice porridge. Her second, according to family legend and against her mother's wishes, was a crumb of okonomiyaki from Isamu's finger, after which she opened her mouth for more like a baby bird and Isamu declared her "a Tanaka". By two and three she loved her father's pork and squid okonomiyaki cut into tiny squares with extra katsuobushi so she could watch it wriggle in the heat ("the dancing"; she believed for a while the flakes were alive and pleased to see her); takoyaki from the stall by the station, which she learned the hard way is lava inside; Fumiko's soft simmered daikon; grilled scallops; mikan by the dozen until her palms turned faintly orange; and custard pudding from the konbini, eaten with the concentration of a surgeon.

The enemies arrived early too. Beni shoga, the pink pickled ginger that goes into okonomiyaki, was hated from the first taste; she picked out every strand and lined them up on the edge of the plate, and Hiroshi, without a word, would add one more strand just to watch her face. Green peppers were unacceptable. Umeboshi made her whole face fold up like origami, and Fumiko, who loved them, ate them in front of her for the entertainment.

### Osaka in the early nineties: the bubble goes pop

The bubble burst slowly, then all at once. Shares fell through 1990, land prices followed in 1991, and by 1992 the papers were full of banks in trouble and companies cutting back. For a toddler this meant nothing. For the shop it meant quieter Tuesday nights, fewer company groups ordering third rounds, and more people choosing the plain pork over the deluxe.

Chiyo noticed first, of course, and at the ledger meetings she tapped her pencil on certain columns. In 1992 there was a tense month when Hiroshi wanted to put the butatama up by fifty yen and Isamu refused ("people are already sad"). Yumiko settled it by keeping the price and pouring the beer a little shorter, which nobody noticed except Chiyo, who approved. One of the Friday insurance men stopped coming for a few months; when he came back he came alone, ordered the cheapest thing on the menu and told Isamu he'd been let go. Isamu gave him a beer and didn't write it down, and that time Chiyo let it go.

Mizuki learned the words before she understood them: "fukeiki", hard times, sighed at the counter, and "baburu", the bubble, which she assumed was something to do with bath time. The lesson she absorbed was the Osaka one: things go badly, you complain loudly, you eat something good and you keep the shop open. "Mōkari makka?" "Akan, akan!" ("Making money?" "Not a bit!") became, in those years, a cheerful greeting with a real edge to it.

### The Tigers: mada mada

Mizuki was born into the long Tigers winter. In 1990 and 1991 the Hanshin Tigers finished last in the Central League. The corner television showed it all, and between okonomiyaki Hiroshi glanced up and said one word, usually "akan" or "mata ka" ("again?"), and Isamu said "mada mada". The regulars explained the Curse of the Colonel to the baby as if she'd joined a support group.

Then came 1992, and out of nowhere the Tigers were good. Two young players, the outfielder Tsuyoshi Shinjo and the speedy Tsutomu Kameyama, became overnight idols, and Kansai went into what the papers called "Kame-Shin fever". The Tigers fought the Yakult Swallows for the pennant deep into the autumn. The insurance men came on weeknights as well as Fridays. Hiroshi, normally a one-word-per-game man, said whole sentences. Mizuki, at two, learned to shout "Kameyama!" at the television, and regulars paid her in sweets to do it. Then the Tigers faded at the very end and finished second, two games behind. Hiroshi turned off the television, wiped the hotplate, said "mada mada" and went to bed. In 1993 they were fourth.

That autumn of 1992 is how Mizuki came to know "Rokko Oroshi" before most nursery songs: her father hummed it at the hotplate, the salarymen sang it badly after beer, and for weeks the whole shop sang it every night. She can still sing it to the end, and will, if anyone in Sydney mentions baseball. Years later her father put the lesson into words for her: "The Tigers teach you patience. And humility. And how to drink beer slowly."

### Manzai, music and television

Comedy in the Tanaka house was less entertainment than weather: always there, and you were expected to take part. On Saturday lunchtimes the shop's television showed Yoshimoto Shinkigeki, the slapstick theatre from Namba, in which the whole cast falls over together whenever someone says something stupid. Mizuki practised falling over in unison with nobody, which mostly meant falling off her cushion, and the regulars scored her falls out of ten. After closing, Yumiko watched the double act Downtown, from Amagasaki, whose sharp, strange, deadpan comedy had taken over national television by the early nineties. She said their boke reminded her of Hiroshi. Hiroshi watched in silence and once laughed, a single "ha", which Yumiko treated as a major event. Mizuki learned before she could read that the worst thing you can do in an Osaka conversation is leave a straight line hanging.

The soundtrack of her first three years was everybody else's. From the shop's cassette player came Isamu's enka and Hiroshi's Southern All Stars. In the summer she was a baby, the band's singer Keisuke Kuwata directed a film, *Inamura Jane*, and its ballad "Manatsu no Kajitsu" ("Fruit of Midsummer") was a huge hit; Hiroshi played it so often that Yumiko says it rocked Mizuki to sleep. In 1992 came "Namida no Kiss", everywhere that summer, which Mizuki at two sang as "Nami nami ki", to her father's quiet delight. The first bars of either still make her think of the hotplate in August and the noren hanging still in the heat.

From Yumiko came the karaoke hits of the early nineties, sung at volume while cleaning: Kazumasa Oda's "Love Story wa Totsuzen ni", the theme of the 1991 drama *Tokyo Love Story*; Chage and Aska's "Say Yes", both parts; and KAN's "Ai wa Katsu" ("Love Will Win"), which she declared the family's official song and sang at Hiroshi whenever the Tigers lost. On Wednesday afternoons out came the Takarazuka records, and Yumiko sang the man's part low and the woman's part high, alone in the upstairs room with her daughter as audience.

Mizuki's own music was children's music. The Anpanman march, from the cartoon about the hero whose head is made of bread and who lets hungry people eat it, on television since 1988; she thought him the most sensible superhero ever invented. And "Odoru Ponpokorin", the nonsense theme song of *Chibi Maruko-chan*, the gentle cartoon about a lazy, funny schoolgirl and her family that began in January 1990, three months before she was born, and was a national craze within the year. Mizuki danced to it on the counter (not allowed). Yumiko said Maruko's grandfather was "exactly like Isamu", which Isamu took as a compliment.

A neighbour lent the family a VHS tape of *My Neighbour Totoro*, Studio Ghibli's 1988 film about two sisters and a giant, soft forest spirit, and it was never returned. By three Mizuki had watched it perhaps a hundred times. She loved the catbus, was frightened of the soot sprites for a week and then wanted one, and marched everywhere singing the opening song, "Sanpo" ("Let's Go for a Walk"). It started her lifelong attachment to Ghibli songs; it's still what she hums walking home through Pyrmont on a nice evening.

In March 1992 a magical-girl series called *Sailor Moon* began on Saturday evenings, and in April *Crayon Shin-chan* arrived, about a five-year-old who says rude things and dances with his bottom out. At two she was too young for either, and Yumiko disapproved of the second on principle. But the posters were everywhere, at the konbini, the station and on buses, and the moon tiara and the girls in sailor uniforms lodged somewhere in her mind. It would be a couple more years before she found the clever blue-haired one, but the moon, as she says, was already assigned.

### Small troubles

Not everything was sweet, though most of what went wrong was small. There was the hotplate blister. There was a high fever in her second winter, the ordinary kind babies get that frightens parents half to death, when Yumiko sat up with her for three nights and Hiroshi, running the shop alone, burnt two okonomiyaki in one evening, a family record. There was hand, foot and mouth disease at two and a half, caught from a regular's grandson, which covered her in red spots and made her look, Isamu said, "like a mixed okonomiyaki". There was the afternoon she got lost for ten minutes in the Kintetsu department store, having followed a woman in leopard print she believed was the auntie, and was found by a floor assistant who used to work with Yumiko and who announced her name over the loudspeaker, which Mizuki found thrilling and her mother found mortifying.

And there was the Anpanman doll. In the autumn of 1992, on the Hankai tram home from Sumiyoshi, she fell asleep on Fumiko's lap and the doll slipped down between the seat and the wall. Nobody noticed until they were home. Yumiko phoned the tram company, and Fumiko walked to the depot herself, but nobody had handed it in. Mizuki was inconsolable for most of a week, until Fumiko sat her under the kotatsu and explained that the doll had chosen to stay on the tram, because Shigeru used to drive that line, and now somebody from the family was riding with the passengers again. Mizuki accepted this completely. She still half believes it, and when she rides the Hankai tram on visits home, she glances every time at the gap between the seat and the wall.

She also had her first taste of not being the most important person in the room, when the taxi driver's grandson, a large and confident one-year-old, was passed round the counter one Sunday and admired. Mizuki watched from her stool with mounting outrage, then climbed down, walked over and announced loudly that she could say "ookini". It was, in hindsight, a rehearsal.

### 1993: the cold summer

In the spring of 1993 Yumiko told the family she was expecting again, due in late November. Mizuki, just three, was told she'd be an onee-chan, a big sister. She asked whether the baby would be able to shout "irasshaimase", and on being told no, not for a while, lost interest. Over the following months she talked to the bump in long, bossy speeches about the shop and its rules, and allowed it, in theory, to share her Totoro tape.

The grown-ups had plenty to talk about. In May the new professional football league, the J.League, kicked off, with Osaka's own Gamba Osaka among its teams, and for a while football was on everything. Hiroshi watched one match on the shop television with polite interest and turned back to the Tigers, and that was the end of football in the Tanaka household. On 9 June, a public holiday and the shop's Wednesday off anyway, the Crown Prince married Masako Owada, and Yumiko opened the shop for an hour so the regulars could watch the parade together on the corner television. Mizuki watched from the counter with a rice cracker, asked why the princess wasn't wearing a crown, and was told it wasn't that kind of princess, which she considered a swindle.

Then summer didn't come. 1993 was one of the coldest, wettest summers anyone remembered, grey and raining through July and August, the rice failing in the fields up north; by the next year the country would be short of rice and grumbling at imported grain. In the shop it meant shocking cabbage prices (Chiyo tapped the ledger so hard her pencil broke) and, for once, no complaints from Mizuki about the heat. She remembers it vaguely and fondly as "the summer the weather forgot to be rude".

Yumiko worked the front until October, perched on a high stool behind the till, heavily pregnant; customers who stood up to pay found her already holding their change. Chiyo began coming in the evenings again, and this time, without any treaty, the two of them worked the till like two halves of one machine. Mizuki spent much of that autumn at Fumiko's, folding paper badly, shelling beans slowly, and hearing gentle yokai stories under a kotatsu that went on early that year because of the cold.

### Shichi-go-san, with a bump

In November Japanese families take girls of three and seven and boys of five to a shrine for Shichi-go-san, "seven-five-three", to give thanks for their health. Mizuki was three, and although the baby was due any day, Yumiko refused to miss it. So on Wednesday 10 November 1993 the whole family took the Hankai tram down to Sumiyoshi Taisha: Isamu in his good jacket, Chiyo and Fumiko in kimono, Hiroshi with the FM2, Yumiko enormous in a loose dress and flat shoes, and Mizuki in a little red kimono patterned with cranes and chrysanthemums, altered by Fumiko from one of Yumiko's own childhood kimonos, with a flower pin in her hair that she kept touching to check it was still there. She was given a long paper bag of chitose-ame, the thin red-and-white "thousand-year" sweets for a long life, and tried to eat one immediately, in full kimono, which Fumiko stopped with lightning reflexes.

At the drum bridge she was terrified as always and rode over on her father's shoulders. At the main hall she bowed very deeply, clapped twice and hid behind Fumiko when the drum went. Afterwards, under a gingko tree turning yellow, Hiroshi took the photograph that hangs upstairs in the shop and that Mizuki has a copy of in Pyrmont: four adults and a small girl in red, Yumiko's hand on her bump, Isamu grinning, Chiyo upright, Fumiko half-smiling, and Mizuki in the middle, sweet-bag in hand, tremendously serious. It's the last family photograph before Kenta and, Mizuki likes to point out, technically the first photograph of her brother.

On the tram home Yumiko said, "Ah." Everyone looked at her. "No, nothing. He kicked." Hiroshi had to sit down.

### 22 November 1993: Kenta

Twelve days later, early on Monday 22 November 1993, in the same Abeno clinic, Yumiko gave birth to a boy, so quickly that Hiroshi, who had popped home to see to the stock pot, missed it, and has never entirely recovered. They named him Kenta.

Mizuki was at Fumiko's and heard over breakfast. She asked whether he had arrived with teeth. Fumiko said no. She asked whether he could say "ookini". Fumiko said no. "Then what is he for?" Fumiko laughed so much she had to put her tea down.

That afternoon she was lifted up to look into the little cot. Kenta was red and squashed and asleep, and smelled, she reported later, of milk and "nothing". Then he woke up and cried. The nurses cooed. Chiyo asked how much the private room was. Isamu, in his cook's coat again, said, "A boy for the hotplate!" and Hiroshi, very quietly, said, "Or a Tigers player." Fumiko said he had Shigeru's ears too, which made it two for two. And Mizuki, three years and seven months old, owner of a stool, a maneki-neko money box and a lifelong grudge against pickled ginger, stood beside the cot with her hands behind her back, considered her brother at length, and announced to the room:

"He's not very good."

Yumiko laughed so hard the nurse came back to check on her. Hiroshi took a photograph. Mizuki did not look at the camera.

She was not impressed at first. He cried, he slept in the middle of the day, and once he came home the regulars asked about him instead of her. But within a few weeks, the family swears, she was found at the end of the counter with the baby in his basket beside her stool, explaining in a long, bossy speech how the shop worked, who sat where, which regulars gave sweets, and that pickled ginger was the enemy. He gazed at her and dribbled. She took this as agreement. She had found an audience that couldn't leave, and she has been his onee-chan ever since.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:** the smell of okonomiyaki sauce, cabbage and katsuobushi together ("home" and "safe"); katsuobushi "dancing" (she thought it was alive and pleased to see her); broken okonomiyaki ("they've been through something"); noise to sleep to (the shop through the floor; silence is "suspicious"); the full moon (her name, "beautiful moon", and the night she was born); being useful and funny at a counter (her stool at eighteen months); "ookini" and "bochi bochi denna" (from Mr Hirano and the regulars; they made grown men laugh); the leopard-print auntie's style ("a decision"); whale sharks (pointing at the Kaiyukan tank at nearly one); foxes, above all Kuzunoha, Abe no Seimei's fox mother (Fumiko's first story); trams and the two-note whistle (Shigeru's line); Fumiko's slow house; grilled scallops at Kuromon; takoyaki, mikan, custard pudding; rubbing Billiken's feet (her father lifting her up); the Tigers and "Rokko Oroshi" (the Kame-Shin autumn of 1992); Southern All Stars' "Manatsu no Kajitsu" and "Namida no Kiss" (her father humming at the hotplate); *My Neighbour Totoro* and "Sanpo"; "Odoru Ponpokorin"; Anpanman (the sensible superhero: he feeds people); Shinkigeki falls and stories with an ochi (her mother's question).
- **Dislikes:** beni shoga (picked out into a pink pile from the first taste); green peppers; umeboshi (her face "folds up like origami"); mothballs ("a ghost's wardrobe"); the hotplate's edge (one blister, lifelong respect); Osaka humidity (Expo '90 in August; the cold 1993 summer was the only one she liked); the steep drum bridge at Sumiyoshi Taisha; the Tennoji Zoo gorilla (he looked at her "on purpose"); losing things on trams (the Anpanman doll); not being the centre of attention (the taxi driver's grandson, then Kenta); princesses without crowns ("a swindle").

### People in these years

- **Isamu Tanaka** — paternal grandfather; opened the shop in October 1964; loud, generous, sang at the hotplate; "mada mada"; bought his Seiko to mark the opening; Nankai Hawks fan until they left in 1988; doted on her.
- **Chiyo Tanaka** — paternal grandmother, from Kishiwada; "the Minister of Finance"; cabbage, the pencil ledger, the till until 1987, the Sunday ledger meetings; bought the money box.
- **Hiroshi Tanaka** — father; quiet second-generation cook and only child; the FM2 (1982); Tigers devotee; deadpan boke; caught her hand at the hotplate.
- **Yumiko Tanaka (née Morita)** — mother; former Kintetsu department store gift counter; tsukkomi with the menu; karaoke queen; Takarazuka fan; ran the front from 1987.
- **Fumiko Morita** — maternal grandmother; widow from 1988; the slow wooden house, songs, gentle yokai and the fox mother, Kuromon and the flea market; Mizuki's first month was spent with her.
- **Shigeru Morita** — maternal grandfather (1924 to 1988), Hankai tram driver who whistled at every stop; known to her only through the altar, his cap and stories.
- **Kenta Tanaka** — brother, born 22 November 1993; "not very good" at first; her first captive audience.
- **Daisuke Tanaka** — second cousin in Kobe; a two-year-old under a table at her parents' wedding; not yet part of her life.
- **Mr Hirano** — first stool on the left every Saturday since 1964; retired Hankai tram conductor who knew Shigeru; no mayonnaise "in protest"; the racing pages and a pencil; taught her the whistle.
- **The negiyaki taxi driver** — twenty-year regular; too much spring onion every week; called her "the manager"; gave her the Anpanman doll.
- **The leopard-print auntie** — Osaka obachan regular with sweets in her handbag; her first fashion icon.
- **The Friday insurance salarymen** — regulars who sang "Rokko Oroshi"; one lost his job after the bubble.
- **Yumiko's department-store friends** — payday regulars who spoiled the baby.
