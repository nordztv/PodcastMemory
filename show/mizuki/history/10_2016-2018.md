## Chapter 10: Goodbyes (2016 to March 2018)

These are the two years in which Mizuki's life, which had been running on very reliable rails since she was a little girl, came off them and onto different ones. At the start of 2016 she was twenty five, living above the okonomiyaki shop in Abeno, working the front desk of a business hotel in Umeda, raiding three nights a week with Aya, dating a calm Kyoto man who apologised to doors, and dropping in on her grandmother around the corner most evenings on the way home. By the middle of March 2018 she was sitting at a window seat over Osaka Bay with her father's camera in her lap, and almost none of that was the same.

It wasn't one big decision. It was a lot of goodbyes, some chosen and some not, arriving one after another like trains, until she realised she was standing on a platform with a suitcase. She tells these years with more laughing than you'd expect, because there was a great deal to laugh at (fried chicken, a zombie, a Pokémon in the lobby, her mother's Australia announcements), and with one or two quiet sentences she doesn't stretch out. Fumiko is in this chapter, at the end of her life. Mizuki keeps that part short, and so does this.

### Early 2016: twenty five, and the desk

By 2016 Mizuki had been on the front desk for three years, long enough to stop being the new girl and start being the person the new girls came to. She could tie the blue-and-white uniform scarf in the dark, fix it with the hidden safety pin in one movement, and do a check-in in Japanese, English or a mixture of English and pointing while also noticing that the man at the end of the queue needed a phone charger. Mr Hamada, her shift manager, still bowed at exactly thirty degrees, still said very little, and had begun, in the last year, to put her on the rota for the difficult nights: the Friday when two tour groups arrived at once, the evening a whole wedding party checked in drunk. He never said why. She knew why.

The tourists kept coming. Japan had nearly twenty million foreign visitors in 2015 and was heading for twenty four million in 2016, and the Umeda lobby showed it: Korean weekenders with tiny suitcases and perfect skin, Chinese families with rice cookers, Taiwanese couples holding phones with the name of a takoyaki stand she had never heard of, Australians in shorts in January on their way to the snow. Her English had become a work tool she no longer thought about. Her notebook of phrases had moved on from "knackered" and "no worries" to "I'll take it on board", "fair enough" and "that's a bit rich", which an Englishman used about the minibar prices and which she thought, for a whole week, was a compliment.

At home the shop ran as it always had. Her father, Hiroshi, was fifty seven and still cooking every night, flipping okonomiyaki with the two flat spatulas in a rhythm she could hear through the floor of her bedroom. Her mother, Yumiko, ran the front, the till, the regulars and the family, said "you're late" instead of "welcome home" when Mizuki came in the back door at eleven at night, and kept a plate of whatever was left warm on the edge of the hotplate. Mizuki paid no rent. Her mother charged her in washing up instead, and in being shown off to the regulars ("our Mizuki, at the hotel, in Umeda, she speaks English to Americans").

Kenta graduated in March 2016 and started that April as a systems engineer at an IT company in Osaka. He was twenty two, and overnight he became, to Mizuki's horror, an adult: a dark suit, a lanyard, a commuter pass, a habit of saying "noted" when their mother told him things. He still played Dragon Quest in the evenings and still sent photos without words, but now the photos were of his desk, his train and his company cafeteria's curry, with no explanation. "He's sending me reports," Mizuki complained. "I am his big sister, not his manager." Her mother said, "You used to be his manager," which was true.

And around the corner, in the old wooden two-room house with the sticking doors and the morning glories, Fumiko was eighty eight and slowing down. She still sat at the kotatsu, still kept the family altar with Shigeru's photo and a fresh cup of tea in front of it every morning, still sent Mizuki home with mikan in her coat pockets. But she had stopped going to Kuromon market on Saturdays the year before, because the train and the walk were too much, so Mizuki went for her on her days off and brought back the mackerel and the gossip: which fishmonger's son had married, which stall had put up its prices, how the arcade was full of tourists eating wagyu skewers for prices Fumiko refused to believe. "Two thousand yen? For one stick? Was it a golden cow?" Mizuki would show her a photo on her phone, and Fumiko would hold it at arm's length and say, "Ara. It's very small, for a golden cow."

Most evenings after a late shift, Mizuki stopped at Fumiko's for ten minutes on her way home. The lights were usually still on; Fumiko slept less and less and liked to listen to the radio. Mizuki would sit under the kotatsu with her uniform scarf in her pocket, eat a mikan, and tell her about the day's guests in the style of an Osaka comedian, and Fumiko would laugh at the right places, and sometimes, very slowly, fold a paper crane while she listened, from the squares of patterned origami paper she kept in a flat tea tin with a picture of Mount Fuji on the lid. She had taught Mizuki to fold a crane when Mizuki was five, and later to fold one in the dark, by feel, "in case of a power cut, or a sad night". Mizuki could still do it. She thought of those ten-minute visits, at the time, as a habit. Later she understood they were the best part of her day.

### May 2016: Seoul with Aya

The Seoul trip was Aya's idea, like most good ideas and some bad ones. Aya Morimoto had qualified as a pharmacist the year before after six years of study, and had just survived her first full year at a hospital pharmacy in Osaka, where, she said, she counted pills, checked doctors' handwriting and was shouted at by nobody, which made her the calmest she had ever been. She wanted a holiday. Mizuki had a TWICE problem. The answer was obviously Korea.

TWICE had debuted in October 2015, and Yui, her colleague on the desk, had shown Mizuki the "Like Ooh-Ahh" video because one of the Japanese members, Sana, was from Osaka. Then, in late April 2016, TWICE released "Cheer Up", and it finished her off completely. She played it on the Midosuji line on the way to early shifts, on the way home from late shifts, and once, by accident, through the staff room speaker while Mr Hamada was eating his rice ball. He looked at the speaker for a long time and said nothing. "Shy shy shy," Mizuki said, very quietly, and left the room.

Aya, who had been a Kanjani Eight fan since junior high and considered herself loyal to Kansai idols on principle, was sceptical about K-pop. She was also, it turned out, the first of them to learn the "Cheer Up" chorus moves, in her own kitchen, in secret. Mizuki caught her through a video call doing the "shy shy shy" hand flick while waiting for a kettle, and has used it against her ever since.

They went for four days at the end of May, after the Golden Week rush had cleared out of the hotel, on an early budget flight from Kansai Airport. It was under two hours, which astonished Mizuki, who had somehow thought of Korea as far away. "It's closer than Tokyo is by Shinkansen," she told Aya on the plane. "Seoul is basically Kyushu." Aya told her not to say that to any Koreans, which was good advice.

They stayed in a small, clean, slightly too pink hotel in Myeongdong, the shopping district in the middle of the city, which Mizuki loved immediately because it was so like Shinsaibashi and Namba: covered in signs, crowded, loud, full of food carts and young staff standing outside skincare shops calling out to passers-by. The difference was that half the calling was in Japanese. Shop assistants spotted Mizuki and Aya from ten metres away and called "irasshaimase!" and "kawaii!" and handed them free sheet masks, and by the end of the first evening they had collected, without buying anything, about forty sheet masks, eleven tiny sample tubes and a hand cream shaped like a peach. "This country understands omotenashi," Mizuki said. "It's omotenashi with a sales target."

The street food in Myeongdong was the first great discovery: tteokbokki, the chewy rice cakes in a red sauce that looked dangerous and was; egg bread, a little loaf with a whole egg baked into the top; a long spiral of potato on a stick; grilled lobster tails with cheese melted on them, which Mizuki photographed, ate, and judged "too much, but correct". She bought fish-shaped bungeoppang and told Aya they were taiyaki, which they basically are, and the man at the cart said something kind in Korean that neither of them understood and smiled so much they bought two more.

On the second day they did the sensible tourist things. They went up Namsan to N Seoul Tower on the cable car and looked at the city in a soft spring haze, which Mizuki photographed on the FM2 with the feeling that it would come out grey (it came out grey). They rented hanbok, the long, bright Korean traditional dress, from a little shop near Gyeongbokgung Palace, because people in hanbok got into the palace free, and walked around the courtyards in pale pink and mint green trying to look elegant. Aya tripped on her skirt on the palace steps and said a word in Osaka-ben that was not elegant at all, and an elderly Korean man on a bench laughed out loud, and so did they. Mizuki's favourite photograph of the trip is of Aya in that moment: one hand on the skirt, one on a pillar, her mouth wide open, the ancient roof in the background perfectly calm.

### The TWICE fan café

The trip's real purpose, as far as Mizuki was concerned, was a small café near Hongdae, the university district full of buskers and young people, that she had found by scrolling fan accounts late at night. It wasn't official; it was just a café whose owner was clearly a fan, with TWICE posters on every wall, the "Cheer Up" video playing on a loop on a screen in the corner, little Polaroids of the members pinned on a corkboard, and a drink named after each of the nine members. Mizuki ordered Sana's, which was a strawberry latte with too much cream, and Aya, out of loyalty to Kansai, ordered Sana's as well, and then Momo's for good measure, because Momo was from Kyoto prefecture "and Kyoto is near enough".

The café was full of girls: Korean students, two Thai sisters, a table of Japanese high school girls on a school holiday who shrieked when they heard Mizuki and Aya's Osaka-ben and asked where in Osaka they were from. For an hour the whole room sang along to the music video, did the "shy shy shy" move every time it came round, and passed phones between tables to compare photos. Nobody spoke the same language perfectly and it didn't matter. Mizuki, sitting with her strawberry latte in a room of strangers singing in Korean, had the first faint version of a feeling that would become very important two years later: that she could be happy in a place where she didn't understand everything.

She bought a TWICE photo card from a shop in Hongdae afterwards (Sana, obviously) and kept it in her phone case until the case broke in 2019. She also learned, on that trip, that she could read the Korean alphabet she had taught herself in two weekends off the back of song lyrics: menus, shop signs, station names. She couldn't understand most of what she read, but she could sound it out, slowly, like a child reading a picture book, and every time she got one right ("Myeong-dong!") she told Aya, who said, "Yes. We are standing in it."

### Fried chicken, and the consequences

The trip's great mistake was fried chicken. Korean fried chicken, with beer, is called chimaek, and Mizuki and Aya had both read about it on the plane. On the first night they shared a whole chicken, half crispy and half in a sweet, sticky, spicy red glaze, with two glasses of beer and a bowl of pickled radish cubes, in a tiny upstairs restaurant in Myeongdong where the owner was watching baseball. It was so good that they had it again on the second night, at a different place, and on the third night they found one that did a soy and garlic glaze and had a whole chicken each, because they were Osaka girls and Osaka girls do not leave food.

On the last morning neither of them could do up their jeans. Aya, a professional pharmacist, lay on the hotel bed and diagnosed them both with "chicken". Mizuki, who had eaten slightly more, spent the flight home holding her stomach and saying she would never eat fried food again, which lasted until dinner, because her father had made okonomiyaki and okonomiyaki doesn't count. "Too much fried chicken" became their phrase for any lovely thing overdone. They still use it. A three-hour raid night is "too much fried chicken"; so is a film with four endings.

She came home with a suitcase of sheet masks, the photo card, two oversized pastel sweatshirts from an underground shopping street, a pair of wide trousers that she wore until they fell apart in Sydney, and the strong feeling, which surprised her, that it had been good to be a foreigner. At the desk she had spent three years watching people be confused and delighted in her city. For four days she had been confused and delighted in someone else's. "I liked being bad at something," she said to Aya on the train home from the airport. "Is that strange?" Aya said it was very strange, and that she should do it more often.

### Summer 2016: Pokémon Go, Shin Godzilla and a very hot city

Osaka summers are wet and heavy, and Mizuki has always hated them. Summer 2016 was a normal one, which means the air in Umeda felt like a damp towel by eight in the morning, and she spent the walk from the station to the hotel in a slow, polite sweat that the uniform scarf made worse.

It was also the summer of Pokémon Go, which came out in Japan on 22 July 2016 and turned the entire country, for about six weeks, into people walking slowly into each other while looking at their phones. Mizuki downloaded it on the first day, on the Midosuji line, and caught a Pidgey on the platform at Tennoji. She was twenty six and had been playing Pokémon since Pokémon Green under the shop counter at six, and she was thrilled for about a fortnight. She went looking for a Vulpix, her favourite since childhood, and could not find one anywhere in Osaka; the game seemed to have decided Vulpix lived somewhere else. Kenta found one near his office in his first week and sent her a photo of it, without words, which she considered an act of war.

The hotel had its own Pokémon Go problems. A spot in the lobby turned out to be a PokéStop, and for a few weeks a steady trickle of people who were not guests wandered in, stood very still near the lifts, tapped their phones and left. Mr Hamada watched them with deep suspicion and asked Mizuki, as the youngest person on the desk, what they were doing. She explained. He was silent for a while. Then he said, "Are they catching anything good?" and she realised, with joy, that he was curious. She never found out if he downloaded it.

Her father refused to understand the game at all. When she showed him at the hotplate, a Magikarp flapping on the screen in front of the shop's own counter, he looked at it, flipped an okonomiyaki, and said, "We have real fish," which was, she had to admit, a good point.

Shin Godzilla came out on 29 July 2016, and Kenta, who liked monster films and had not yet worked out that he could now afford to go to the cinema alone, took her. She enjoyed it more than she expected and less than he did. "It's a film about meetings," she said afterwards, over gyoza. "Godzilla walks into Tokyo and everyone has a meeting about whether he is walking into Tokyo." She loved the part where the scientists stayed up all night solving the problem with their laptops and instant noodles, because it was the most Japanese thing she had ever seen, and she loved the strange, frightening early form of the monster, crawling up a river with its big empty eyes, because it was a yokai. She did not love the destruction, which she watched with her fingers half over her eyes, and Kenta said, "Nee-chan, fingers?" in the dark, and someone behind them said "shh". Much later, in a Sydney cinema in 2025, a different Godzilla would matter a great deal more. In 2016 he was just a big lizard and a lot of meetings.

### Your Name, three times

Makoto Shinkai's Your Name came out in Japan on 26 August 2016, and she saw it three times in a cinema before she left Japan, which for Mizuki, who doesn't usually like spending money twice on the same thing, is a love letter.

The first time was with Aya, at a big cinema in Umeda on a Sunday afternoon in early September. Neither of them knew much about it except that the trailer had beautiful skies and a song by RADWIMPS. They came out two hours later in a state. It had everything Mizuki loves in a film: a romance with an ache in it, a puzzle, a twist that made her actually gasp out loud in the cinema (Aya grabbed her arm so hard it left a mark), a comet, a shrine, a little town by a lake, and skies painted like photographs. At the climax both of them cried so much that they sat through every name in the credits to recover, and then sat in a café in the Umeda Dungeon and cried a little more over iced coffee while pretending to be fine. Aya said it was "too much fried chicken, but in a good way". Mizuki went home and shot clouds for a week on the FM2, from the hotel's back step, the shop's roof and the platform at Tennoji, trying to get the bright edges of evening cloud the film had made her notice. Most of the frames were a disappointment. Three were not.

The second time was with Takuya, in October. He liked it. He sat through it calmly, said it was beautiful, and then, on the escalator down from the cinema, said the words she has never forgiven: "It was good, but a bit complicated." Mizuki stopped on the escalator, which you are not supposed to do in Osaka, and turned round to look at him. "Complicated? They swap bodies. Then there's a comet. What is complicated?" He said the time part. She explained the time part, at length, all the way to the station, with diagrams on her hand. He nodded and said he understood now, in the reasonable voice that meant he didn't. It's a joke between them, she says, even now, even though there is no "them" any more: she never says anything bad about Takuya except about Your Name, "which is a separate matter".

Everyone in Japan was seeing it that autumn. It became the most-watched film of the year by a mile, and the songs were everywhere. "Zenzenzense" played in shops and konbini and the hotel's staff room, and the hotel lobby filled with young Chinese and Taiwanese guests asking, in careful English, how to get to the real places from the film: the staircase in Tokyo, the lake that looked like the one in the film. Mizuki drew them little maps on hotel notepaper and felt a quiet pride, as if she'd painted the skies herself.

The third time came later, early in 2017, and it was a different film by then. That part belongs further on in this chapter.

### Autumn 2016: USJ, and the castle

Mizuki had been to Universal Studios Japan once, in August 2001, the summer it opened, with her family and Daisuke, when she rode the Jurassic Park boat, got completely soaked, and declared it the happiest day of the year. In fifteen years she had never been back. The park was half an hour from home on the train and she had always meant to go, in the way Osaka people always mean to go to the castle.

What finally got her there was Harry Potter. The Wizarding World of Harry Potter had opened at USJ in July 2014, with a whole snowy village, a castle on a hill and a ride inside it, and for two years Aya had been saying they should go, and Mizuki had been saying she was busy, and Aya had been saying she was not busy, she was just raiding. In October 2016, on a weekday when they both had a day off, Aya bought the tickets herself and sent Mizuki a photo of them with no words, like Kenta, so she had no choice.

They got there when the gates opened, because Aya had read that you needed a timed ticket to get into the Harry Potter area on busy days, and she treated it like a raid: she had a plan, a route and a schedule written in her phone. Mizuki, who had read the books in Japanese at fifteen and seen all the films, did not have a plan. She had a beret and a camera.

The village took her breath away anyway. It was autumn outside and permanent snow inside: the steep roofs of Hogsmeade covered in white, the shop windows crowded with sweets and wands, steam coming out of a little train, and above it all, on the hill, the castle. She stood at the entrance to the village with the FM2 to her eye for so long that Aya pushed her gently forward by the shoulders, "like moving a statue". She found it, and still finds it, one of the most beautiful built places she's ever seen: "Like Ishgard, but you can buy sweets."

They queued for the ride inside the castle, which carries you through the air past dragons, spiders and a Quidditch match, and Mizuki held Aya's hand through the spider part and kept her eyes shut through the dementors. They drank Butterbeer, which Mizuki pronounced "too sweet, correct foam", and had to admit she finished. They bought wands. Aya chose hers carefully, with long consideration, like a pharmacist choosing a medicine. Mizuki chose hers because it was the darkest and had a little twist in the handle that fitted her fingers. They decided, after a long and serious argument in the queue, that Aya was Gryffindor (brave, loud, says "let's just go") and Mizuki was Hufflepuff ("loyal, kind, hungry"), and Mizuki has defended that decision since in all company.

In October USJ also had its Halloween season, and they had both forgotten that, from late afternoon, zombies were let loose in the streets of the park. They were in the New York area, eating churros, when the light began to go orange, the music changed, and a man with a grey face and a torn shirt came lurching round a corner towards a shrieking group of high school girls. Mizuki, who can do ghosts, curses, haunted videotapes and yokai but has never been able to do gore, took one look at the man's make-up and walked very quickly, without speaking, all the way to the exit gates, with Aya running after her laughing so hard she couldn't breathe. They left the park at half past five, which Aya still calls "the earliest anyone has ever left USJ in October". Mizuki maintains it was "a strategic retreat". She knows they're only actors. She was not taking chances.

On the train home she looked at the photographs on the back of her phone, the castle, the snowy roofs, Aya with a wand pointing at a pigeon, and thought, slightly sadly, that it had been wonderful, and that it had taken her fifteen years to come back to a place thirty minutes from her house. "In Osaka you never go to the things in Osaka," she says now. "You only go when a guest asks you how to get there."

### Fumiko's last months

Through the autumn of 2016 Fumiko got slower. Nothing dramatic happened, which, Mizuki says, was almost the hardest part, because there was nothing to point to. She ate a little less. She walked to the corner and back and then sat down. She dozed at the kotatsu in the afternoons with the radio on and woke up embarrassed. She stopped folding cranes because her fingers were stiff, and once handed Mizuki a square of origami paper and said, "You do it, I'll watch," and watched with great satisfaction as Mizuki folded one in front of her, then told her the head was crooked. It was.

The family did what Osaka families do, which is to feed the problem. Yumiko sent Fumiko's favourite things round from the shop: a small okonomiyaki with no beni shoga, a pot of oden, a plate of the sweet egg rolls she liked. Kenta went round on Sunday mornings to change the light bulbs and fix the doors, which had always stuck and which he oiled so thoroughly that they slid shut on their own, and Fumiko complained that the house had become too quiet without the doors making noise. Mizuki kept up her ten-minute visits after late shifts, and on her days off took over the Kuromon shopping completely, and on some Saturdays stayed the afternoon and told Fumiko yokai stories, which was the wrong way round, and Fumiko corrected her pauses. "No. You told the scary part too early. You have to make them think it's a nice story about a cat."

In November, on one of those Saturdays, Fumiko asked her to take a photograph. Mizuki had taken hundreds of pictures of her grandmother over the years, including the prize-winning one of her hands shelling peas, but Fumiko had never asked for one. She wanted a picture of herself sitting at the kotatsu, properly, with her hair done and her good cardigan, the altar behind her with Shigeru's photo. "A nice one," she said. "Not my hands this time. My face." Mizuki took it on the FM2 in the soft November light from the window, one frame, then another. She didn't say anything about why Fumiko might want a nice picture of her face, and neither did Fumiko. They had tea.

That photograph sits on the Tanaka family altar now, next to Shigeru's.

### Winter 2016

In early December 2016 Fumiko caught a winter chest cold that she couldn't shake, and the doctor sent her into hospital, a short taxi ride from Abeno, "to rest". Everyone said she would be home for New Year. She was eighty nine, and tired, and she didn't come home.

Mizuki was with her at the end, on a quiet evening, holding her hand. Her mother had stepped out for a few minutes to talk to a nurse, and it was just the two of them, the way it had been on so many evenings under the kotatsu. Fumiko had been asleep most of the day. Mizuki held her hand, which was small and dry and warm, the same hands from the photograph, and talked to her about nothing, the guests, the Tigers, the cold, and at some point she understood that Fumiko had gone, gently, without any fuss, in the way she had always done everything.

That is all Mizuki says about it, and all that needs saying. When it comes up on air, it is one quiet sentence, and then she moves on.

### The funeral

The next days went the way they go in Japan, with a great deal to do and a great deal of help. The family chose a funeral hall nearby. There was a wake on the first evening, the tsuya, and the funeral service the next day, with the priest, the incense, and the long line of people coming up one by one to bow. Mizuki wore a black dress and black tights and her hair tied back plainly, and noticed, as she noticed everything, who came.

It was more people than she expected. The neighbours from both sides of the street. The old ladies Fumiko had chatted with at the bathhouse for forty years. Three of the shop's oldest regulars in their best suits, who had known Fumiko since before Mizuki was born and stood at the back with their hands folded. Daisuke came from Kobe in a black suit, thirty one now, an accountant, and stood next to Mizuki for a long time without speaking, the way he had sat in front of her in 1997 playing Final Fantasy VII. Afterwards he said, quietly, "She let us stay. After the earthquake. Four months. She never once made us feel like guests." Then he squeezed her arm and said, "Hang in there, audience," and she laughed, at a funeral, which she decided Fumiko would have liked.

Her paternal grandmother, Chiyo, eighty one and very much alive, came in a taxi with a black handbag and examined the flowers with professional interest, then told Yumiko the hall had overcharged them for the chrysanthemums. Yumiko, who in any other week would have argued, said, "Yes, Okaasan," and Chiyo patted her hand, and for a moment mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, who had argued politely about money for thirty years, understood each other completely.

Takuya came to the wake from Kyoto, in a dark suit, and bowed properly to the family, low and long, in the beautiful, slightly old-fashioned way Kyoto people bow, and said exactly the right words to her parents. Fumiko had liked him. "He apologises to doors," she'd said once, approvingly. "He'll apologise to you when he's wrong. That's rare." Mizuki remembered that at the wake and was very glad he'd come, and remembers it still.

Her father stood next to her mother through everything, said almost nothing, as always, and at one point simply took the heavy box of thank-you gifts out of her hands and carried it for the rest of the day without being asked. That is how Hiroshi says things.

Her mother was the one Mizuki watched most carefully. Fumiko was Yumiko's mother, her only parent for twenty eight years, and Yumiko got through the wake and the funeral with the same brisk, cheerful efficiency she used to run the shop on a Friday night: greeting everyone, remembering names, making sure the old ladies had chairs. She reopened the shop three days later, because the regulars would worry, she said, and because she didn't know what else to do with her hands. She broke only once that Mizuki saw, a week later, in the shop's back kitchen, when she found a jar of pickled plums in the fridge with a label in Fumiko's handwriting. She held it and cried for a few minutes, standing up, and Mizuki held her, and then Yumiko blew her nose, put the jar back, and said, "Well. She always made them too salty," and went out front to take an order.

At the hotel, Mr Hamada changed Mizuki's shifts for the next two weeks without being asked, moved her off the early starts, and never mentioned it. When she came back, he bowed to her, thirty degrees, as usual, and said, "Welcome back." On her locker there was a small packet of good green tea with no note. She has never been sure it was from him. She is sure it was from him.

And every night that December, Aya logged in to Final Fantasy XIV, and found Mizu Nyan, and the two of them stood together in the snow in Ishgard, the cold city of Heavensward, not doing anything, not raiding, not talking much. Aya's Paladin in her heavy armour, Mizuki's White Mage in her robe, side by side on a bridge with the snow falling. Sometimes they sat on a bench in the game. Once Aya typed, "Do you want to talk?" and Mizuki typed "no" and Aya typed "ok" and they stayed another hour. "That," Mizuki says, "was the best thing anybody did for me. She came to the funeral, too. But the snow was better."

### A quiet New Year (2017)

A family in mourning in Japan doesn't celebrate New Year: no pine decorations on the door, no "akemashite omedetou", no cheerful cards. The Tanakas had a quiet one. Yumiko made a small, plain osechi box anyway, "because we still have to eat", and the four of them ate it upstairs above the closed shop, watching the New Year's Eve singing show on television with the sound low. Hiroshi didn't make his usual joke about the Tigers' chances. Kenta did, on his behalf, and their father nodded as if it had been his own.

Because Fumiko had died so close to the end of the year, there was no time to send out the mourning postcards that tell people not to send New Year greetings, so New Year cards arrived anyway, and Yumiko answered each one in January with a winter card explaining. Mr Ishida, Mizuki's old boss at the ramen shop near Namba, had already posted his usual brush-written New Year card before he heard. Then a second card came in late January, a plain midwinter greeting in the same thick black brush, with one line: "Your grandmother raised a girl who never let anyone wait. Look after your mother." Mizuki kept both.

In the shower that winter she listened to Utada Hikaru's Fantôme on repeat. It had come out in September 2016, Utada's first album in eight years, and it was full of songs about losing someone and carrying them with you, and she had liked it in the autumn and needed it in January. "Hanataba wo Kimi ni", flowers for you, was the one she couldn't get through. She never told anyone that at the time. She just used a lot of hot water, and her mother, for once, didn't mention the gas bill.

### Clearing the house

In February and March 2017, on Sundays, the family cleared Fumiko's house. It was the hardest part of the whole winter for Mizuki, harder than the funeral, because the funeral was a ceremony with a shape and the house was just Fumiko's things, everywhere, waiting for her.

They did it slowly, one room each Sunday, with the sliding doors open for the cold air and the smell of incense and mothballs coming out to meet them. Yumiko did the clothes and the kitchen, because they were hers to do. Hiroshi and Kenta did the heavy things, and the old radio, and the boxes in the cupboard nobody had opened since Shigeru died. Mizuki was given the small things: the drawers, the shelves, the tins.

There were a great many tins. Fumiko kept everything in tins: buttons, rubber bands, receipts from 1994, ten-yen coins, keys that fitted nothing. Under the television, still in its cardboard box, was Daisuke's Super Famicom, the one he'd brought from Kobe after the earthquake in 1995 and never taken home, with the cartridges in the biscuit tin: Super Mario World, Super Mario Kart and Seiken Densetsu 2, the Secret of Mana game she'd played with Kenta every weekend of their childhood. Mizuki rang Daisuke to ask what he wanted done with it. He said, "It was never mine after 1995. It's yours. The audience gets the console." It went into a box in Kenta's old bedroom above the shop, where it still is.

The tin she kept for herself was the flat one with Mount Fuji on the lid. Inside it was Fumiko's origami paper: a thick stack of patterned squares, chiyogami with tiny cranes, plum blossoms, waves and fans, some of it faded at the edges, some of it new, and at the bottom three paper cranes Fumiko had folded and never given to anyone. Mizuki sat on the tatami with the tin on her knees for a long time. Then she took one square of paper, the one with the little red plum blossoms, and folded a crane on the spot, with the doors open and the cold coming in, and put it on the altar next to Shigeru's photo. Her mother came in, looked at it, and said, "The head's crooked." It was. They both laughed until they cried, and then just cried a bit, and then got on with the cupboards.

The tin of origami paper went to Sydney with her a year later and is in her flat in Pyrmont now. She doesn't use it much: it's for special things, a crane for a birthday, a crane for a sad night. She can still fold one in the dark.

The framed photograph of Fumiko's hands shelling peas, which had hung above the till at the shop and then, since Fumiko asked for it, above her kotatsu, came off its nail on the last Sunday. Mizuki carried it home under her arm. Fumiko's altar and the November photograph of her face went to the Tanaka flat above the shop, where the morning cup of tea in front of the photographs is now Yumiko's job. What would happen to the little wooden house itself was a slow grown-up conversation between her mother and various men in grey suits, and when Mizuki left Japan it had not been finished. The morning glories, she noticed that summer, came up in the yard anyway.

### Your Name, the third time

The third time she saw Your Name was on a quiet weekday morning in March 2017, after a night shift, alone, in an almost empty cinema in Umeda, with a coffee she wasn't supposed to have brought in.

By then it was a different film. The first time it had been a romance with a twist. The third time it was a film about people trying to find each other across distance and time, about the strange feeling of remembering someone you can't quite reach, about a town that's gone and the people who carry it. She understood it better, and it hurt more, and she was glad she was alone. Afterwards she walked out into the bright spring morning in Umeda, looked up at the sky, and thought about her grandmother, and about Takuya, who had just been offered a job in Tokyo, and about distance, and she had the clear, uncomfortable feeling that something in her own story was about to swap places.

She still looks up whenever there's a comet in the news.

### Takuya, and Tokyo (spring 2017)

Takuya Ono had been her boyfriend for nearly five years by then, since the summer of 2012 at university in Kyoto: calm, tidy, kind, good with her parents, steady on bad days, and terrible at video games. He had a sensible job at the precision-parts company in Kyoto, and they met in the middle, on the Keihan line, at Kyobashi or Yodoyabashi, for films and dinners and walks. In 2015 they'd gone to Taipei together, night markets and Jiufen in the rain. Her mother adored him. Her father, who adores nobody out loud, once gave him the last piece of squid, which in the Tanaka family is a declaration.

In March 2017 Takuya was offered a good job in Tokyo, a real step up, and he asked her, carefully and seriously, to come with him.

She thought about it for weeks. She made lists in her notebook on the train. She pictured herself in Tokyo: a hotel job there would be easy to find, the city was exciting, and he would be there. And every time she pictured it, something in her quietly said no. Not to him. To Tokyo, and, she slowly realised, to the shape of the life: moving to the place his job was, because his job was there. She didn't want to leave Osaka for Tokyo, which for an Osaka girl is almost a philosophical position. But under the joke there was something truer, which she couldn't put into words yet: that if she was going to leave Osaka, she wanted it to be for something she'd chosen.

They talked about it properly one evening in April, sitting on the bank of the Kamo River in Kyoto where couples sit at evenly spaced distances as if someone had measured them, the same river they'd walked along when they were students. It was gentle and sad. Nobody shouted. He said he understood. She said she was sorry. He apologised too, of course, because he was Takuya. They agreed that it wasn't anybody's fault: he was choosing his life, and she was going to choose hers, she just didn't know what it was yet. They walked to Sanjo station together. He bowed, a little, and she laughed, and that was the end.

He moved to Tokyo in May. For a few months they messaged now and then, kindly, and then less, the way it goes. She never says a bad word about him, except about Your Name, which, again, is a separate matter.

The weeks after were heavier than she let anyone see. She wasn't heartbroken in the way of films; she was tired, and a bit lost, and kept reaching for her phone at nine o'clock to tell him something funny about a guest and then putting it down. She cut her hair shorter, to just above her shoulders, which her mother said made her look "like a girl in a French film", meaning it kindly, and which Aya said made her look "like a girl who has just broken up with someone", meaning it kindly too. She went to hitokara, solo karaoke, in a tiny booth in Namba after a late shift, and sang Utada's "First Love" and Perfume and, for reasons she can't defend, the whole of "Cheer Up" in phonetic Korean, alone, at midnight. It helped. Solo karaoke has been one of her cures ever since.

Her mother, who had cried at Takuya leaving more than Mizuki had, said nothing at all about it for a week, which was a miracle, and then said, while wiping the counter, "Well. Tokyo people eat natto," as if that settled it. Her father, at the hotplate, said, "He was a good boy," and then, after a long pause, "You're better."

### Stormblood (June 2017)

Final Fantasy XIV: Stormblood came out on 20 June 2017, in the middle of all that, and Mizuki says it arrived like a friend who doesn't ask questions and just brings food.

It took the players east to Doma, a land drawn from old Japan, with rice fields, wooden villages, a castle and music played on Japanese instruments, and to Ala Mhigo, a desert homeland taken back from an empire. The whole story was about people going home, or deciding where home was, which she found, that summer, almost unbearably on the nose. She loved Kugane, a red-lacquered port city like a dream of old Nagasaki, and stood on its bridges at sunset taking screenshots the way she took photographs, waiting for the light. She loved that you could finally swim and dive, and spent an entire evening underwater in the Ruby Sea looking at fish instead of doing the main story, "because that's who I am".

She levelled the new Red Mage job, a sword-and-magic fencer in a big red coat, "because it's stylish", and enjoyed it very much, and never left White Mage. Aya's Paladin stayed in front of her, as she had since 2013. In July the first of the new hard raids, Omega, came out, and their group of eight on the Japanese server, the same group from the Heavensward days, cleared the first floors that summer, three nights a week, "yoroshiku onegaishimasu" at the start and "otsukaresama deshita" at the end. The quiet tank from Nagoya, who had once by pure coincidence checked into her hotel, still logged in from business hotels on work trips. She still hadn't told him. Her verdict on Stormblood, then and now, is loving but honest: "Beautiful, good music, the story is a bit up and down. But it was there when I needed it." It's last on her list of the expansions. It's not last in her heart.

Kenta, meanwhile, had Dragon Quest XI, which came out in July 2017, and played it in his new flat, a small one-room apartment near his office that he'd moved into in the spring, the first time either Tanaka child had lived anywhere but above the shop. Mizuki visited, inspected his fridge (beer, eggs, one onion), and told their mother it was "fine, for a prison".

### The Sky Building, and the question (summer 2017)

The question that Takuya had left behind was simple, and it followed her round all summer: if not Tokyo, then where?

She was twenty seven. She was very good at her job, with four years of keigo and maps and difficult guests behind her. She had a family she loved, a city she knew down to the station exits, a best friend across town and a raid group three nights a week. Nothing was wrong. And every day, the whole world walked up to her desk with suitcases, talking about Brisbane, Toronto, Seoul, Barcelona, Melbourne, Auckland, and handed her a passport, and she said "welcome" and gave them a map, and they went out into her city and had the adventure, and she went home on the Midosuji line.

One evening in late July, after a day shift, she went up to the floating garden observatory on top of the Umeda Sky Building with a thermos of tea, as she had dozens of times, to watch Osaka turn gold, then blue, then into a carpet of lights. Below her a plane was coming down slowly towards Itami airport, all its lights blinking. She watched it all the way down and realised, quite calmly, with the thermos in her hand, that she had spent four years helping other people see Japan, and that she wanted to be the one with the suitcase.

She didn't tell anyone that night. She went home, said "I'm home", got "you're late", ate leftover okonomiyaki at the counter, and then lay in bed and looked up working holiday visas on her phone until two in the morning.

### Deciding on Australia

Japan has working holiday agreements with a lot of countries, and for a few weeks she looked at all of them. Canada, because a Canadian couple at the hotel had shown her photos of mountains. New Zealand, because it was near Australia and quieter. Britain, briefly, because of the English she'd learned at the desk, until she read about the weather. But it was always going to be Australia, and she knew it before she admitted it.

There were the Australians themselves, her favourite guests: cheerful in shorts in the January Umeda wind, sunburnt, saying "no worries" when the room wasn't ready and seeming to mean it, calling her "mate" on the first day. There was the sun, after Osaka winters and wet Osaka summers. There was the light: the hard, bright, blue Sydney light she'd seen in other people's photographs and wanted to learn, so different from the soft grey light of Kansai that she'd shot all her life. There was the fact that the time difference was only an hour or two, so Aya and the raid group would still be awake when she was. And there were, she admits, the rumours that the coffee was very good and the birds very loud, which she wanted to check personally. (Both true.)

Sydney, not Melbourne, because the old couple from Brisbane who'd shown her a screaming cockatoo on a phone had said Sydney had "the best harbour in the world", and she wanted to see if that was an Australian exaggeration. (It wasn't.)

She told Aya first, at a family restaurant in Tennoji, over a parfait. Aya put down her spoon, looked at her for a long time, and said, "Good." Then she said, "Will the internet be fast enough for raids?" Then, a bit later, in a smaller voice, "You'll come back, though?" Mizuki said of course, one year, see the world, come home, very sensible. Aya said, "Hmm," in her pharmacist voice, the one she uses when a patient says they'll definitely finish the antibiotics.

### Telling the family

She told her parents on a Sunday night in August, after closing, at the counter, while her father was scraping the hotplate. She'd rehearsed it in English and Japanese. She said it in Osaka-ben, quickly, all at once: a working holiday, Australia, Sydney, one year, next spring.

Her mother cried. Then she stopped crying, hit Hiroshi on the arm with the menu for no reason, and asked a great many practical questions about spiders. Then, the next evening, she began announcing it to every regular at the counter, and did so for about a month: "Our Mizuki is going to Australia. With kangaroos." Some of the regulars heard it four or five times and congratulated her every time.

Her father said nothing at all that night. He finished scraping the hotplate, washed his hands, and went to bed. Mizuki lay awake wondering if she'd broken something. The next evening, as she came in the back door, he was at the hotplate, and without looking up he said: "Take the camera." That was all. The FM2 had been hers since 2006, of course; it went everywhere with her. But she understood that he was saying yes, and that he was saying something about the camera, and about himself at twenty four buying it with his first proper wages, and about going and looking at things. She said "un", yes, and went upstairs and sat on her bed for a while.

Kenta, the systems engineer, was the most useful person in the whole process. He came round on Sunday evenings with his laptop and checked her visa application line by line, found the right health insurance, worked out how much money she needed to show the Australian government, and, most importantly, set up a family group chat and taught their parents to video call. Yumiko took to it immediately and called him at work twice in the first week to test it. Hiroshi learned to hold the phone so that you could see more than his ear. It turned out, in the long years that followed, to be one of the most important things anyone ever did for her.

### The visa (autumn 2017)

She applied for the Australian working holiday visa online in September 2017, at the desk in her bedroom, at midnight, with Kenta asleep on the floor because he'd missed the last train. The forms were all in English. She filled them in with a calm that would have amazed the girl who'd studied English at university, because by then English forms were part of her job: passport numbers, dates, "purpose of travel", the polite question about whether she had ever been convicted of anything, at which she said "no" out loud to the screen in the voice she used for American guests. She paid the fee in Australian dollars, converted it into yen three times to make sure, and pressed submit, and then didn't sleep at all.

It came through in a few days, by email, which felt far too easy for something so large. She read it at the hotel during a break, in the staff room, on her phone, and then went into the toilets and read it again. It said she had a year from the grant date to enter Australia, and once there, a year to stay. She booked a one-way flight for the middle of March 2018, "after the plum blossoms, before the cherry blossoms", so that she'd miss the season she loved most at home and not have to be sad about it in Sydney.

### Mr Hamada

In November 2017 she gave Mr Hamada her notice, in his small office behind the front desk, with the plinky hotel hold music faintly audible through the wall. She had written a formal letter and practised the speech. She bowed forty five degrees, the apology bow, though she wasn't apologising, because it was the deepest one she knew.

Mr Hamada read the letter. He was silent for a long time. Then he stood up and bowed back, forty five degrees. In nearly five years she had never seen him bow more than thirty to anyone, not to a company president, not to the hotel's owners. Then he sat down and said, "Australia. The guests are very relaxed," and she said, "Yes," and he said, "You will need to be less relaxed than them, or nothing will get done," which she still thinks about every time she runs the ramen shop. He asked her to train her replacement properly, and she did, a nervous young woman from Nara whom she taught the hidden safety pin trick on her first day.

Her last day at the hotel was at the end of February 2018. The staff gave her a card signed by everyone, a box of Osaka sweets, and a drawing, by one of the cleaning ladies whose granddaughter was good at art, of a kangaroo wearing the hotel's blue-and-white uniform scarf. Mr Hamada shook her hand, which nobody had ever seen him do, and said "Thank you for your hard work", the ordinary end-of-shift phrase, in a voice that wasn't ordinary. She took the scarf home. She has never worn it again and never thrown it away.

### Winter 2017 to 2018: lasts

The last winter was full of lasts, and she noticed every one, which was exhausting.

TWICE had made their official Japanese debut in June 2017, and that autumn "Likey" and the Japanese single "One More Time" were everywhere. On New Year's Eve, at home above the shop for the last time in years, the whole family watched TWICE on the big New Year's Eve singing show, and Mizuki did the "TT" pose at the television, and her mother, who had no idea who they were, did it too, slightly wrong, and Mizuki laughed so much she had to lie on the floor. Then her mother switched to the Takarazuka recording she'd saved, and they watched that too, and Yumiko sang along to every song, the way she had since Mizuki was nine.

Kenta, who had never understood her loyalty to old games, came round in the winter with a present: the Super Famicom Mini, the tiny new version Nintendo had brought out in October 2017 with the old games built in, Seiken Densetsu 2 among them. She told him it was "not the real thing, a toy, a souvenir". Then they played Secret of Mana on it for four hours on a Sunday, him as the sprite and her as the girl, shouting "Heal me! Why are you casting on the rabbit?" exactly as they had at Fumiko's at six and nine. It went to Sydney in her suitcase. It's the only video game console she has ever packed.

She came down with a proper flu in early February, the kind that goes round every Osaka office and school in winter, and spent five days in bed above the shop with a fever, her mother bringing her rice porridge with umeboshi (which she has hated since childhood and ate anyway, because she was too weak to argue) and her father, once a day, standing in the doorway, looking at her, nodding, and leaving. She thought, lying there, about being ill in a country where nobody would bring her porridge, and nearly cancelled everything. On the sixth day she got up, ate an okonomiyaki, and didn't.

There was a last Saturday at Kuromon, for Fumiko: grilled scallop with soy and butter on a stick, eaten standing up, and the old fishmonger who'd known Fumiko giving her a piece of mackerel sushi for free and saying, "Your obaachan would've told you to come back." There was a last bowl at Mr Ishida's near Namba, where he set down her tonkotsu with an extra slice of chashu without a word, charged her full price, and, as she left, said "Firm noodles. Always," which is the closest thing to a blessing he had. There was a last walk through Nakanoshima, in the cold, the rose garden all bare stalks, and a last sunset on the Sky Building with the thermos. There was a last raid night with the group, on a Thursday in early March, where they cleared a fight they'd been stuck on for two weeks, and the Nagoya tank typed "say hi to the Australian staff for me", and she took a screenshot. She still never told him she had once checked him in.

And there was a call with Aya that lasted until four in the morning, about nothing and everything, promising to keep playing together across the ocean. (They did. "The time difference is only an hour or two. Honestly, I didn't go very far.")

### Packing

One suitcase was the rule. She set it herself, because Fumiko had once said that you can only really own what you can carry, and because she'd watched five years of guests with too much luggage fighting their suitcases through the Umeda Dungeon. She packed and unpacked it about forty times over three weeks on her bedroom floor, with her mother coming in regularly to add things and her taking them out again when her mother had gone.

What went in, in the end:

- **The Nikon FM2**, in her carry-on, wrapped in a white T-shirt, with ten rolls of film, because she'd read that film was expensive in Australia. (It was.) It lives on her shelf in Pyrmont now, and she still puts a roll through it now and then, though since 2024 the Zf is her everyday camera.
- **The Vulpix keyring**, moved off her house keys, which she left on the hook by the shop's back door for her mother, onto her backpack. "It's my fox. It's lucky. And I need one friend from home."
- **The framed photograph of Fumiko's hands**, wrapped in her denim jacket.
- **Fumiko's tin of origami paper**, the flat one with Mount Fuji on the lid.
- **A bottle of the shop's own okonomiyaki sauce**, her father's blend, which he decanted himself into a smaller bottle, taped shut, wrapped in two plastic bags and a sock, and put in her suitcase without saying anything, "in case Australia has no sauce" (that was her mother's line, said loudly over his shoulder). Australia had sauce. She didn't tell her mother for about a year. It was not the same sauce, and she has never pretended it was.
- A bag of her mother's dashi powder, furikake and instant miso soup.
- The Super Famicom Mini, two berets, one oversized vintage denim jacket, five white T-shirts, the wide trousers from Seoul, her "old man's watch" from her grandfather Isamu on her wrist, the hotel scarf, the TWICE photo card in her phone case, and the kangaroo drawing.

What stayed: her vintage clothes collection, boxed up in Kenta's old room and guarded by her mother; Daisuke's Super Famicom in its box; her winter coat ("Australia doesn't have winter", which Sydney corrected that July); her bicycle; and about two hundred prints and negatives from her teens and twenties, in shoeboxes under her bed, which her mother promised not to touch and has not.

### Kansai Airport (March 2018)

The night before she flew, after closing, her father cooked her favourite: pork and squid okonomiyaki with extra bonito flakes, the bonito curling and dancing on the hot sauce. He cooked it on the hotplate at the counter where she had grown up, and her mother and Kenta sat either side of her, and nobody said much, which in the Tanaka family is how you know it's serious. When she'd finished, her father scraped the hotplate, washed his hands, and said, "It'll be better when you come back. I'll practise." It was the longest sentence he said all week.

In the late afternoon, mid-March, cool and bright, with the plum blossoms finished and the cherry buds still tight on the trees by Shitennoji, they drove her to Kansai International Airport, the long bridge out over the grey water of Osaka Bay to the airport on its own man-made island. Her mother talked the whole way about everything except Australia: the regulars, the price of cabbage, a Takarazuka star who was retiring. Her father drove and carried the suitcase and would not let her take it, even at the check-in desk, even when the airline staff tried. Kenta took photos on his phone of everything, without words, including one of her checking in that she still has: she's bowing to the counter staff, fifteen degrees, without noticing.

At the departure gate her mother finally cried, and hugged her hard, and said, "You'll be late," which made no sense and made complete sense. Her father put his hand on her head for a second, the way he had when she was small, and said, "Take care," and then, because it was him, "Don't eat natto." Kenta made a terrible joke about kangaroos having boxing licences, which made her laugh, which is the Osaka way to say goodbye: you leave laughing, so nobody sees you cry.

At the window seat, with the Nikon in her lap and the Vulpix keyring in her pocket, Mizuki watched the lights of Osaka Bay fall away into the dark. She had spent five years watching people leave with suitcases. Now she was twenty seven, a month short of twenty eight, with one suitcase, a camera, a fox, a tin of her grandmother's paper and a bottle of her father's sauce. "I thought I was going for one year," she says. "See the world, come home. Very sensible." Then she laughs. "I was not very sensible."

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:**
  - **Korean street food and Myeongdong:** the first place she was happily a foreigner; omotenashi "with a sales target".
  - **TWICE, especially Sana, and "Cheer Up":** an Osaka girl being brave in a second language in a foreign country, which she found inspiring before she knew why.
  - **Korean fried chicken:** loved, but forever "too much fried chicken", her phrase with Aya for any lovely thing overdone.
  - **Your Name:** romance, a twist and skies like photographs; it meant more each of the three times.
  - **The Harry Potter area at USJ:** snowy roofs and a castle, "like Ishgard, but you can buy sweets"; she's Hufflepuff, Aya is Gryffindor.
  - **Stormblood's Doma and Kugane, and swimming in Eorzea:** a story about going home, there when she needed it.
  - **Utada Hikaru's Fantôme:** the album that got her through the winter Fumiko died.
  - **Solo karaoke (hitokara):** her cure since the spring of 2017.
  - **Origami cranes from Fumiko's tin:** kept for special things and sad nights.
  - **The Umeda Sky Building at sunset with a thermos:** where she decided to leave.
  - **Old games, even on the Super Famicom Mini:** Secret of Mana with Kenta, still shouting about the rabbit.
- **Dislikes:**
  - **Zombies and gore:** the Halloween zombies at USJ sent her out of the park at half past five.
  - **"A bit complicated":** anyone who says it about Your Name.
  - **Pokémon Go's distribution of Vulpix:** a personal insult, made worse by Kenta.
  - **Rooms that are too quiet:** Fumiko's house with oiled doors; the house after it was empty.
  - **Umeboshi:** still hated, even in flu porridge.
  - **Wet Osaka summers:** a damp towel by eight in the morning.
  - **Too much luggage:** the one-suitcase rule, from Fumiko and from watching guests in the Umeda Dungeon.
