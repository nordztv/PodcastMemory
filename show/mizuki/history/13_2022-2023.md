## Chapter 13: Opening up (2022 to 2023)

### The shape of a Pyrmont week (early 2022)

By the start of 2022 Mizuki had lived in Sydney for nearly four years and in her Pyrmont flat for about a year, and she had done what Osaka people do with any new place: she had given it a routine, a set of opinions and a list of where to eat. The borders were still mostly shut, the second long lockdown was behind her, and the city was coming back to life in a careful, half-masked way, like someone getting out of bed after a fever.

The flat was the same small, square, one-bedroom place a few streets back from the water: the low table she sat at on a cushion, the shelf with her father's Nikon FM2 in the middle like a family shrine and the Vulpix keyring hanging off its strap, the framed print of Fumiko's hands shelling peas, the rice cooker that played a little tune when it finished ("the only flatmate who never complains"). The balcony held one folding chair, the shiso, a pot of mint that was trying to take over the world and a chilli plant that had produced exactly two chillies in its life and seemed embarrassed about both. She called the slice of harbour she could see if she leaned out at the right angle "my one-chair view", and she was as proud of it as if she had built the harbour herself.

Her week had a shape, and she defended it.

**Weekdays** began at about a quarter to ten, when she left the flat with her bag, her headphones and usually her camera. The walk to work took twenty-five minutes if she didn't stop, and she always stopped: over the old timber deck of Pyrmont Bridge, along the Darling Harbour waterfront, past Tumbalong Park and the walls of the Chinese Garden, up through Haymarket to World Square. She photographed the light on the water, joggers in matching outfits, office workers carrying three coffees in a cardboard tray, and the ibis, whom she still found "disgusting and magnificent, like a Kansai auntie at a sale". The walk was her daily photo walk, and it is why she can tell you how Cockle Bay looks in every month and every kind of weather.

At the shop she had been shift lead since 2021. That meant she held the keys on Mr Kondo's days off, did the roster in pencil (Mr Kondo did not trust the roster app), trained the new casuals to fill water before anyone asked, and was the person customers were sent to when they wanted to complain about the queue. She was good at it. She had her mother's memory for regulars and her old hotel manager's bow, and she had Mr Ishida's rules in her bones: firm noodles, broth that had suffered properly, and the customer never waits for water.

**Tuesday and Thursday nights** were raid nights with the Tsukimi Noodles Free Company at nine, Sydney time. **Wednesday** was her lazy night: a sheet face mask, a Japanese drama, an early bed and no apologies. **Saturday mornings** were the fish market at Blackwattle Bay, a short walk from the flat, where she bought one good thing the way Fumiko had taught her at Kuromon: a few scallops, a piece of salmon belly, half a dozen oysters, sea urchin only when she was "feeling rich or feeling sad". Saturday afternoons were for a photo walk, a beach, or the K-pop dance class with Nana. **Sunday** was laundry, the video call with her parents, a call with Aya, a long and very serious clothes-shopping trip in the city that she insisted counted as exercise, and okonomiyaki for dinner, her mother's recipe, never pressed down.

She was, she admits, quite Japanese about the routine. Lily once moved the raid night in Mizuki's paper diary as a joke and Mizuki noticed within four seconds.

In January a small padded envelope arrived from Abeno. Inside was a **yakuyoke** charm from Sumiyoshi Taisha, to ward off bad luck, and a note from her mother pointing out that 2022 was Mizuki's **yakudoshi**. In the old way of counting, where you are one on the day you're born and everyone gets a year older at New Year, she was thirty-three, and thirty-three is the great unlucky year for a woman, with a "front" unlucky year before it and a "back" one after. "Big one," the note said. "Be careful. Carry this. Don't climb anything." Mizuki, who is not superstitious, put the charm in her bag that same afternoon and has never been able to explain why.

On **Setsubun**, 3 February, she did the bean-throwing in the flat for the first time, with a packet of roasted soybeans from the Japanese grocer. She opened the balcony door, threw a handful out and whispered "oni wa soto", demons out; then threw a handful into the living room and whispered "fuku wa uchi", luck in. She whispered because of the neighbours, who already knew her as the woman who clapped at a bookshelf. Then she picked the beans up off the floor, because mottainai, and ate one for each year of her age, plus one for luck: thirty-two, slowly, at the low table, followed by her eho-maki, facing the lucky direction her app gave her and feeling, she says, "extremely protected for a woman in a yakudoshi".

### A wet year

2022 was the year it would not stop raining in Sydney. It turned out to be the wettest year the city had ever recorded, and Mizuki, who had arrived in 2018 expecting a sunny country, took it personally. In the floods of March and again in July the news showed rivers over the roads to the west and the north, and she watched with the quiet seriousness of someone who grew up with earthquake drills and a disaster bag by the door. In the city it was mostly just endless, sideways, umbrella-breaking rain.

She already owned the raincoat she had bought after her first Sydney storm in 2018, a long navy one with a hood, and in 2022 she wore it so much that Lily said she looked "like a very small detective". She bought proper waterproof boots from a shop on George Street and declared them the best money she had spent in Australia. Her umbrella count for the year was four: one lost on a bus, one turned inside out on Pyrmont Bridge, one left at the shop and adopted by Mr Kondo, and one that survived and is still by her door. In Osaka, she pointed out, you can buy a clear plastic umbrella at any konbini for a few hundred yen when the rain surprises you, and you can leave it in the stand outside a shop knowing it will be there when you come out. In Sydney the umbrellas cost twenty dollars and lasted about as long as a TikTok.

The rain had one gift. Wet nights in Darling Harbour, with the lights doubled in the puddles and people running with their jackets over their heads, were the closest Sydney ever came to the Dotonbori nights she had shot with the FM2 in her twenties. She took some of her favourite night photos of those years in 2022, standing under the awning of a closed shop near the Chinese Garden with her film pushed a stop and her hands cold, waiting for someone with a red umbrella to walk past. Someone always did, eventually.

### The shop comes back

The ramen shop came out of lockdown slowly. In early 2022 the office towers around World Square were still half empty, because many people were working from home and in no hurry to stop. The lunch rush, which had once been a queue out the door from noon to two, became a lunch "busy" from half past twelve to half past one. Mr Kondo, who hummed when he was worried, hummed a great deal that summer. He hummed old Hokkaido folk songs, then Southern All Stars, which Mizuki pointed out was her father's music, which made him hum it more.

By autumn the offices were filling up again, at least from Tuesday to Thursday. Mondays and Fridays stayed quiet, because, as Lily explained, "nobody in Sydney is going into the office on a Friday ever again". Mizuki found this both shocking and enviable. In Osaka, she said, the office worker who stayed home on a Friday would be discussed at every desk in the building on Monday. In Sydney the person who went in on a Friday was the one people worried about.

The menu didn't change, but prices did. Like everything else in 2022 the cost of pork bones, flour, gas and rent went up, and Mr Kondo had to put the price of a bowl up twice in a year. He wrote the new prices on the board himself, looking like a man signing his own confession. Mizuki took the complaints with her best front-desk face. Privately she thought Sydney ramen had always been too cheap for how long a proper broth takes, and too expensive for how most of it tasted, "both at the same time, which is very impressive".

Lily was still there. She was twenty-one in 2022, in her last years of study at UTS, working three shifts a week and singing Red Velvet under her breath while she folded napkins. Their great argument that year was Red Velvet's "Feel My Rhythm", which Lily played on a loop in March because it used Bach's "Air on the G String" ("this is culture, Mizuki"), against TWICE, whom Mizuki defended out of loyalty and Osaka pride, since Sana, Momo and Mina are Japanese and Sana is from Osaka itself. The argument came back every time either group released anything. Neither ever won. Both of them agreed, privately, that the argument was the best part.

Lily had become something like a little sister. She asked Mizuki about boys, rent, Korean grammar and whether a dress was too much ("there is no too much, only not enough confidence"), and Mizuki asked Lily how to word an email to the real estate agent so it sounded firm but not rude, which in Australian English is an art form. Lily taught her that "no dramas" means yes, "yeah, nah" means no, and "nah, yeah" means yes, and that this is not a joke. Mizuki taught Lily to fold a towel the Japanese way, to say "otsukaresama" at the end of a shift, and to give the difficult customer a bow just deep enough to make him feel important and just shallow enough that he notices you are only doing it because you have to.

### The regulars, and the shop TV

The regulars came back in ones and twos, and each one felt like a small celebration. The tradie in hi-vis who ordered the tonkotsu with extra garlic every Wednesday came back in February with a new beard and the news that he had become a grandfather. The quiet Japanese businessman who ate alone, nodded once and left exact change came back in April, nodded once, and left exact change, and Mizuki was so pleased she had to go into the back for a moment. The elderly woman who came on Mondays for the shoyu and told Mizuki she was too thin came back thinner herself, and Mizuki began bringing her a small bowl of pickles on the house, which the woman pretended not to notice and always finished.

The small television on the wall by the kitchen pass, which Mr Kondo kept on the news with the sound off, became a little window on the year. They watched the floods, the federal election in May, with its sausage sizzles outside polling places (Mizuki could not vote, not being a citizen, but she bought a democracy sausage from a school stall in Pyrmont anyway, "to support the system"), and in June the return of Vivid Sydney after two cancelled years. She went three nights in a row, shooting the light shows on the Customs House and the Opera House sails, and decided Vivid was "the one time Sydney admits it is a little bit Dotonbori".

### Lockdown leftovers

Some habits from the closed years stayed. She still carried a mask in her bag and wore it on crowded trains long after most people had stopped, partly from Japanese habit and partly because she did not want to be the reason the shop was short-staffed. She kept the Sunday okonomiyaki video call with her mother, though now it was less a lesson and more a competition, with her father passing behind the screen to say "too much cabbage" or, once that year, "that's a good one", which the whole family discussed for a week.

And she kept the weekly calls with Aya. Aya was a hospital pharmacist in Osaka by then, single, busy, sharp as a pin and very tired, and she had spent the pandemic years in the middle of everything at work. Their calls were the same as ever: Mizuki said something silly, Aya hit back, and for half an hour both of them were fourteen again. They watched Japanese dramas "together", starting the episode at the same moment and messaging through it. Aya's shiba inu, Mame, appeared in every call and looked at the screen as if Mizuki owed her money. And Aya's Paladin was still in the same raid static as Mizu Nyan on Japanese servers, so on Tuesday nights the two friends from the first day of junior high still stood side by side in Eorzea, Aya with her shield and Mizuki behind her with her staff, the way it had been since 2013: "the tank and the healer, rice and pickles."

### Music for walking home

Her walking-home music in the first half of 2022 was a mixture of old and new. TWICE always; Aimyon for rainy evenings, especially "Marigold", which she had loved since 2018 and which made her think of summers at Shirahama; YOASOBI, still, though Lily had banned "Yoru ni Kakeru" from the shop speaker in 2020 and the ban had never been lifted. That spring she added Official Hige Dandism's "Mixed Nuts", the opening song of the new anime *Spy x Family*, which she and Lily watched every week from April. They both loved Anya, the small pink-haired telepath who says "waku waku" when she's excited. By May the two of them were doing Anya's smug face at each other across the shop whenever a customer said something ridiculous. Mr Kondo did not understand the face and asked once if they were unwell.

In January, as she did every year, she listened to triple j's Hottest 100, the countdown of Australia's favourite songs of the previous year, which she had adopted as her own Australian New Year's custom. The 2021 countdown was won by the Wiggles, the children's entertainers in coloured skivvies, with their cover of Tame Impala's "Elephant". Mizuki, who had a soft spot for Tame Impala as "music for walking home in the heat", was delighted beyond reason. "This country is very serious about being not serious," she told her mother, who had never heard of the Wiggles and was not sure she wanted to.

### July 2022: a ramen worker with no tongue

In early July 2022, in the middle of the winter wave that swept through Sydney that year, Mizuki finally caught COVID.

She had avoided it through two lockdowns, a share house with a nurse in it, and two and a half years of serving soup to strangers, and she had started to believe, privately, that she had a special Osaka immune system made of okonomiyaki sauce and stubbornness. Then one Tuesday afternoon she had a scratchy throat, by the evening she felt as if someone had filled her head with wet towels, and the next morning the rapid test on her kitchen bench showed two lines so dark and immediate that she photographed it with the FM2 out of a sort of grim respect.

It was mild. She wants that understood. She had a fever for two days, a cough for a week, and the kind of tiredness that made walking to the fridge feel like climbing the stairs at Fushimi Inari. In New South Wales that winter, people who tested positive still had to isolate for seven days, so she rang Mr Kondo, who hummed down the phone for a long moment and then said, "Rest. I'll do the roster. Don't come back until you're better, and don't come back early to prove you're Japanese." She came back exactly on the eighth day anyway.

The week alone in the flat was quiet and a little bit lonely, and also very well fed, because her people turned up in the ways people do. **Lily** left a bag on her doorstep with honey, lemons, Korean instant porridge, a pack of Pocky and a handwritten note: "GET WELL. RED VELVET IS STILL BETTER. — L." **Nana** sent a voice message singing a TWICE song badly, and a parcel of nail stickers "for when you have energy to be beautiful again". **Fiona**, who had been a nurse through the whole pandemic, texted her every evening with the same three questions (temperature, breathing, drinking enough water?) in the same brisk Glasgow tone, and then, every time, one line that wasn't a question: "You're doing fine, hen." On the second evening she also left a tub of her own lentil soup on the doormat, knocked, and was halfway down the stairs before Mizuki reached the door, which Mizuki says is the most Glasgow way to be kind. In Osaka her mother rang twice a day and asked whether she was eating, which in the Tanaka family is the same as asking whether you are alive. Her father came on the phone once, said "sleep", and handed the phone back.

On the third day she made herself rice porridge with an umeboshi in it, the food her mother made when she was sick as a child, and noticed that it tasted of nothing at all. Not "a bit bland". Nothing. She put a whole umeboshi on her tongue, the sourest thing in any Japanese kitchen, the thing that makes children's faces fold up like origami, and it was like eating a warm wet button. She sat at her low table and laughed, and then she nearly cried, and then she rang Aya.

"I've lost my taste and smell."

"You never had taste. Look at that jacket you wore in 2010."

The loss lasted three weeks, and it was, she says now, the strangest three weeks of her working life. She was a ramen shop shift lead who could not taste ramen. She couldn't smell the broth, which she had always judged as much by nose as by mouth: the deep, slightly farmyard richness of tonkotsu that tells you it has boiled long enough, the burnt-sugar edge of chashu at the right moment, the sharp clean smell of fresh spring onion. Everything in the kitchen was just warm and wet. She started calling herself "a ramen worker with no tongue", and the phrase stuck, because Lily repeated it to every regular who asked where Mizuki's sparkle had gone.

She worked anyway, once she tested negative, and learned to cheat. She judged the broth by its colour against the white of the bowl and by how it clung to the back of a spoon. She judged the chashu by the sound it made under the knife. And she judged everything else by Mr Kondo's face, which she knew so well by then that she could tell from the angle of his eyebrows whether the soup was a seven or an eight. He found this both flattering and stressful. "Stop looking at me like I'm the soup," he said.

At home it was worse. Her Sunday okonomiyaki tasted like hot cardboard. Her morning coffee was hot brown water. The fish market on Saturday was a sad trip: she looked at a tray of beautiful scallops, remembered she couldn't taste them, and bought nothing, which had never happened before in her life. She ate for texture for three weeks (crunchy tenkasu, chewy mochi, the snap of a cold cucumber) and discovered, to her horror, that she could not tell her favourite brand of instant miso soup from the cheap one, "which means I've been paying extra for nothing for years, or that the world is a lie".

Her smell came back first, all at once, on a Tuesday morning at the end of July. She walked onto the balcony, brushed the shiso with her hand the way she always did, and the smell of it hit her so hard she sat down in the one chair: green, sharp, minty and peppery, the smell of summer in Osaka, of cold tofu and somen and her grandmother's kitchen. She sat there in her pyjamas smelling a plant like a mad person until she was late for work. That afternoon, at the shop, she tasted the tonkotsu for the first time in three weeks, took a long, thoughtful sip, and announced, "Seven and a half." Mr Kondo, who had been hoping for an eight, said, "Welcome back," in the tone of a man who wished she had stayed away a bit longer.

For a few weeks after, some things tasted odd. Coffee was slightly burnt, and onions faintly metallic. Then that passed too. The lessons she kept: that she should never take the smell of shiso for granted, that a cook's tongue is a tool you only notice when it's gone, and that you should have honey and lemons in the cupboard before you need them, not after. And she kept the photograph of the test with the two dark lines. She is not sure why. "It is the ugliest photo I own," she says, "and somehow I can't throw it away."

Her mother had a diagnosis of her own, delivered on the first video call with Mizuki's voice still a croak: "Yakudoshi." From then on Yumiko blamed everything that went wrong in 2022 on Mizuki's unlucky year, starting with the COVID and working backwards and forwards through the rain, the price of cabbage, Chiyo's fall and the Tigers' start to the season, which she held was not Mizuki's fault exactly but "can't have helped". Mizuki pointed out that the charm had been in her bag the whole time. "Imagine," said her mother, "without it."

### The friends she had by then

By the second half of 2022 Mizuki had what she called "a Sydney family in four flavours", and it's worth saying who they were, because they carried her through these years.

**Fiona MacLeod**, her old housemate from the Glebe house, lived in Camperdown, a short walk from the hospital where she worked. She was dry, tired, kind, and incapable of pretending. She and Mizuki had a standing Sunday brunch once a month at a café in Newtown or Camperdown, where Fiona ordered something with a fried egg on top and Mizuki ordered something Fiona called "a small pile of virtuous leaves". They talked about work, families on the other side of the world, the rain, and Fiona's love life, which Fiona described as "a documentary about a slow-moving natural disaster". Fiona was the one person Mizuki could ring at eleven at night when something was wrong, and she knew it. She was her closest non-Japanese friend in Sydney then, and still is.

**Nana Yoshida**, the friend from Kobe she had met in 2018 over an overpriced box of curry roux at a Japanese grocery near Town Hall, worked as a nail artist at a salon in Chatswood. Nana was the friend who said yes to everything: a 6am photo walk, a K-pop dance class, a late-night trip to a Korean fried chicken place in Strathfield, a weekend away booked on a Thursday. They had been going to a weekly K-pop dance class together in the city since before the pandemic, and on Saturdays they were back in the studio, in the back row, learning the choreography for whatever had just come out. Nana was a better dancer. Mizuki had better facial expressions, "which is half of K-pop". Nana did Mizuki's nails once a month for nothing but lunch, and they were always slightly too much for a ramen shop, which was the point.

**Lily** was the shop sister. And **Kazu**, the leader of Tsukimi Noodles, a Japanese dad in Sydney's north-west who played a big Roegadyn tank, and who, now that his kids were old enough to read their own bedtime stories, had moved raid nights to Tuesdays and Thursdays at nine, was the steady centre of the online family. Kazu was the sort of leader who remembered everyone's birthday, handed out potions before a raid like a father handing out sandwiches, and said "shall we try once more?" in a voice so calm that even after the fifteenth wipe nobody could be angry with him. He was the first person in the Free Company Mizuki told when she booked her ticket home, months later. He said, "Bring us back some Osaka," and meant it literally: she came back with a box of Pocky in a flavour you couldn't get in Australia and posted a photo of it in the FC chat like a holy relic.

Rafa and Bia, the Brazilian couple from the Glebe house, were back in São Paulo by then. They messaged on birthdays and sent photos of their barbecues, which Mizuki still found too big.

### Late September 2022: the Gold Coast with Lily and Nana

The Gold Coast trip began, like most of Mizuki's adventures in these years, with Nana saying yes before anyone had finished the sentence.

It was August, in the staff room, and Lily was complaining that her uni break was at the end of September and she had no plans and no money and no boyfriend, "which is three nos". Mizuki said, half-joking, that she had never been to the Gold Coast and was the only person in Australia who hadn't. Nana, who had come to meet her for lunch, looked up from her phone and said, "Theme parks. Let's go." By the end of the day there was a group chat called "GOLD COAST OR DIE" (Lily's name), a cheap holiday apartment in a tower in Surfers Paradise, and three return flights to Coolangatta booked for the last week of September, when Lily's semester had its break and Mr Kondo could cover Mizuki's shifts if she took the next month's Saturdays.

Theme parks meant something particular to Mizuki. She had grown up on them: Nara Dreamland in 1998, with its copy of Sleeping Beauty's castle, which closed in 2006 and broke her heart quietly; Hirakata Park, first with her class in 2000 and again the next spring, when the new wooden coaster called Elf made her cry in front of all her classmates; Expoland the same year with Daisuke, where she loved the Ferris wheel; Universal Studios Japan in 2001, soaked on the Jurassic Park ride, the happiest day of that year; Tokyo Disneyland in 2005, "too clean, but the churros were correct"; and then Luna Park in Sydney in 2018, where the giant smiling face at the entrance had frightened her so much she'd had to be talked through the gate by Bia. She had not been on a proper roller coaster in years. The last time, she reminded the others, she had cried.

"You won't cry," said Nana.

"I'll film it if you do," said Lily.

**The flight and the light.** The flight from Sydney took about an hour and a half. Mizuki had the window seat and photographed the whole coastline: the long beaches north of Newcastle, the river mouths, the green mountains behind everything, until they came in over the sea and the towers of Surfers Paradise stood up along the beach "like a Monopoly board somebody left in the sun". The light was different again from Sydney's: softer, warmer, more holiday. It was spring, about twenty-five degrees, and the air smelled of sunscreen and frying.

They got around on the tram, the G:link light rail that runs along the coast, which Mizuki loved immediately because it came when the timetable said it would. "Finally, a train in Australia with Japanese manners," she said, loudly enough for a man in board shorts to look round. They ate fish and chips on the sand at Surfers, bought matching bucket hats from a shop that sold nothing but bucket hats and fridge magnets, and walked to the end of the beach at sunset. Mizuki wore her long-sleeved rash vest into the sea with sunscreen on her feet, of course, and was teased for it by a group of local teenagers until Lily turned round and gave them a look that made them find somewhere else to be.

**Movie World** was the first full day. Mizuki had dressed for it with great seriousness: wide-leg cotton trousers, a white T-shirt, her oversized denim jacket tied round her waist, her grandfather Isamu's Seiko watch strapped tight ("if I lose this watch on a ride I will have to live on the ride forever") and the new bucket hat, which she pretended not to like and wore for the rest of the trip. She left the FM2 in the apartment and took only her phone, after Nana pointed out that Hiroshi Tanaka's camera did not deserve to be thrown upside down at a hundred kilometres an hour.

They began, on Lily's orders, with the biggest one: the DC Rivals HyperCoaster, a long, tall, purple and green monster of a ride that climbs very slowly and then drops you almost straight down. Mizuki spent the climb explaining calmly, in Japanese, to nobody, that she was a grown woman of thirty-two who had lived abroad alone for four years and was not afraid of a machine. Then they went over the top and she screamed the whole way round, a long, high, continuous Osaka scream that Lily said "had different chapters". When the car rolled back into the station she was laughing so hard she couldn't get the lap bar off. "Again," she said. "Immediately. Before I remember to be scared."

So they did it again. Then Superman Escape, which launches you from a standstill to a hundred kilometres an hour in about two seconds in the dark, and on which she screamed so loudly at the launch that the ride attendant clapped when they came back. Then the smaller spinning rides, the Scooby-Doo coaster in the dark (she screamed at the ghosts and then apologised to them), and the wet log ride, which soaked all three of them so thoroughly that Mizuki said it was "USJ 2001 all over again", the Jurassic Park ride, her best day as an eleven-year-old, and was suddenly so happy she had to sit down on a bench in her wet trousers and just look at everything for a minute.

Nana asked her what she was thinking. She said she was thinking about her eleven-year-old self, soaked at USJ with Daisuke and her family, and about her ten-year-old self crying on Elf at Hirakata Park, and that if she could tell both of them one thing, it would be that one day they would be screaming on a roller coaster in Queensland and loving every second of it. "Also, I would tell them not to buy the felt hat in 2010."

Movie World had characters walking about, and Mizuki, who was cheeky about everything American, decided she disliked Batman ("too serious, needs a snack") and was very fond of a person in a Daffy Duck costume who bowed to her when she bowed to him, which she counted as a cultural exchange. Lily photographed her with Wonder Woman, standing exactly like Sailor Mercury, and that photo is still on her fridge.

**Sea World**, the next day, was gentler and, she says, even better. It sits on the Spit at Main Beach, with the Broadwater on one side and the sea on the other, and it felt to her like a cross between an aquarium and a summer festival. She thought immediately of the Churaumi Aquarium in Okinawa, where she and Aya had stood in front of the whale sharks on their graduation trip in 2013, sunburnt and silent. Sea World had no whale sharks, but it had dolphins, sea lions, penguins and polar bears, and Mizuki spent much longer than anyone needed to in front of the polar bears, who were sleeping in a heap "like Dad on his day off". She photographed the dolphins in their show and then stopped photographing and just watched, because "some things you should look at with your face".

They rode the coasters there too. Jet Rescue launches you forward on a little jet-ski-shaped car, and Mizuki screamed. The Storm Coaster is a water coaster that ends with a splash, and Mizuki screamed and got soaked again, and decided that the purpose of Queensland was to make her wet in a different way every day. Even the gentle rides got a scream, more out of habit by then. By the afternoon of the second day Lily had a whole folder of videos titled "Mizuki screams (complete works)", and played them at the shop for weeks.

What she loved most about the trip, she said afterwards, wasn't any single ride. It was screaming as loudly as she wanted in a place where screaming was the point. Mizuki had spent most of her adult life being polite for a living, first at a hotel front desk in Umeda and then at a ramen counter, smiling at people who were rude, apologising for things that weren't her fault. On the Gold Coast, for two days, she was allowed to be as loud as an Osaka obachan at a bargain sale, and nobody minded, and two friends screamed with her. "It was very healthy. Like a sauna for the voice."

**Food and evenings.** They ate cheaply and happily: fish and chips, a Korean barbecue place in Surfers that Lily approved of with reservations, gelato on the esplanade, and on the last night, at Nana's insistence, a "fancy" seafood dinner where Mizuki ordered a plate of local prawns and Moreton Bay bugs, the flat, slightly prehistoric-looking crustaceans that are a Queensland favourite. She had never eaten one. She declared it "a lobster that has been sat on" and then ate the lot. In the evenings they sat on the apartment balcony on the twenty-something floor, with the lights of the coast below them, and played cards and talked: about Lily's degree and what she'd do next, about Nana's salon and her plan to have her own one day, and about home. Nana missed Kobe. Lily had grown up in Sydney but spent childhood summers with her grandmother in Seoul and missed that too, which surprised Mizuki and made her love her a bit more. Mizuki said she hadn't seen her parents' faces anywhere but a screen in four and a half years, and that she was starting to forget what the shop smelled like. Nobody said anything clever. Nana put her head on her shoulder.

On the plane home, sunburnt on one ear (the one place the bucket hat missed), Mizuki looked at the videos of herself screaming and thought it was the happiest she had looked in two years. She sent the best one to her parents. Her mother replied with a long voice message laughing. Her father replied: "Loud." She has decided, on reflection, that this was a compliment.

### 11 October 2022: the storeroom

Two weeks after the Gold Coast, the news she had been waiting for since March 2020 finally came. Japan had been reopening by stages through 2022, slowly and cautiously, the way Japan does things, but on 11 October it opened its doors properly again: visa-free visits back, the daily arrival caps gone, the package-tour-only rules gone. For a Japanese citizen like Mizuki, going home had been technically possible for a while, but with quarantine and testing at both ends, scarce flights and rules that changed every few weeks, it had always felt like trying to catch a train that kept moving. Now the country was open, and the flights were coming back, and the thing she had stopped letting herself think about was suddenly real.

She heard it on a Tuesday, at work, from the shop TV with the sound off: a short item with pictures of tourists arriving at Haneda and a headline about Japan's reopening. She read it twice. Then she said, very calmly, "I'm just getting more napkins," walked into the storeroom at the back of the shop, closed the door, sat down on a sack of flour among the boxes of noodles and the cans of bamboo shoots, and cried for about five minutes, the way you cry when something you didn't know you were carrying gets put down.

It wasn't sad crying, exactly. It was everything at once: the cancelled flight for her thirtieth birthday in April 2020, the photo she had taken of the cancellation email, Kenta's wedding watched on a phone propped against the shiso pot, two lockdowns alone in a small flat, every Sunday call where her father had looked a little greyer and she had pretended not to notice. It all came out in the storeroom, between the noodles and the bamboo shoots.

Then she wiped her face on a clean shop towel, folded it properly, put it in the laundry bag, picked up a stack of napkins she didn't need, went back out and served three tables as if nothing had happened. Lily noticed her eyes and said nothing until the rush was over, then gave her a can of green tea and said, "Go home soon, unnie." Mr Kondo noticed too. He hummed for the rest of the afternoon, the folk song he hummed when he was moved and didn't want to say so, and at closing time he said, "When you book, give me the dates. I'll do the roster." He meant: go.

She didn't book that night. She was too frightened of the price, and of wanting it too much. She rang her mother instead. Yumiko had seen the news and had already, she said, "started cleaning your room, which is a lot of work because you never cleaned it". Her father was in the background. Mizuki asked to talk to him. There was a pause. Then Hiroshi's voice, flat as ever: "Spring is good. The cherry blossoms." And then, after another pause, "Don't come in the Golden Week. Too many people." It was, by his standards, a long speech about how much he missed her.

### Booking the ticket

She booked the flight in November, on a Sunday night, after the video call, with a cup of hojicha and the Zoom window still open in another tab as if her parents could watch. She chose to land at Kansai at the end of March and fly back towards the end of April, so she would be home for the cherry blossoms and for her thirty-third birthday on 10 April: five years after she had left with one suitcase. The fare was more than she had ever paid for a flight in her life, because everyone in the world was flying again at once. She paid it immediately, before she could think, then lay on the floor for ten minutes.

Then she told everyone. Aya screamed down the phone and then said, "Fine. I'll take two days off. You're paying for takoyaki." Kenta sent a thumbs-up and a photo of the window of the spare room at their flat, without words, which Miho explained a few minutes later meant "you can stay with us any night you like". Daisuke messaged from Kobe: "The audience returns." Mr Kondo wrote the dates on the roster in red pen and drew a small aeroplane next to them, the first drawing anyone had ever seen him make.

Then she made lists, as any Tanaka would: people to see, in order of who would be most offended to be second; food to eat, two pages, beginning with her father's pork and kimchi okonomiyaki; presents, priced, because her grandmother Chiyo would ask. On bad days at the shop she opened her journal in the storeroom, read "Kuromon, grilled scallop, standing up", and felt better at once.

### Small stings of 2022

Not everything that year was rain and roller coasters.

In April, on the bus home, she scrolled past a photo on Instagram of Takuya, her boyfriend of five years until 2017, at a small shrine wedding in Kyoto, in a black formal kimono beside a bride in white. Nobody had told her, and she had no right to be told. She put the phone away, went home, sat in the one balcony chair with a tub of matcha ice cream and ate the whole thing slowly while the light went. The next morning she pressed "like", and that was the end of it. "He looked happy. Good. It was a small sting, like a mosquito. You notice it, you don't die." Aya offered to be rude about his haircut. Mizuki said yes, a little, and it helped.

In June her mother told her that Chiyo had had a small fall at home: no broken bones, no lasting harm, but she had lain on the floor of the bonsai house for a while before a neighbour heard her, and lately she had been muddling her days. The family agreed on a care home in Abeno, fifteen minutes from the shop. Chiyo chose it herself, after comparing three on price, and moved in with her soroban in the bedside drawer. It was the first time Mizuki properly understood that her family were getting older while she wasn't there to see it.

### Waiting (late 2022 to March 2023)

Lily graduated from UTS in 2022, and Mizuki took the afternoon off to photograph her in her gown on the FM2. Mizuki gave her a card in Korean that took an hour and had two mistakes; Lily kept it, mistakes and all. **Fiona** adopted Haggis that year, a fat, grumpy, ten-year-old ginger rescue who hated men with beards and the sound of Mizuki's shutter; Mizuki said he looked "like a mayor who has been caught doing something". On Saturdays Nana and Mizuki learned LE SSERAFIM's "ANTIFRAGILE", Nana pointing out that Kazuha is from Osaka "so even you can be proud". Mizuki could not do the hips. She could do the face.

Christmas 2022 was lunch at Fiona's with Nana and Lily, Haggis on top of the fridge, and the giddy knowledge that next year the borders wouldn't matter. New Year was toshikoshi soba on the balcony and Fiona singing "Auld Lang Syne" down the phone at midnight: the "noodles and singing" New Year, as they call it. On Burns Night in January she tried Fiona's haggis and called it "a Scottish menchi katsu that has been through something".

In March, the shop TV gave her one more thing to connect with going home. Japan played the United States in the final of the World Baseball Classic in Miami, which in Sydney was a weekday morning during prep. Mr Kondo, who keeps a framed photo of Shohei Ohtani in his Nippon-Ham Fighters uniform in the staff room, turned the sound on for the first time in the shop's history. When Ohtani struck out Mike Trout, his own Angels teammate, for the final out, Mr Kondo stood perfectly still with his ladle and then went into the storeroom for a minute. Her father sent a tiger emoji, though the Tigers had nothing to do with it. "In Osaka it's all Tigers. Even when it's not."

The night before she flew, Nana painted Hanshin Tigers stripes on her nails, yellow and black, for her father. It has been a tradition before every trip home since. Hiroshi has never commented on them. He has looked. On the morning of the flight, with the suitcase by the door, she stood in front of the little shelf on her bookshelf, changed the water, and clapped twice, as she now does before every flight home, the old Sumiyoshi travel charm swinging from her backpack zip. The Vulpix keyring had long since retired to the FM2's strap; the charm still travelled.

### Late March 2023: "You're late"

She flew to Kansai at the end of March 2023, five years and two weeks after she had left, with a suitcase full mostly of presents: Tim Tams for everyone, macadamias for Chiyo, Vegemite for Kenta as a long-running joke, honey for her mother, a terrible Opera House fridge magnet for Aya, and a bottle of Australian beer for her father, who would call it "thin". She set Isamu's watch to Japan time over the sea, as he would have.

Japan still wanted arrivals to show a vaccination certificate that spring, as a QR code on an app; she had filled it in four times and went through so smoothly she felt cheated of a drama. Then the Haruka express, Hello Kitty painted on its side, straight to Tennoji. She photographed Osaka through the window, badly, with her face too close to the glass.

It was an early year for the blossoms, and they were at their best. She walked from the station through the backstreets, past the Shitennoji wall where she had broken her arm in 1999, and under the shop's noren at five in the afternoon. Her mother looked up from the till and said, as if Mizuki had popped out for milk, "You're late." Then she went into the back for a minute, and everyone pretended not to hear. Her father said nothing at all. He nodded once and started a pork and kimchi okonomiyaki, and put it in front of her on the third seat from the left, her seat since she was small enough to need a cushion on it. She ate it with the little metal spatula straight off the hotplate, burnt her tongue and didn't care. "Not the best okonomiyaki of my life. The best meal. There's a difference."

After closing she sat at the counter while he scraped the hotplate and noticed what the screen had hidden: he moved more slowly, and pressed a hand into the small of his back when he thought nobody was looking. Upstairs her old futon smelled of the sun, because her mother had aired it that morning. She lay on it listening to the trains and her father's radio playing Southern All Stars very quietly downstairs, and slept for eleven hours.

### A month at home

She had four weeks, and used them the way Fumiko had taught her to use the market: slowly, tasting everything.

**Chiyo** came first, on her first full day, as she has on every trip since. Her grandmother, upright in a lavender cardigan with a plum-blossom brooch, knew her at once, which Mizuki had been afraid she wouldn't. "Are you eating? You're not eating." Then: "How much was the flight?" Mizuki told her. Chiyo said nothing for a long time, and then: "You should have come by boat." She called the macadamias a robbery and ate six.

At **Kuromon**, full of tourists now, she ate a grilled scallop with a little soy standing up in the street, for Fumiko's sake, with her eyes closed.

**Sumiyoshi** she did with her mother, on a Wednesday when the shop was shut, on the Hankai tram. Yumiko had insisted: you go back to give thanks when the big unlucky year is over, even if there's a small one still to come. Mizuki crossed the drum bridge without being carried, for the first time with her mother watching, washed her hands at the basin (left, right, mouth, ladle upright), put a hundred-yen coin in the offering box and bowed, clapped and bowed. Then she untied the kōtsū anzen charm her mother had given her at Kansai in 2018, faded from five Sydney summers, and dropped it in the box for old charms to be burned, and Yumiko bought her a new one and tied it on herself, with a double knot. Mizuki drew an omikuji. It said **chūkichi**, middling good luck, and went into her wallet.

**Mr Ishida**, slower on his feet now, looked at her over his counter near Namba and said, "You've got old." Then he gave her a free egg, which from Mr Ishida is a hug. His New Year card had said "Knees bad. Soup good." She could confirm both.

**Aya** took two days off, as promised. They spent an afternoon under the cherry trees at Osaka Castle with a blue sheet, cans of chu-hi, Mame the shiba looking at Mizuki as if she owed her money in person now, and a boat of takoyaki eaten too fast. "Video calls are fine," said Aya with her mouth full, "but you can't share takoyaki over the internet." That evening they saw *Suzume* in Namba, Makoto Shinkai's film about a girl closing magic doors to stop earthquakes. When the phone earthquake alert sounded on screen, Mizuki grabbed Aya's arm hard enough to leave a mark and thought of the kotatsu in 1995. They both cried at the end; Aya denied it.

**Kenta, and Miho by video.** Two days after she landed, Miho came down with the influenza that was going round Osaka out of season that spring, so Kenta came to the shop alone. By the time Miho was better it was April, the new nursery year, with new children crying from eight till six, and then Kenta caught it too. So Mizuki and her sister-in-law lived ten minutes apart for a month and met only on video calls, which they agreed was "the most Tanaka thing possible". Miho sent a tin of her melon bread to the shop "for Onee-san". They would not meet in person for another two years. **Daisuke** came over from Kobe one Sunday with his two sons and said, "The audience returns."

**Her birthday.** On 10 April she turned thirty-three at home for the first time since 2017. The blossoms had fallen, and the trees were in fresh leaf, hazakura, which she says she prefers, "because everyone has gone home and the trees can relax". Her father closed early and her mother sang "Akai Sweet Pea" at the counter without being asked. Her father gave her a roll of black-and-white film and said nothing about it. She shot it all in Abeno that week: the noren, her mother's hands at the till, her father's back bent over the hotplate.

**What had changed.** Osaka was full of tourists again, far more than in her hotel days. Masks had become "a personal choice" in mid-March, and nearly everybody had personally chosen to keep wearing one, which she found very Japanese and rather moving. Out in the bay, Yumeshima was a building site for the Expo. The things that hadn't changed she loved more than ever: trains to the minute, konbini onigiri, Dotonbori neon on a wet night, and shopkeepers saying "maido" and "ookini". In Amemura she bought a faded navy work jacket and a mustard wool beret that her mother said "makes you look like a curry". She still wears it every winter.

**Going.** She flew back in the last week of April, before Golden Week, as ordered. At Kansai her mother cried and then said, "Go on, you'll be late," which was the right ochi. Her father, who had driven them over the long bridge in the shop van as he had in 2018, handed her a cloth bundle with a bottle of the shop's okonomiyaki sauce inside. "For the miso man. For looking after you." That was the start of the gift exchange between two men who have never met: every year a bottle of sauce goes to Sydney, and a box of Shiroi Koibito biscuits from Hokkaido goes back to Abeno. Hiroshi's review so far: "Good biscuit."

One small loss: her mother had hidden a vacuum-sealed pack of pork miso at the bottom of the suitcase. Mizuki found it while filling in the arrival card, declared it, and watched a kind biosecurity officer at Sydney airport put it in the bin, because Australia does not let pork products in. "I know," she said, "but it was my mother's."

### May 2023: Dad's back

She had been back less than three weeks when it happened, in the way it was always going to. Early one morning in May, Hiroshi lifted a crate of cabbages off the delivery van himself, as he had every morning since he was a teenager in his father's shop, and as Yumiko had told him not to for nearly as long, and something in his lower back went "like a bad chopstick". A slipped disc. For a few days he could hardly stand. Kenta drove him to the clinic.

The shop closed for two weeks, the longest closure in its history. Yumiko ran it like a military operation: a handwritten sign on the shutter, a phone call to every regular, the hotplate oiled every other day so it wouldn't sulk, and Hiroshi flat on a futon upstairs watching the Tigers on a television propped on a chair. Mizuki heard about it two days late, because her parents had decided not to worry her, and was furious, then guilty, then helpless, which was worst. She had only just come home from home, and now she was eight thousand kilometres away again exactly when it mattered. Her father came on the phone once, lying down, holding it above his face, and said, "Ceiling," by way of explanation.

So she did the one thing she could do from Sydney: she sent money home, a real amount, from her savings for the next trip. Her mother accepted it "as a loan", which they both knew it wasn't. Part of it bought a thick standing mat for the floor in front of the hotplate, which her father had refused for twenty years because his own father never had one. He calls it "Mizuki's mat" and stands on it every day. He went back to work at the end of the month, slower, with a back brace under his apron that he denies. Since then his back has had good weeks and bad ones, and Kenta drives him to the Wakayama rocks on his fishing days and sleeps in the van. Mizuki says she owes her brother for 2023. He sends a slime emoji.

Mr Kondo, who had heard her side of every phone call in the quiet hour, hummed old enka for a week and then gave her two extra shifts in June without a word. "Fathers," he said once, to the soup. "They lift the cabbages."

### Winter 2023

Her work visa, the one Mr Kondo had sponsored in 2019, ran out that year, and there was another round of forms, another envelope of documents from Osaka covered in her mother's handwriting, and another bill that Mr Kondo paid and never mentioned. She keeps the details light. By the end of winter it was done, and she baked him a castella, badly. He ate all of it and said "namara umai", which she knows was a lie and treasures.

In July Nana turned twenty-seven and, because she says yes to everything, jumped out of an aeroplane over the beach at Wollongong, while Mizuki stood on the sand holding her shoes and looking up at a dot. Nana's partner Ryan, a quiet electrician from the Central Coast, fixed Mizuki's flickering balcony light that winter and would take no payment but a Sunday okonomiyaki. From August, Lily taught her Korean on Wednesdays in exchange for okonomiyaki; Mizuki skipped one week in three for her lazy night, and Lily always noticed. From São Paulo, Rafa and Bia sent a photo of their newborn son, Theo, in a tiny Corinthians shirt and a tiny Palmeiras hat, because they still couldn't agree.

In August the whole country went mad for the Women's World Cup. Mizuki, who says Australia has too many football codes, understood this one at once, because it was a story. The shop TV showed the Matildas' quarter-final against France go to a penalty shootout so long that customers stood up with their noodles going cold. When the Matildas lost the semi-final to England in Sydney, all of World Square seemed to sigh at once. "The first time I felt the whole of Australia feel one thing," she wrote. "Like the Tigers. But tall."

### Games, music and films

In Final Fantasy XIV the years after Endwalker were gentler. In August 2022 the game added Island Sanctuary, a little island where you grow vegetables and keep animals, and Mizuki fell on it like a woman on holiday: a pumpkin patch, a row of cabbages "for Dad" and a fat white sheep called Hiroshi. Raid nights stayed on Tuesdays and Thursdays at nine; in Osaka she raided on a borrowed laptop from her old bedroom, and the FC said it was the first time she had ever been early. At the end of July 2023 the next expansion, *Dawntrail*, was announced for the following summer: a new continent of sunshine and jungle. When the trailer ended, Kazu said, very calmly, "Holiday."

*Final Fantasy XVI* came out on the PS5 on 22 June 2023, and she took the day off. She loved the music, the summons fighting like kaiju and Torgal, the hero's wolf. But it was far darker and bloodier than any Final Fantasy before, and she played much of it through her fingers, which Kenta spotted at once on video: "Nee-chan, fingers?" Her verdict: "A very good game that forgot to be a little bit silly."

Her music followed the trip. In late 2022 she and Aya watched the drama *Silent* together across the time zones, and its theme, Official Hige Dandism's "Subtitle", became her song for rainy walks over Pyrmont Bridge. TWICE's "Set Me Free" came out in March 2023, just before she flew, and she played it until Lily threatened a second ban. And YOASOBI's "Idol", the opening of the anime *Oshi no Ko*, came out in April while she was in Osaka and was suddenly everywhere, in every konbini and every phone, so that it still smells to her of melon bread and cherry leaves. Lily, meanwhile, declared NewJeans the future. Mizuki said TWICE were the past, the present and the future. "Unnie, no."

She watched *Spy x Family* faithfully and refused *Chainsaw Man*, which began in October 2022 and which Lily loved; five minutes was enough. "I like ghosts. I don't like inside-out." In July 2023 Lily dragged her and Nana to *Barbie*, all three in pink, Mizuki in a borrowed cardigan and an expression of suffering; she laughed so loudly a man turned round, and said afterwards that Ken reminded her of a type of customer: "The type who explains ramen to me." From Japan came reports of Miyazaki's *The Boy and the Heron*, released in July with no trailer at all. Her mother went with Kenta and reported: "Very beautiful. Very confusing. The heron is rude."

### Clothes in these years

Her style didn't change so much as settle. Off work it was the uniform her friends could draw from memory: the oversized vintage denim jacket, wide-leg trousers, a white T-shirt, a beret, Isamu's watch. "No logo bigger than my hand." These years added the raincoat and boots, the bucket hat, the curry-coloured beret, and a long grey wool coat from a Newtown op shop for eight dollars, which she considers the bargain of her life and which her mother, on the video call, asked the price of twice.

There was one style disaster. A week before the trip home she went to a new hairdresser in the city, asked for "a little shorter, neat, for my mother", and came out with a short, very straight bob and a fringe "like a Lego person". Lily said it was "very NewJeans". At the shop door in Abeno her mother said "you're late" first and "what happened to your hair?" second, which Mizuki says shows her priorities are correct. It grew.

### Autumn 2023: A.R.E.

All through 2023 her father's Hanshin Tigers were, for once, very good. Their manager, Akinobu Okada, banned the word for championship so as not to jinx it, and called it only "are", "that thing", written A.R.E., and soon all of Osaka was saying it, except her father, who wouldn't even write it. In mid-September the Tigers won the Central League pennant, their first since 2005, against the Giants at Koshien; people jumped into the Dotonbori canal, as Osaka people do. Her father sent one tiger emoji. She sent one back. That is their whole conversation when the Tigers win, and it is perfect.

September also brought the announcement of a camera, a new Nikon that looked almost exactly like the FM2, and a noise from Mizuki in the staff room that Lily has imitated ever since; she did not buy it until February, and that story belongs to the next year. And it brought a photo from Kenta, no words, of a tiny pair of white socks on the counter of his flat. Mizuki looked at it for a full minute, thinking her brother had taken up an alarming hobby, before she understood, rang him and screamed. "Yes. Yes. April. Yes. Please stop." From then on the family group chat had only one subject.

Then the Japan Series: the Tigers against Orix, Osaka's other team, the first all-Kansai series since 1964, the year Isamu opened the shop. It went to seven games. On the night of the seventh, a Sunday in early November, Mizuki watched on a tilted video call from the Pyrmont balcony while her parents watched in the shop with the regulars: her mother shouting, the regulars on their feet, and, at the last out, the Tigers champions of Japan for the first time since 1985, her father standing very still behind the hotplate with his arms folded, and then slowly holding up the towel. He said almost nothing, "which for him was screaming". Daisuke, a Kobe man with a soft spot for Orix, sent him a photo of a white flag. Then Mizuki rang back to tell him he had waited thirty-eight years "so I would be in the wrong country for it". He said, "Mada mada," and hung up. In December "A.R.E." was named Japan's buzzword of the year.

### Christmas 2023

With the Christmas bonus Mr Kondo always pretends is from Megumi she bought her grandmother a soft grey merino scarf and posted it to the care home; Chiyo wore it for years and told everyone what it cost. Christmas Day was at Fiona's again, with Nana's glitter crackers, Haggis on the fridge and a strawberry shortcake on Mizuki's knees on the bus. New Year was noodles and singing, and at midnight a message from her mother, two hours behind in Osaka: "You're early."

She thought, at the end of it, that 2022 had been the year of waiting and 2023 the year of going home. Before the trip, Sydney had been where she lived while she waited to go back. After it, Osaka was not somewhere she had lost but somewhere she could go, and Sydney was somewhere she had chosen. "I tied the rope back on," she says. "Now I can swim."
