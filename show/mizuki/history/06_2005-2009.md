## Chapter 6: The camera in the cupboard (2005 to early 2009)

### Spring 2005: the watch and the empty corner of the counter

The spring of 2005 began with a gap in the shop. Grandpa Isamu had died suddenly that winter, and for weeks afterwards everyone kept glancing at the far end of the counter, where he used to sit on seat one every morning from ten, long after he'd handed the hotplate to Hiroshi, reading the sports paper and telling customers their cabbage was cut too thick. Nobody moved the stool. Hiroshi wiped it every night with the same cloth he used on the counter, as if Isamu might walk in at ten the next morning and complain about the batter.

Mizuki was fourteen, and she turned fifteen on 10 April that year. It was a quiet birthday, a strawberry shortcake from the Kintetsu department store and her mother saying "you're late" when she came in from school, exactly as usual, which Mizuki found more comforting than any speech would have been. On her wrist, under her cardigan cuff, was Isamu's **Seiko**, the "old man's watch" Chiyo had put in her hands a few weeks after the funeral, still too heavy, still running two minutes slow a week. On exam days she still turns it round on her wrist three times before she starts, a habit that began that spring.

Grandma **Chiyo**, seventy that year, did not fall apart. She was not the falling-apart sort. She moved into the routine of a widow with the same efficiency she brought to the shop's accounts, which she had kept in pencil for thirty years before handing the daily figures to Yumiko. Every Sunday afternoon she came round with her abacus and the ledger and went through the week's takings with Yumiko at the kitchen table, tutting at the cost of eggs. "Your grandfather gave away too many free beers," she told Mizuki. "Your father gives away too many free okonomiyaki. Somebody in this family has to count." Mizuki, who was hopeless at maths, sat next to her and was allowed to read out the numbers. It was the closest she ever felt to Chiyo, and she came to like the clack of the abacus beads the way she liked the snap of her flip phone.

The shop itself carried on. That is the Osaka way, and the Tanaka way. The regulars came in, some with envelopes of condolence money, some with nothing but a pat on Hiroshi's shoulder, and ordered what they always ordered. One old man, a retired tram worker who had known Grandpa Shigeru as well, came in every day for two weeks and ate his pork tama in silence, then left a little extra on the counter. Yumiko never told him off for it. Hiroshi said almost nothing about his father, then or later, but Mizuki noticed he started humming Southern All Stars at the hotplate again some time in May, and she took that as the end of the worst of it.

### The last year of junior high

April 2005 started her third and final year of junior high in Abeno, the year everyone in Japan calls *juken*, the exam year. Third-years were supposed to be serious. Mizuki managed serious for about two weeks.

The May school trip to Tokyo, Disneyland churros and all, was over almost before it had begun, and by June it had become a story she told rather than a place she'd been.

The rest of the year was cram school. Like most of her class she went three evenings a week to a *juku* above a pachinko parlour near Tennoji station, where a tired young man in a cardigan taught maths with a whiteboard marker that was always running out. She sat with Aya at the back and passed notes written in kaomoji. Mizuki's English was good, her Japanese essays were good, and her maths was, in the juku teacher's words, "brave". On one practice test she scored 31 out of 100 and drew a small crying face next to the score before her mother could. Yumiko looked at it, looked at the face, and said, "At least you're honest," which from Yumiko was nearly praise.

She stayed in the **art club** all year, mostly because Aya was there and the art room had the best heater in the school. Her drawings were still average. Her still lifes were not. The art teacher, the elderly woman in the grey cardigan with paint under her nails, kept saying she was good at *choosing*: where to put the apple, what to leave out, which way the light came from. She once spent an entire club afternoon moving a single persimmon around a white cloth while everyone else drew, and the teacher let her, because the final arrangement was better than anything anyone drew. Nobody said the word "photographer" yet, but it was in the room.

That autumn the **Hanshin Tigers** won the Central League pennant again, clinching it at Koshien at the end of September 2005, only two years after the 2003 miracle. Hiroshi was less speechless this time and more nervous, because a pennant meant the Japan Series, and the Japan Series meant hope, which he distrusted. He was right to. In late October the Tigers met the Chiba Lotte Marines and lost four games straight, by a combined score of **33 to 4**. The number became a national joke, and in Osaka a wound. During the third game Hiroshi turned the shop television off at the seventh inning and said to the room, "Mada mada" ("not yet, not yet"), and the regulars nodded solemnly as if he'd said something profound. "33 to 4" became a family phrase for any total disaster: a burnt okonomiyaki, a failed maths test, a typhoon on a festival day. Mizuki still uses it, and has tried to explain it to Scott more than once.

At New Year 2006 she collected her otoshidama, the envelopes of New Year money, and did something unusual: she didn't spend it. She was saving it for **Final Fantasy XII**, which was due out in March, and she made a deal with herself that she could only buy it if she passed her high school entrance exam. The exams came in March in the cold. She turned the Seiko round three times, wrote her name very neatly, and did reasonably at everything except maths. When she found her number on the results board at the public high school she'd wanted, a solid school with a good reputation and a long walk from the station, Aya's number was two rows above hers. They hugged, jumped, and were told off by a teacher for jumping.

She bought FFXII that same week at Den Den Town and spent the spring holiday in Ivalice. She liked it more than she later admitted. **Balthier**, the sky pirate who kept calling himself the leading man, made her laugh out loud, and she thought **Fran**, his tall, silver-haired partner, was the most elegant character in any game she'd played. She was less sure about the battles, which ran themselves if you set up the "Gambits" correctly: "It's like being the manager of a shop instead of cooking." There was also an enormous amount of walking. She finished it, eventually, in the summer, and filed it as "beautiful, but a long commute". Kenta, twelve, refused to touch it on principle. He was a **Dragon Quest** boy and had been playing Dragon Quest VIII since 2004 on the same save file, which he guarded like a family heirloom.

Junior high graduation was in March 2006. Mizuki's sailor uniform went into a plastic bag in the cupboard. She cried at the graduation song, which she had sworn she wouldn't, and Aya, who had sworn the same, pretended her eyes were watering because of the gym's heater.

### April 2006: two new uniforms

April 2006 was a double first in the Tanaka house. Mizuki started high school and Kenta started junior high, on almost the same morning, and for one week the upstairs flat smelled of new fabric and nerves. Kenta's uniform was a black gakuran with a stand-up collar, a size too big "to grow into", so his hands disappeared inside the sleeves. Mizuki's was a **navy blazer**, a grey pleated skirt, a white shirt and a ribbon tie in dark red, which she considered a great improvement on the sailor collar. Yumiko made them stand together in front of the shop for a photograph, and Hiroshi took it with the shop's compact camera, a silver Canon Yumiko used for New Year cards. In the photo Kenta looks resentful and Mizuki looks as if she's trying not to laugh. Both are true to life.

Mizuki's style rules for the uniform were established within the month. A **pastel cardigan** under the blazer from October, sleeves pulled over her hands. Navy knee socks, never the slouchy white loose socks of the previous decade, which she now considered "very 2002". The skirt rolled once at the waist, never twice, because Yumiko checked at the door every morning like a station guard. Aya rolled hers twice and unrolled it on the stairs when she came round.

The new school was bigger, further and more serious. Mizuki took the train and walked, and the walk became her thinking time. There were more students from all over the south of Osaka, girls from Sakai who said everything slightly differently and boys from Hirano who were already obsessed with university. There were also, for the first time in her life, real clubs with real equipment. In the first week, during club recruiting, the corridors were lined with tables: brass band, kendo, the tea ceremony club handing out sweets, the baseball team shouting at passers-by like fish sellers at Kuromon. Aya was leaning towards the **brass band** (she would play the trumpet, loudly and well, for three years). Mizuki had meant to join the art club again. Then she stopped at a small table at the end of a corridor where nobody was shouting at all.

The **photography club** had one handwritten sign and a cork board of black-and-white prints. One print stopped her: a cat asleep on a bicycle seat in the rain, drops of water on the handlebars, the cat completely unbothered. It was the calmest picture she had ever seen. Behind the table sat a small woman in her forties dressed entirely in black, sleeves pushed up, a silver camera round her neck, eating a rice ball. "Do you have a camera?" the woman asked. Mizuki said no. "Do you want to look at things for a long time without anyone asking why?" Mizuki said yes. "Then you're in," said the woman, and wrote her name down. That was **Ms Kuroda**.

### The camera in the cupboard

At dinner that night, over a plate of yakisoba her father had made with leftover cabbage, Mizuki mentioned she'd joined the photography club and that she'd need a camera, "but a cheap one, a club one, it's fine". Yumiko started to say something about money. Hiroshi put his chopsticks down, stood up without a word and went upstairs.

They heard the hall cupboard open, the one with the winter futons and the box of New Year decorations. There was the sound of something heavy being moved. Then he came back down with a scuffed **brown leather case** and put it on the table between the bowls, like a dish he wasn't sure anyone would order.

Inside was a **Nikon FM2**, black, heavier than it looked, with a 50mm lens and a few chips in the paint at the corners. Hiroshi had bought it in **1982**, when he was twenty-four, the first month Isamu paid him a proper wage instead of pocket money and dinner. Mizuki had not known it existed. Neither, it turned out, had Kenta. Yumiko had known, and said nothing, because that was between Hiroshi and his cupboard.

"The light meter still works," Hiroshi said. "I put a new battery in it last year. In case." He tapped the top of the camera. "The rest is up to you." Then he sat down and finished his yakisoba.

Mizuki says it was the longest speech about feelings he made during her entire teenage life, and she has spent twenty years unpacking the "in case". He had put a new battery in a camera he hadn't used for nearly two decades, the year before his daughter joined a photography club she hadn't yet heard of. Either her father could see the future, or he'd been hoping.

Later that week, when she asked, he told her a little more. In his twenties he'd taken it fishing off the rocks in Wakayama, and to Koshien for the Tigers, and he had thought, for a while, that he might be someone who took pictures. Then the shop got busy and his hands smelled of batter all day, and the camera went into the cupboard. There was a shoebox of his old prints, which he let her see once: the sea at Shirahama, rocks, a lot of out-of-focus baseball players, and three pictures of a young woman with an eighties perm laughing at whoever was holding the camera. "That's your mother," he said, unnecessarily. "Don't tell her I showed you." She told her mother immediately. Yumiko hit him with the menu and then kept one of the prints in her purse.

The FM2 is entirely manual. There is no autofocus and no screen. You set the shutter speed with a dial on the top and the aperture with a ring on the lens, you focus by turning the lens until the split circle in the middle of the viewfinder lines up, and you wind on with a lever after every shot. The only electronics are the light meter, three tiny red lights in the viewfinder: a plus, a circle and a minus. When only the circle glows, the exposure is right. Mizuki called them "the traffic lights" and learned to read them without thinking. The shutter is mechanical and works without the battery at all, which her father pointed out with quiet pride: "It'll outlive both of us."

Each roll of film gave her thirty-six chances, and a roll of black-and-white film cost about four hundred yen at the camera shop near Tennoji station, which on a high school allowance was a lot. So she learned patience. Wait for the blink to finish. Wait for the tram to cross. Wait for the cloud to move off the sun. She still hates what she calls "machine-gun shooting", firing off a hundred frames of one thing and hoping, and calls it "fishing with dynamite". Her father, who fished with a single line off a single rock, agreed with this completely.

### Ms Kuroda and the club

Ms Kuroda taught art for her day job and photography for love. She was small, quick and wiry, with short grey-streaked hair she cut herself, black clothes every day and, in the darkroom, that famous chemical-stained **apron**, whose pocket held a light meter, three rolls of film, a cloth, a pencil, rubber gloves, a packet of salted plums and, once, memorably, a live cicada she had rescued from the darkroom. She had grown up in Nara and still said "Nara people are more elegant than Osaka people" in front of a room full of Osaka teenagers, purely to make them shout. She shot with an old silver Olympus OM-1 and printed beautifully.

The club had nine members in Mizuki's first year, which Ms Kuroda considered the perfect number: "enough for a darkroom rota, not enough for politics". There were two other first-years: Natsumi, a girl who photographed only her own feet in interesting places, and a tall, nervous boy called Shun who was mostly interested in trains. There were three second-years, a pair of best friends who did everything together including, somehow, the same photographs, and three third-years, among them a quiet boy who printed landscapes of the Yodo River at dawn. More on him later.

Ms Kuroda had rules, and she said them so often that the club could chant them.

- **"Light first. Then the face."** Before you look at the person, look at where the light is coming from.
- **"Walk closer. Then one more step."** Most bad photographs were taken from too far away by someone who was shy.
- **"Never shoot a stranger who says no."** If somebody shakes their head, you put the camera down, bow and thank them. No arguments.
- And the one that stuck hardest: **"Talk to them, then stop talking."** When you photograph a person, chat first so they relax, then go quiet and wait, because people only stop posing when they forget you are there.

When Mizuki repeated this last rule at dinner, very pleased with herself, Hiroshi turned an okonomiyaki and said, "Same with customers." It was, he explained in about nine words, how you got a regular to tell you what was actually wrong with their day: you asked one question, then you shut up and kept cooking. To this day Mizuki can't decide which of them taught her the rule. She says both of them did, and that it's the most useful thing she knows, in photography, in hospitality, and with Scott.

Ms Kuroda gave her first-years one assignment in April: a whole roll, thirty-six frames, of one street. Mizuki chose the **Abeno shopping street** near the shop. Her first contact sheet was mostly blur, sky and the backs of people's heads, plus one frame of the fishmonger laughing that was nearly good. Ms Kuroda circled the fishmonger with a red pencil and wrote, "This one. Why?" Mizuki thought about it for a week and decided it was because she'd known the fishmonger since she was four, and he had forgotten she had a camera. Ms Kuroda nodded. "Then you already know the whole secret. The rest is technique."

### The darkroom

The club shot cheap black-and-white film, mostly **Fujifilm Neopan 400** and **Kodak Tri-X**, and developed and printed it themselves in the school **darkroom**, a converted storeroom behind the science labs. It had a blacked-out window, a **red safelight**, two enlargers, a long sink with three trays and a cheap radio on a shelf, always on low. There was a hand-written sign on the door, "OPEN THIS AND DIE", in Ms Kuroda's handwriting, with a small cartoon skull she had drawn to look friendly.

The first thing Mizuki learned was how to load film onto a developing reel in total darkness, by feel, inside a light-proof bag, with her eyes shut because somehow that helped. She practised with a ruined roll at home in front of the television until she could do it in under a minute. The second thing she learned, in May 2006, was what happens if you open the back of an FM2 before you've rewound the film. She lost a whole roll of the Abeno street, including a second, better fishmonger. She went pale, shut the camera, and sat on the classroom floor. Shun the train boy, who had done the same thing the week before, patted her on the shoulder and said, "Everyone does it once." She has never done it again. Even now, with every camera she owns, she checks the rewind crank twice.

Developing was chemistry and timing: developer, then stop bath, then fixer, then a long wash, with the tank turned upside down every thirty seconds and a kitchen timer going off like an alarm. Printing was the magic. Under the enlarger she would expose a blank sheet of paper, slide it into the developer tray, and rock it gently under the red light. For a few seconds nothing happened. Then grey shapes swam up out of the white, and a face arrived. She never got tired of it. It felt like Fumiko's yokai stories, where something you couldn't see was in the room all along and only had to be waited for.

She loved the quiet. She loved the radio murmuring the evening news or a baseball game. And, oddly, she loved the smell, the sharp vinegar tang of the **stop bath**, which is mostly weak acetic acid. A bottle of rice vinegar opened in a kitchen still makes her a little nostalgic. She called the darkroom "like a temple, but with chemicals", and Ms Kuroda liked the line so much she wrote it on the door under the skull.

There were bad days. Prints came out muddy and flat, or so contrasty that faces turned into chalk masks. She once spilled fixer down the front of her school skirt and smelled of it for two days, and Aya made her sit at the far end of the bench at lunch. She learned about **dodging and burning**, holding back light from part of a print to lighten it, or giving one corner extra to darken it, using bits of card and her own hands. And she learned to read a contact sheet, the strip-by-strip proof of a whole roll, with a magnifying loupe, crossing out the failures without mercy. Ms Kuroda's verdict on the failures was always the same: "That's not a photograph. That's a receipt." It meant you'd only proved you had been somewhere. Mizuki still uses the phrase about other people's holiday pictures, and sometimes about her own.

### Love Letter on the "DVD player"

That autumn, in October 2006, Aya came round with a rental DVD in a blue plastic case and said, "You'll cry. I've already cried. Return it by Thursday." It was **Love Letter** (1995), Shunji Iwai's film about a woman in Kobe, still grieving her fiancé two years after he died in a mountain accident, who writes a letter to his old childhood address in **Otaru**, Hokkaido, a place that no longer exists, and impossibly gets a reply. The reply comes from a woman with exactly the same name as him, and slowly, through letters about a school library and a boy who borrowed books, a past nobody knew about comes to light.

Mizuki watched it alone on a Sunday night on the **PlayStation 2**, which, five years after her father had brought it home "for DVDs", finally did what it said on the box. She watched it twice more that week, once with Fumiko, who fell asleep and woke up for the last twenty minutes and cried harder than anyone. Snow, letters, a mystery, a library, old memories hidden inside the cards of library books, and a woman standing in a white valley shouting "**O-genki desu ka? Watashi wa genki desu!**" ("How are you? I'm fine!") to someone who can't answer. It went straight to the centre of her.

What was new was that, six months into the photography club, she could suddenly name *why* it looked so beautiful. The soft, low winter light. The pale palette, almost no strong colour. Faces lit from the side by a window, the way Ms Kuroda kept telling them to look for. Snow acting as a giant reflector and filling in every shadow. She took notes, which she has never done for any other film. It is still, to her, the most beautiful film ever made, and the reason the word "Otaru" makes her go soft in the middle. She returned the DVD a day late and paid the hundred-yen fine without complaint, because some things are worth a late fee.

### Amemura, vintage denim and the first beret

High school gave her a little more freedom on Sundays, and she spent it the Osaka way: on foot, on a budget, with Aya telling her honestly what looked terrible. They took the Midosuji line up to Shinsaibashi, walked the covered **Shinsaibashi-suji** arcade, then crossed the big road into **Amerika-mura**, "Amemura", the youth neighbourhood west of Midosuji full of second-hand shops, record shops, hairdressers with blue hair and teenagers sitting on the steps of **Triangle Park** (*Sankaku Kōen*) eating takoyaki and watching each other's outfits.

Amemura is where her style really started. Uniform was for weekdays. On Sundays, from 2006, she went for **vintage denim**: faded American jeans, a little too big, rolled at the ankle, and old denim jackets with soft collars that other people had already broken in. The first denim jacket she'd found in junior high had finally split along the back seam in the winter of 2005, and she replaced it in May 2006 with a heavier one from a basement shop that smelled of cedar and dust, 3,800 yen, the label so faded you couldn't read it. Her rule even then was that she didn't want a jacket that looked new; she wanted one that looked like it had already been somewhere interesting.

The **berets** began in the autumn of 2006. She saw a girl at Triangle Park in a dark red wool beret, a plain white T-shirt and wide jeans, and thought she looked like a photographer in an old French film, which, to a sixteen-year-old with a manual camera, was the highest possible compliment. Mizuki bought a navy beret the following weekend for 1,500 yen at a shop with a hand-painted sign. She wore it badly at first, flat on her head like a pancake, until Aya said, "You look like a mushroom," and showed her how to push it back and slightly to one side. Within a year she owned three: navy, black and a mustard one Aya called "the omelette". The beret, the denim jacket and the Seiko watch were the beginnings of the look she still has.

Not everything was a triumph. In the winter of 2007 she went through a short and much-regretted phase of very long, very thin scarves wound around her neck five times, which made her look, according to Kenta, "like a pencil wrapped in a bandage". She also bought a pair of white platform boots at a sale that were so high she nearly fell down the stairs at Shinsaibashi station, and she wore them exactly twice. Yumiko, who had worn platforms herself in the seventies, laughed until she had to sit down.

The Osaka obachan style, leopard print and a tiger on the jumper, she still admired from a safe distance: "Leopard is not a print, it is a personality." And she discovered, in Amemura's tiny record shops, that she liked flicking through things even more than buying them. Old LPs, vintage cameras in glass cases, second-hand film magazines. She'd spend a whole afternoon and buy one roll of film and a takoyaki. Aya called these "Mizuki's museum trips". They ended, nearly every time, with crepes on the steps of Triangle Park, comparing what they'd nearly bought.

### Senpai

There was a boy. He was one of the three third-years in the photography club, a quiet, long-faced boy with his shirt always buttoned to the top, who printed beautiful, empty landscapes of the **Yodo River** at dawn: mist, power lines, a single heron, a lot of grey sky. He hardly spoke. When he did, it was usually to say something about developer temperature.

In the summer of 2006 he showed Mizuki how to lighten a shadow in a print using a small circle of card taped to a bent piece of wire, a *dodging tool*, moving it gently over the paper under the enlarger so the edges didn't show. He stood next to her in the red light and said, "Keep it moving. If it stops, everyone can see it." That was the whole romance. Mizuki didn't understand for several weeks that the warm, sick, fizzing feeling in her chest whenever the darkroom door opened was anything other than the chemicals.

She spent most of that school year making sure she was in the darkroom on the same evenings he was. She learned his rota better than her own timetable. She said almost nothing to him, and pretended to be fascinated by the Yodo River. She went to the river at Juso one Saturday morning at five o'clock, entirely alone, to photograph it at dawn, so she'd have something to show him. The photographs were bad. The light was beautiful. She still has the contact sheet.

By the new year she knew what it was, and Aya knew too, because Aya always knew. In early 2007 Aya said, "Just talk to him. Ask him about the river." Mizuki said she couldn't, because he was a senpai and she was a first-year and it would be "against the order of the universe". Aya said the universe didn't care. Mizuki didn't do it anyway.

He graduated in **March 2007**. On graduation day the club gave the third-years a small framed print each and a bunch of flowers. Mizuki handed him his and said, "Thank you for everything, senpai," and bowed very correctly. He said, "Keep it moving," and smiled, the first and only time she'd seen him smile, and left. She went home, ate a whole box of strawberry **Pocky** in her room and lay on the floor looking at the ceiling. Aya sent one message: a crying face, a broken heart and the line, "Baka. You should have asked for his second button," the second button on a boy's school uniform, the one nearest the heart, which girls traditionally ask a graduating boy to give them.

Mizuki laughs about it now. It was her first crush, and it is the true, slightly humiliating origin of her fondness for "**notice me, senpai**", because he never did, or if he did he was much too quiet to say. She never found out where he went next. The dodging tool he made her is still in a box at her parents' place, and she uses the line "keep it moving, or everyone can see it" about almost everything: editing a photo, hiding a mistake at work, even, once, about how to ride an awkward silence at a dinner party.

### 2007: Saturdays at the bakery

In April 2007, just after her seventeenth birthday, Mizuki got her **first part-time job**: Saturdays at a small family bakery on a side street between Tennoji station and the Abeno crossing. It was run by **Mr and Mrs Nishikawa**, a couple in their fifties, and it was the kind of neighbourhood bakery that opened at seven, sold out of curry bread by eleven and closed when the shelves were empty. A card in the window said they needed a Saturday girl. Mizuki went in with her school record and her mother's advice ("smile, bow, say you're not afraid of mornings, and don't mention maths") and got the job the same afternoon.

The pay was **750 yen an hour**, just above Osaka's minimum wage at the time, from seven in the morning until two. Her school allowed part-time work if you kept your grades up and filled in a form, and Yumiko signed it on condition that Mizuki's marks didn't drop and she still helped at the shop on Sunday evenings. There was an argument about it first. Yumiko's position was that the family already had a shop, and if Mizuki wanted to work she could cut cabbage for free like everyone else. Mizuki's position was that she wanted money that was hers, for film, without having to ask. Hiroshi's position, delivered from behind the newspaper, was "Let her. Other people's kitchens teach you things." That settled it.

Mr Nishikawa was the baker. He was round, quiet and floury to the elbows, and started work at four in the morning. He had trained in Kobe, which he considered the bread capital of Japan, and he spoke about yeast the way her father spoke about the Tigers: with love, worry and very little hope. Mrs Nishikawa ran the front with a sharp tongue, an apron with a cat on it, and exactly the same eye for customers as Yumiko. The two women met once, by accident, and got on so well that Mizuki felt surrounded.

Her job was the shop floor: stacking the wooden shelves as the trays came out, wrapping, the till, and the eternal Japanese bakery ritual where customers walk round with a tray and a pair of **tongs**, pick their own bread and bring it to the counter to be bagged. She learned to slide a hot **curry bread** into a paper bag without crushing it, to tie a ribbon on a cake box with one hand, and to say "irasshaimase" brightly at seven in the morning, which for her was a genuine achievement. She learned the regulars: the old man who bought one anpan every day and told her about his late wife; the office lady who bought a croissant and ate it on the step; the schoolboys who arrived at 12:55 hoping for anything half-price.

And she fell in love with **melon bread**, *melon pan*: the round, sweet bun with a crisp, sugary cookie crust scored in a criss-cross pattern so it looks a little like a melon (though there's usually no melon in it at all). Mr Nishikawa's was famous in the neighbourhood: still warm at nine, the top crackling when you bit it. Staff were allowed one item from the shelf at the end of the shift, and for two years Mizuki chose melon bread every single Saturday. It became her comfort food and her reward food and, later, her yardstick for every bakery in Kyoto, Osaka and Sydney. "Melon bread forever" is a family phrase now. In Sydney she can find good croissants but almost never a proper melon pan, and it is one of her standing complaints.

The bakery also taught her that she liked work. She liked the rhythm of a shift, the shelves filling and emptying, the satisfaction of a clean counter at closing time. She liked being trusted with the till. She liked customers, which was not a surprise for an okonomiyaki shop daughter, but it was the first time she'd been liked by customers who didn't know her parents. And she liked having her own money. Her first pay packet, in a small brown envelope at the end of April, went on three rolls of film, a beret, and a box of Mr Nishikawa's cookies for her parents, which Hiroshi ate in silence, all of them, over two days.

There were small disasters. In her second month she dropped a full tray of cream buns onto the floor in front of a queue of customers, and stood looking at them in horror until Mrs Nishikawa said, very loudly, "Well, that's lunch for the staff, then," and the queue laughed. She overslept once, in November 2007, and ran the whole way from Abeno with her hair still wet, arriving at 7:20 to find Mr Nishikawa had left her a warm melon bread on the counter anyway, with a note: "Late is still here." She never overslept again.

### May 2007: the news from Expoland

On 5 May 2007, Children's Day, in the middle of the Golden Week holidays, the evening news led with an accident at **Expoland**, the old amusement park in Suita in the north of Osaka, built on the site of the 1970 Expo. A car on one of its roller coasters had come off its track partway round the ride. A young woman, only a couple of years older than Mizuki, had died, and many other people were hurt.

The family watched it in the shop, with the regulars, in a silence that was very unusual for that room. Mizuki had been to Expoland once, in 2000, with Daisuke, when she was ten. She had refused the big coasters and loved the **Ferris wheel**, the slow, swinging height of it, the whole of north Osaka spread out like a map. She thought of that, and then she thought of the woman, who had gone to an amusement park on a holiday, which is the most ordinary happy thing in the world, and she went cold all the way through.

Daisuke, who was twenty-one and at university in Kobe, sent her a mail that night: "Remember the Ferris wheel? I'm glad we only went on that." She replied with a single sad face. Later the news explained that a part on the car had worn out and hadn't been properly checked. The park closed for a long time, and never really recovered; it shut for good in 2009. Mizuki didn't follow the details closely. She didn't want to.

What stayed with her was a feeling rather than a fact: that the machines built for fun are still machines, and that somebody, somewhere, has to look after them carefully. She went quiet on rides for a while afterwards. She still loves theme parks and still screams on everything, as the Gold Coast would discover fifteen years later, but she looks at a ride before she gets on it now, properly, and she thinks the people who check the bolts are among the most important workers in any park. She only talks about Expoland rarely, and briefly, and she always says the woman's family must still miss her.

### Perfume, Polyrhythm and the soundtrack of high school

The soundtrack of her high school years started with **Utada Hikaru**, as everything had since she was nine. "First Love" was still her shower song (with the high bit done badly on purpose), "Hikari" from Kingdom Hearts was still on her MD player, and in early 2007 Utada released "**Flavor of Life**", the ballad version of which was the theme for the second series of the TV drama *Hana Yori Dango*, and it was everywhere: in shops, in the bakery's radio, on the train through other people's earphones. Mizuki thought the title was strange English and the song was perfect, and on the walk to the bakery at quarter to seven in the cold, it was what she hummed.

**Aya** stayed loyal to **Kanjani Eight**, the Kansai boy band who sang in exaggerated Osaka-ben and did comedy between songs. She had a favourite member, a fan-club card and a wall of posters, and she would not hear a word against them. Mizuki's verdict, "half music, half comedy, which is very Osaka", was meant as a compliment, mostly. Aya went to their concerts at the Osaka Dome with her cousin and came back hoarse. Mizuki went once, in 2007, and admitted afterwards, under pressure, that she had enjoyed it more than she'd expected, and had waved the fan in the right places.

Her father still played **Southern All Stars** and old **enka** on the shop radio, and her mother still sang **Takarazuka** songs while she washed up. Mizuki pretended to find both embarrassing and knew every word of all of it.

Then, in the summer of 2007, a public-service advert about **recycling** came on the shop television, and Mizuki stopped with a plate in her hand. The song was "**Polyrhythm**" by **Perfume**, a trio of young women from Hiroshima: glittering, robotic electro-pop, the voices processed until they sounded almost like machines, and a long middle section where the rhythm folds over itself and seems to go wrong on purpose, then snaps back. She had never heard anything like it. The single came out that September, and it became Perfume's breakthrough after years of being an unknown idol group.

She loved everything about it: the futuristic sound, the strange, precise choreography where the three of them moved like parts of one clock, their outfits that matched but didn't quite match. She bought the CD single, played it until Kenta threatened to hide the stereo, and learned the dance with Aya in Aya's bedroom, both of them getting tangled in the polyrhythm section every single time. They performed it at the class party before the winter holidays in December 2007. It was, by universal agreement, very bad, and the class asked for it again. When Perfume's album **GAME** went to number one in April 2008, Mizuki felt personally vindicated, as if she had discovered them herself.

Perfume matters in her story because they are the bridge between Utada and the K-pop she fell for in her twenties. What she loves in music started there: pop that is precise, a bit futuristic and danced in perfect formation, made by people who have obviously rehearsed until their feet hurt. She still puts "Polyrhythm" on when she's cleaning the flat, and still trips over the middle.

### Films, anime and games, 2006 to 2008

These were the years Mizuki's taste in films set hard. She wanted romance with something hidden in it: a ghost in the corner, a twist, a secret in the past. Love Letter was the peak, but there were others.

In the summer of 2006 she and Aya went to see the anime film **The Girl Who Leapt Through Time** (*Toki o Kakeru Shōjo*), which opened in a handful of cinemas and became a word-of-mouth hit. A high school girl discovers she can jump back in time, uses it for silly things (avoiding a test, eating a pudding twice), and then finds out the cost. Mizuki loved it because it was funny for the first hour and then quietly broke her heart, which she considers the correct order. In 2007 she found Makoto Shinkai's **5 Centimeters per Second** on DVD, a short, beautiful anime about two childhood sweethearts drifting apart over years, with a famous sequence of a train delayed by snow. She thought the backgrounds were the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen drawn. She was less happy with the ending ("he needs to post a letter, that's all, just post it"), but she's watched it every winter since.

Phones were part of the story too. In 2007 the craze was **keitai shōsetsu**, novels written and read on mobile phones, and one, *Koizora* ("Sky of Love"), became a bestselling book and then a film. Mizuki read a few chapters on her flip phone on the train, found them "very dramatic, a bit like someone's diary got too much sugar", and secretly kept reading anyway. Aya read the whole thing and denied it.

She still drew her hard line on violence. When a boy in her class lent around a gory horror DVD in 2007, she politely declined. "I'm a darkroom person. I like things that appear slowly. I don't like things that explode." Ghosts were still fine. She watched old Japanese horror with Kenta on summer nights, with the lights off and her fingers ready.

Games slowed down in high school, between the club, the bakery and homework, but they didn't stop. Kenta got a **Nintendo DS Lite** in 2006 and **Pokémon Diamond** that autumn, and Mizuki, sixteen and far too old for it, borrowed it "to check something" and played for three days. She chose **Chimchar**, the fire monkey, because it was the closest she could get to a fire-type fox, and caught a **Ponyta** to keep it company. She still prefers the old games and still says so, but she made an exception for Diamond's underground mining, which she found "very relaxing, like being a mole with a hobby". She also replayed **Final Fantasy X** in January 2008, when she had flu.

That flu deserves a line. In January 2008 she caught **influenza** properly, a high fever, aching all over, five days in bed upstairs with a cold towel on her forehead, the sound of the shop downstairs coming up through the floor. Yumiko brought rice porridge with pickled plum three times a day and left it at the door "so I don't catch your nonsense". Fumiko came round and sat by her futon and told her a story about a fever-yokai who lived in the corners of hot rooms and could only be got rid of by drinking enough water, which Mizuki was sure was invented on the spot. She missed a week of school and a Saturday at the bakery, and Mrs Nishikawa sent home two melon breads in a bag. She was well again just in time for the school trip, and Yumiko said, with great relief, "Good. I wasn't paying for Hokkaido twice."

### The darkroom key and the second year

When the senpai graduated in March 2007, Ms Kuroda did something she never explained. She handed Mizuki a box of printing paper and the **darkroom key** on a length of red string, and said, "Spring holidays. Somebody has to keep the chemicals company." Mizuki spent most of those two weeks alone in the dark with the radio on, printing the Yodo River contact sheet until the bad photographs were at least well printed. It was, she says, the best possible thing to do with a broken heart: stand in a small dark room and make something appear. Neither of them has ever mentioned it.

In April 2007 she became a second-year, a senpai herself, and kept bowing back to the first-years until Aya said she looked like two pigeons arguing. At the **culture festival** that autumn, when every club turns a classroom into a café, a haunted house or an exhibition, the photography club hung black cloth on the windows and pinned up prints. Mizuki showed six, among them the fishmonger at last, Kenta asleep on the shop stairs with his DS on his chest, and her father's hands on the two spatulas. Yumiko stopped at the hands and said, "Those are dirty. Didn't he wash them?" Then she bought two copies. Hiroshi came after the lunch rush, stood in front of the same print for a long time, and said, "Mm." In Tanaka, that is a review.

The lesson of the second year was that she liked photographing people better than places, and hands better than faces. Faces posed. Hands, she said, "forget to".

### February 2008: Hokkaido and Otaru

The second-year **school trip** was four days in **Hokkaido** in early February 2008, and for Mizuki it had only ever been about one place: **Otaru**, the old harbour town from Love Letter.

It was her first time on a plane. Coming down into New Chitose the ground was white in every direction, a white Osaka never produces, with dark lines of trees drawn across it like pencil marks. The first day was **Sapporo**, with the Snow Festival sculptures in Odori Park and a dinner of **miso ramen** with butter and sweetcorn that she judged "very heavy, like being hugged by a bear", a verdict Mr Kondo, a Sapporo man, has since endorsed. The middle two days were skiing. She fell over getting off the lift, fell over getting back on it, and was slowly overtaken by small children, while Aya, good at once, kept skiing past shouting "Mizu! Lean forward!"

The FM2 travelled in a woolly sock inside her coat, and taught her two things about snow. The good one: being mechanical, it didn't care about the cold, while three classmates' digital compacts died on the slope. The bad one: Ms Kuroda had warned the club that snow fools a light meter, which tries to turn everything middle grey, so you must open up a stop or two to keep the white. Mizuki forgot for her entire first roll, and every frame of Sapporo came out the colour of wet cardboard. "Thirty-six receipts," she says.

Then, on the last day, Otaru, in a soft, steady snowfall: the old stone warehouses along the **canal**, snow on every roof and rope, gas lamps on the water, a long street of glass shops and music box shops where everything rang. In her free time she photographed the canal from the bridge exactly as she had imagined it for over a year, one stop open, and one frame of a single figure in a red scarf on the far path she still likes very much. Then she found a snowbank on a quiet street up the hill, nothing behind it but white and the grey sea, and made Aya stand on top. Aya, who had lent her the film in the first place, knew what was coming. She faced the sea and shouted, at the top of her considerable voice, "**O-genki desu ka?**" A man shovelling his drive stopped to stare. Aya waited a beat and shouted the reply herself, in entirely the wrong tone: "**I'm fine! My feet are not!**" Her trainers were soaked through. Mizuki laughed so hard she missed the first frame and made her do it twice. The picture, Aya with her hands round her mouth, snow on her hat, cheeks red, is one of her favourite photographs of anyone, and "I'm fine! My feet are not!" has been their phrase for anything cold ever since.

On the steep street back to the bus she slipped on packed ice and sat down hard, holding the camera above her head like a waiter saving a tray. The camera was fine. She had a bruise the size of a rice cracker for a fortnight, and Kenta imitated her walk for most of it. "I fell over for Love Letter," she says. "It's an honour." She came home with three rolls of film, Shiroi Koibito biscuits for the regulars, a little glass wind bell from the canal for Fumiko, who said it sounded "like a cold place being polite", and a plan, still unfulfilled, to go back to Otaru in winter as a grown-up.

### Spring 2008: Fumiko's hands

The photograph that won her a prize was taken on an ordinary Saturday afternoon in late April 2008, at Fumiko's low table by the window. It was pea season. In Osaka that means **usui peas**, soft and sweet, grown south of the city in Habikino and made into **mame gohan**, rice cooked with peas. Fumiko shelled them herself every spring at the window, a bowl on her knee, a newspaper on the table for the pods. Mizuki had come round to photograph the morning glory seedlings and stayed because the light through the window was soft and sideways, falling across the table. "Light first. Then the face."

She didn't get the face. Fumiko said, "Ara, not my face, it's Saturday," and went on shelling. So Mizuki did what the club had taught her: she talked for a while, about the bakery and Otaru, and Fumiko talked back about Shigeru. Then Mizuki stopped talking. After a few minutes her grandmother forgot the camera, and her hands did what they had always done: thumb along the seam, a little snap, peas rolling into the bowl, never still. The knuckles were swollen, the skin thin and spotted, and there was a thimble mark on one finger older than Mizuki's mother. She took eleven frames and knew the eighth as she took it.

Ms Kuroda looked at the first print for a long time and said, "**One more.**" Mizuki held back the shadow under the bowl. "One more." She burned in the corner of the newspaper. "One more." She printed it **eleven times** in all, the hands sharp and silver, a single pea halfway between pod and bowl, the rest of the room falling into soft grey. She called it *Mame gohan*.

Ms Kuroda entered it in the **Osaka high school photography competition** without telling her until the form was posted, and in the autumn it won a small prize: not first, not second, but a proper one with a certificate. The ceremony was on a cold Sunday in November in a community hall with folding chairs, and the whole family came, which meant closing the shop for a lunchtime, which the Tanakas never did. Fumiko wore her one **best coat**, camel wool, kept in tissue paper for ceremonies. When the print appeared on the screen she told the stranger next to her, quite loudly, "Those are mine. The hands. Those are my hands," and then told the row behind, and by the end of the afternoon most of the hall. Ms Kuroda stood at the back with her arms folded and something very close to a smile.

Hiroshi's review was "Mm." He then had it framed and hung **above the till**, where Yumiko told every customer whose hands they were and whose daughter had taken it. Later Fumiko asked for it, "so I can see what my hands are doing when they're not doing anything", and it moved to the wall above her kotatsu. Mizuki's own print is on the shelf in Pyrmont now, next to the FM2.

### Shirahama, the last summer

The family still went to **Shirahama** every August: three days, the shop's only holiday, the same small inn, the same sunset through the arch of **Engetsu**. With the FM2 the trips changed for her. She photographed Kenta, fourteen that summer and newly too cool to swim with his family, sulking magnificently under a beach umbrella with a manga; Fumiko in a sunhat tied under her chin, feet in the water, skirt held up like a girl's; and, one early morning, her father from behind on the rocks with his line out and a towel round his neck. She didn't tell him. When he found out years later he said, "You should have asked. I'd have stood up straighter."

The summer of 2008 was the last of those trips, though nothing about it felt like an ending. In the evenings the family watched the **Beijing Olympics** on the inn's small television, cheering Kosuke Kitajima's two swimming golds and the softball team's.

Osaka's real grief that summer was baseball. In July the **Hanshin Tigers** led the Central League by thirteen games. Then the Yomiuri **Giants** came back, game by game, and in October took the pennant, and the Tigers' manager resigned. Hiroshi said "Mada mada" at the hotplate and then, days later, to no one in particular, "Thirteen games." It became "33 to 4"'s quieter cousin in the family vocabulary.

### The exam year

April 2008 began her last year of high school, and this time the exams were serious. The plan was a **national university in Kobe**: cheaper fees, a good name, a hill with the sea below it, and Daisuke nearby, newly graduated and starting at an accounting firm in Sannomiya. The second choice was a private university in **Kyoto** with a good department of English and tourism. The difference mattered: the national university meant the **National Center Test** in January, five subjects, maths included. Kyoto wanted English, Japanese and history, and no maths at all.

So the year was maths, five evenings a week at the juku above the pachinko parlour, with Aya beside her doing chemistry problems for pharmacy, for fun, which Mizuki found frightening. The mock exams graded every choice from A to E. For Kyoto she got A's and B's. For Kobe she got C's, all year, never better, never worse. "C is for 'could'," she told her father. "C is for cabbage," he said, which helped nobody.

There was one real argument, with her mother, about the **bakery**: Yumiko said a girl in her exam year shouldn't be selling curry bread at seven on Saturdays; Mizuki said Saturday mornings were the only hours when nobody asked her about maths. It ran through most of May. Chiyo settled it on a Sunday afternoon with her abacus, pointing out that a girl who earned her own film money was costing the family less. Mizuki kept the job. She left the photography club in the summer, as third-years do, but Ms Kuroda let her keep the darkroom key "for emergencies", and she had about one a month. Kenta had his own entrance exams coming in March, so that winter the flat held two candidates, one exhausted mother and a bag of exam-season **Kit Kats**, bought because "Kitto Katsu" sounds like "you'll surely win".

In early January 2009 Fumiko took her to **Osaka Tenmangu**, the shrine to Tenjin, god of scholarship. The plum trees were bare and the racks of **ema**, the wooden wishing plaques, hung over each other like roof tiles. Fumiko paid for one and watched Mizuki write the Kobe university's name and "Please let the maths be kind." Then she took the pen and added underneath, in her small neat hand: "and if not, let her be kind to herself". Mizuki didn't understand that until March.

The **Center Test** was on 17 and 18 January, in a lecture hall full of coughing. She turned the Seiko round three times before each paper. English, Japanese and history went well. The maths was the maths, and she came out knowing, the way you know a print has gone wrong the moment it starts to come up in the tray. Aya met her outside with a can of hot milk tea and, for once, said nothing at all.

The good news came first. In February she sat the Kyoto exam, no maths, and the letter came: she was in. Yumiko put it under a magnet on the fridge and said, "So. Somewhere." Hiroshi read it once and said, "**Good.**" Then, without consulting anyone, he gave every customer that evening their okonomiyaki free, which cost a night's takings and brought Chiyo round specially to tell him so. The regulars still mention it. At the end of February came the Kobe university's own exam, on the hill above the sea; Daisuke met her afterwards and bought her a pork bun in Chinatown, and she told him it had gone "medium". She went home with a little hope, which she has since decided is the most dangerous food there is.

### March 2009: graduation

Graduation was at the start of March: a cold gym, ticking heaters, a school song sung badly by three hundred people who would never sing it again. Mizuki didn't even pretend she wasn't going to cry. Aya blamed the heaters, for old times' sake.

Afterwards Ms Kuroda found her by the darkroom door, under "OPEN THIS AND DIE", and gave her a small package in brown paper: a single roll of **Kodak Tri-X** and a note, "**For Kyoto. Wait for the light.**" Mizuki said, "But I might be going to Kobe." Ms Kuroda said, "Then take it to Kyoto on a Sunday. The temples won't mind," and went into the darkroom and shut the door. They would not speak again for fifteen years. Mizuki still doesn't know whether her teacher simply knew how bad her maths was.

A few days later the Kobe results went up, and her number wasn't there. Because it was a Saturday, she went to work. What happened in the bakery storeroom belongs to the next part of her life, and to a melon bread.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Isamu's Seiko**: worn daily from spring 2005, turned three times before every exam.
- **The Nikon FM2**: works without a battery, survived Hokkaido when the digital cameras died; "it'll outlive both of us".
- **The darkroom**: "a temple, but with chemicals"; faces arriving slowly in the tray; stop bath's vinegar smell, still nostalgic.
- **Patience over quantity**: thirty-six chances a roll; hates "machine-gun shooting" ("fishing with dynamite").
- **Hands over faces**: "Faces pose. Hands forget to." The root of *Mame gohan*.
- **Sayings she kept**: "Talk to them, then stop talking" (Ms Kuroda and her father); "One more"; "That's a receipt"; "Keep it moving, or everyone can see it".
- **Love Letter and Otaru**: the most beautiful film ever made; snow, letters, side light; she wants to go back in winter.
- **Romance with a secret in it**: The Girl Who Leapt Through Time ("funny first, then heartbreak, the correct order"); 5 Centimeters per Second ("just post the letter").
- **No gore**: "I'm a darkroom person. I like things that appear slowly."
- **Melon bread**: "melon bread forever"; the yardstick for every bakery since, and missing in Sydney.
- **Work and her own money**: the rhythm of a shift, a clean counter, film she paid for herself.
- **Amemura vintage denim and berets**: jackets that have "already been somewhere interesting"; navy, black and "the omelette". **Dislikes**: her five-times-round scarves, white platform boots, loose socks ("very 2002").
- **Perfume's "Polyrhythm"**: precise, futuristic pop in formation, the bridge to K-pop; Utada's "Flavor of Life" for the cold walk to the bakery; Kanjani Eight, "half music, half comedy, which is very Osaka".
- **Theme parks, carefully**: since Expoland she looks at every ride before she gets on.
- **Games**: FFXII "beautiful, but a long commute"; Diamond's underground, "a mole with a hobby".
- **Maths**: still "brave"; the subject that decided her life.
- **"33 to 4" and "Thirteen games"**: the family's words for disaster, courtesy of the Tigers.
