## Chapter 8: Takuya, Okinawa and Eorzea (mid-2011 to 2013)

### Summer 2011: a dimmer Japan

The summer of 2011 was the first one Mizuki remembers as quiet. After the earthquake and tsunami in the north-east that March, the whole country turned its lights down. The nuclear plants were being checked one by one, and even in Kansai, hundreds of kilometres from Tōhoku, the power company asked everyone to save electricity. Station escalators stood still with apologetic little signs. Half the lights in the Keihan carriages were off, so the morning commute to Kyoto had a soft, underwater look. Shops set their air conditioning to twenty-eight degrees, and the salarymen of Osaka took their ties off for "Super Cool Biz" and looked, Mizuki thought, like a whole city of uncles at a barbecue.

She was twenty-one, in her third year of a four-year degree in English and tourism at a university in Kyoto, still living above her parents' okonomiyaki shop in Abeno. The hotplate couldn't be turned down, so her mother saved electricity everywhere else with the zeal of a convert. Yumiko unplugged the rice cooker the second the rice was done, unplugged the television when nobody was watching it (twice while Hiroshi was), and taped a sign above the upstairs light switch: "Tōhoku is using this electricity. Turn me off." Nobody argued with that sign. Even Kenta, seventeen and allergic to instructions, turned the light off.

Mizuki doesn't joke about that spring, and says only a sentence about the donation boxes outside Tennoji station. But the dimness gave her something. It was the first time she noticed how beautiful Osaka looked with less light: Dotonbori with a third of its signs off, the shop's red lantern brighter because the street around it was darker. She shot a whole roll of half-lit streets on the FM2 that summer, the beginning of her favourite subject, the city at night. "Sometimes you only see the light when someone turns some of it off," she says, and then, because she is from Osaka, "also, my electricity bill was very low."

At dawn on 18 July 2011, Japan time, Nadeshiko Japan, the women's football team, beat the United States on penalties in the World Cup final in Germany. Mizuki had set her alarm for half past three "as a joke" and then couldn't stop watching. She and Kenta sat on the living-room floor with the sound almost off, and when the last penalty went in they jumped up and hugged in total silence, miming shouting. Their mother came out in her nightdress, said "You're both mad," saw the replay, and shouted for real. Hiroshi slept through it, and at breakfast said only "Football," in the tone other people use for "tax". It was, Mizuki says, the first good news Japan had had in months.

### The end of the forest girl

In 2011 her "mori girl" period quietly died. For two years she had dressed like a girl who lived in a cottage in a European wood: layered skirts in oatmeal and moss green, knitted cardigans, lace-edged blouses, thick socks, a leather satchel and a brown felt hat with a ribbon, which she now denies owning even when shown the photograph. Aya called the look "a curtain that has learned to walk".

The Kyoto summer killed it. Four layers of cotton in that wet basin heat, on a half-air-conditioned train, was not a fashion but a punishment. One afternoon in late July she caught her reflection in a shop window near Gion-Shijo station, saw "a hot, damp tablecloth with a face", and went straight into a UNIQLO for two plain white T-shirts. She calls it the most important purchase of her twenties. What replaced the forest girl was closer to who she is now: wide trousers, plain T-shirts, a denim jacket from Amerikamura when it cooled, her hair in a messy knot, and her grandfather Isamu's old Seiko watch, which she had worn every day since she was fourteen. The felt hat went into a box in her wardrobe, where, according to her mother, it still lives.

### Third year: the seminar and the toilet thesis

In their third year Japanese students join a professor's seminar, the "zemi", and start thinking about a graduation thesis. Mizuki chose tourism and intercultural communication, run by a soft-spoken professor who had worked for a travel company and answered every question with "Well, let's go and look." He meant it. Twice a term the seminar went out to Kyoto Station, Nishiki Market and the bus stops below Kiyomizu-dera, counting tourists and watching what confused them.

That was where her thesis came from. She became obsessed with English signs in Kansai and collected hundreds on a cheap digital compact ("film is for beautiful things"): a restroom sign in Osaka reading "Please do not stand on the toilet seat and do it", a temple notice asking visitors to "refrain from noisy shouting and other violent", and a menu near Umeda that translated chashu as "cooked pig meat of happiness". The thesis, finished in January 2013, had a long serious title about multilingual signage and the visitor experience in Kansai, but everyone, eventually including her professor, called it "the toilet thesis". Its argument was very Mizuki: a sign is a host. A confusing sign is a waiter who ignores you; a clear, kind one fills your water before you ask. She had learned that rule at Mr Ishida's ramen counter and was pleased to find it worked for a whole country.

Her TOEIC score of 865 that autumn was still being announced at the okonomiyaki counter weeks later, to the point where a very old regular decided TOEIC was a medicine and asked whether Mizuki was feeling better.

### Autumn 2011: the newspaper on the wall

Four or five evenings a week she was still behind the counter at **Mr Ishida's** Hakata ramen shop near Namba, six until about eleven. After two and a half years she had become, without anyone saying so, the senior part-timer, and she trained new students in the rules: greet everyone loudly, remember faces, wipe the counter the second someone stands, and never let a customer wait for water.

In November 2011 the Fukuoka SoftBank Hawks won the Japan Series in the seventh game against the Chunichi Dragons. Mr Ishida, Fukuoka-born and still Hakata-accented after more than twenty years in Osaka, did not smile. He cut the back page out of the sports paper and taped it to the wall exactly opposite the stool where Mizuki's father sat on his monthly visit. Hiroshi, a Tigers man since birth, came in, looked at it for a long moment, and ate his whole bowl without raising his eyes above the noodles. Next visit he brought a small Tigers towel, folded it into a perfect square and set it beside his bowl. Neither man ever mentioned it, and the newspaper and the towel kept their places for the rest of her time there: "the most Osaka and the most Fukuoka thing I have ever seen, at one counter".

The Tigers finished fourth that year and fifth the next. "Mada mada," said her father both times. Not yet.

### December 2011: the black suit

On 1 December 2011 the job-hunting season opened for students graduating in March 2013. (The business federation had pushed the start back from October that year.) Every third-year in the country logged on to the big recruitment websites at the same moment, and some of the websites fell over.

Shūkatsu has unwritten rules everyone knows. You buy a "recruit suit": black, plain, with a white blouse, black pumps and a black bag that stands up on its own beside your chair. Hair low and neat; if you've dyed it, you dye it back. Mizuki's soft "milk tea" brown from the forest-girl days went back to black at a salon in Tennoji, and she looked in the mirror and felt "like a new pencil". She and her mother bought the suit at one of the big suit chains with a giant sign and a permanent sale, and Yumiko negotiated a free second blouse with the energy of a woman buying a car. The pumps were a disaster. She wore plasters on both heels for a month and still has a small scar on the right one, "the shūkatsu medal".

What she hated most was the sameness. At a joint company fair in a vast exhibition hall by Osaka Bay, thousands of students in identical black moved between booths like a slow river of ink. She had the FM2 hidden in her stand-up bag and shot a roll from the balcony. One frame, a single girl in the crowd with her head tipped back laughing at something nobody else could see, is one of her favourite photographs from her twenties. "Everybody was trying so hard to be the same. That girl forgot for one second. I was very happy for her."

### Entry sheets and praying mail

Before an interview comes the "entry sheet", in 2011 often still handwritten, with the most famous question in Japanese job hunting: "What did you put the most effort into at university?" Mizuki wrote about the water glass: Mr Ishida's rule, learning to spot a half-empty glass from across the shop, and her belief that hospitality is mostly noticing. She rewrote it about thirty times. Her mother read every version and said "too long". Her father read one, was silent for a while, and said, "It's true, though," which was the best review she had ever had.

She aimed at travel companies, airlines (ground staff, not cabin crew, because she'd giggle through the safety demonstration) and hotels, and sent thirty-eight entry sheets over the winter and spring. She logged every one in a notebook in highlighter: yellow for waiting, green for through, pink for no. By April it was mostly pink.

The rejections came by email, and students called them "o-inori mēru", praying mail, because nearly every one ends by praying for your future success. She got about twenty between February and May 2012 and learned to recognise one from the preview line. Her friends rated them for sincerity, and a boy from Nara in her seminar, applying to banks, announced his total every Monday: "Twenty-seven companies are praying for me. I am the most prayed-for man in Kansai."

Some of them hurt. The worst was an airline ground-staff job she wanted badly: she passed the written test, the group discussion and two interviews, and the final rejection arrived in late March while she was wiping Mr Ishida's counter. She put the phone in her apron and kept working, and only cried, a little, into a dish towel while stacking the stools after closing. Mr Ishida said nothing. As she left he handed her a bowl of plain broth, nothing in it, and said "Drink." She did. It was the first time she understood that soup can be a sentence.

There were other small humiliations: the aptitude test's maths section, which she had not thought about since high school; a group discussion where she sat on her hands to stop herself making a joke; and an interview for a Tokyo-based company where the interviewer asked her to drop her Kansai intonation "for practice". She managed it the whole way through and then said "ookini", the old Osaka thank you, at the door. She didn't get that job and has never regretted the "ookini".

Her family coped in their own ways. Yumiko slipped a charm from Sumiyoshi Taisha, the great old shrine just south of Abeno, into her bag. Hiroshi, who had never had an interview in his life (at eighteen he simply walked downstairs into his father's shop), didn't understand the process and expressed support in pork. "Rejected?" he'd say. She'd nod. He'd put more pork on.

### Kenta's exams, winter 2012

That winter her brother had his own battle. Kenta, in his last year of high school, sat the national Center Test in mid-January 2012, aiming for information engineering at a university in Osaka. He was her opposite as a student, organised and unbothered, and revised at the kitchen table to Dragon Quest music, which he said helped him concentrate and she said was an insult to music. Mizuki, who still remembered crying in the bakery storeroom over her own failed exam, was more nervous than he was. She bought him Kit Kats, every student's exam snack because the name sounds like "kitto katsu", "you'll surely win", and wrote "Kenta ganbare" on the box. He ate them while saying, "You know this is just marketing."

In late February he passed his first choice and told her by sending a photo of the results page with no words at all, which is still how he communicates. She was proud and, for exactly one evening, a little jealous, and said so. "But you got Kyoto," he said. "I got the Keihan line," she said, and they both laughed and the sting went. He started in April, and that August, when Dragon Quest X came out on the Wii as the series' first online game, she could hear its level-up jingle through her bedroom wall. "My brother had an online fantasy world before me," she says. "Dragon Quest. It was unbearable."

### April 2012: the debate

In April 2012, at the start of her final year and still in the thick of job hunting, she signed up for an English discussion class to keep her speaking sharp for interviews. In the second week the teacher paired students for a debate on whether Japan should welcome far more foreign tourists, and put Mizuki with a tall, slightly stooped Kyoto boy called **Takuya Ono**.

He was born in 1989, a year ahead of her, but in her year at university because he had spent a year as a rōnin, studying at a Kyoto cram school to retake the entrance exams. He told her this in their first conversation as calmly as a weather report. Most people hid their rōnin year. "I failed the first time. So I tried again." Mizuki, who had failed her own first choice and gone somewhere else, felt something like recognition.

They had the "yes" side. Mizuki, full of toilet-thesis opinions, did the talking; Takuya, who said almost nothing while they prepared, had read every government tourism statistic and recited them calmly while the other side got flustered. They won. "You did the talking, I did the numbers," he says. "Exactly," she says. "The talking."

What she noticed first was that he was calm. She had grown up in a shop where everyone talked at once with jokes; Takuya spoke slowly and felt no need to fill a silence. His pencil case was organised by colour. He ironed his shirts on days without interviews. He walked so slowly she had to keep stopping for him, and he apologised to doors when he bumped them, and once, in a corridor, to a pillar. She found it all ridiculous and couldn't stop watching him do it. He was job hunting too, so at first they swapped praying-mail totals (his was lower, which annoyed her) and interview horrors. One interviewer had asked him what animal he would be, and he'd said "a tortoise" and been rejected. "You should have said lion," she told him. "I'm not a lion," he said. She agreed, and somehow that was the beginning.

### June 2012: the hotel says yes

Hotels recruit later than banks, and that spring she applied to several, including a business hotel in Umeda: tall, narrow, sensible, built for salarymen on two-night trips. Her airline-applying friends raised their eyebrows. But at the company briefing she watched a woman at the front desk refill a waiting businessman's water glass without being asked, while answering the phone, without looking, and thought, "Oh. I want to work here."

At the final interview in early June, one of the two managers asked her to explain the water glass. She told them about Mr Ishida, and then, nervously, about her mother at the okonomiyaki counter, handing the man in the white shirt an extra napkin before he'd picked up his chopsticks. "My mother has never had a job interview," she said, "but she would get this job." Both managers laughed. She was sure she'd ruined it.

In mid-June the hotel rang, not emailed, to offer her a "naitei", the informal job offer Japanese companies give students a year before graduation. She took the call on the platform at Yodoyabashi and said "arigatou gozaimasu" so many times that the man next to her moved away. Then she rang her mother, who screamed, put the phone down on the counter still connected, and announced it to the whole shop while Mizuki listened from the platform: "Our Mizuki! A hotel! In Umeda!" Her father came on, said "Good," and handed the phone back.

That night the family went to a karaoke box in Abeno. Yumiko, the family karaoke queen, sang three Takarazuka numbers and a Southern All Stars song back to back and refused to surrender the microphone to Kenta's Dragon Quest theme ("there are no words, it doesn't count"). Mizuki sang Utada's "First Love" with her eyes closed, as always, and her father tapped the table in time, his version of a standing ovation. The last pink highlights in her notebook were for companies she turned down, "the most satisfying stationery of my life". On 1 October she signed her papers at the formal offer ceremony with eleven other recruits, all looking "like we had escaped from a war, very politely".

Takuya got his own offer in July, from a sensible company in Kyoto that made precision parts for other companies' machines. He explained what the parts did three times. She still doesn't know. "Very small, very important," she says. "Like him."

### Summer 2012: the Kamo River

With the black suits put away, they kept finding reasons to meet: English practice over coffee that quickly became no English and a lot of arguing about whose city was better. He said Osaka was too loud; she said Kyoto was too quiet to know if it was alive. He said Osaka food was too salty; she said Kyoto food whispers and she needed hers to shout. He said Kyoto had a thousand years of history. She said Osaka had takoyaki. He said that wasn't an argument. She said it was the only argument.

He showed her his Kyoto, not the temples she had already photographed but small things: a tofu shop in the north of the city run by the same man every morning for forty years, a cat that always sat on the same step near Demachiyanagi, the turtle-shaped stepping stones across the river there, which she crossed wobbling in sandals while he walked alongside on the path "in case". He was good with silence, and she discovered, to her surprise, that with him so was she.

On summer evenings they walked the **Kamo River**. Kyoto students joke that couples on its banks always sit at exactly equal distances, like birds on a wire, and that a new couple will sit precisely in the middle of any gap: "the Kamogawa equal-distance law". Mizuki had rolled her eyes at it for three years. One July evening they sat down, she looked left and right, and saw they were perfectly spaced between two other couples. She laughed so hard she had to lie back on the grass. He didn't see the joke. "Of course," he said. "Where else would we sit?" That, she says, was when she knew she liked him.

In late July they saw Mamoru Hosoda's **Wolf Children**, about a young mother raising two children who can turn into wolves. Mizuki had expected to cry, and didn't, because Takuya cried first, quietly, at the scene in the snow, and didn't hide it. He took out an actual ironed handkerchief, wiped his eyes, and said, "Sorry. Snow." She thought it was the most attractive thing a man had ever done in a cinema. She still calls it "the film where the boy cried first".

### 16 August 2012: a confession by firelight

Japanese relationships often begin with a "kokuhaku", a confession: one person says plainly that they like the other and asks them to go out, and after that you are officially a couple.

Takuya's came on 16 August 2012, at **Gozan no Okuribi**, when great bonfires are lit on the mountains around Kyoto at the end of Obon in the shapes of characters, most famously the giant 大 ("dai") on Mount Daimonji, to guide the spirits of the dead back to the other world. They watched from the Kamo riverbank among thousands of people as the character appeared on the dark mountain one stroke of fire at a time. Mizuki was thinking about her grandfather Isamu, and about the photo of Shigeru, the tram-driver grandfather she never met, on Fumiko's altar, and was getting a little sad, when Takuya, staring straight ahead, said very quietly, "I like you. Will you go out with me?"

"He confessed at a festival for sending the dead back to the afterlife," she says on the phone. "Very romantic. Very Kyoto. The spirits were leaving and he was arriving." She said yes. Then she asked, "Why now?" and he said, "Because it's dark and you can't see my face," which was so honest she said yes again. They walked back to the station holding hands, slowly, because he always walked slowly, and she didn't mind waiting any more.

### Being a couple

Takuya was her first proper boyfriend (the quiet senpai in high school had never got further than looking), and at twenty-two she found being in a relationship mostly very funny and sometimes wonderful.

He planned; she improvised. He checked train times the night before; she ran for trains. He liked history museums, tidy cafés and long cycle rides by the river; she liked arcades, karaoke and anything with a queue that smelled of fried food. He was kind in small, practical ways: when she had a cold he turned up at closing time with lemon honey drinks and waited outside rather than disturb the shop, and he carried a spare umbrella for her because she always forgot hers, and never once said "I told you so".

He was also, she says with love, a bit boring about anime. He had grown up on NHK documentaries and samurai dramas with his father, and the phrase "a bit complicated" first appeared in November 2012, when she dragged him to **Evangelion 3.0** on its opening weekend. She came out shaking with theories. He thought about it all the way to the station and said, "It was a bit complicated." She didn't speak to him until Kyobashi. It became their running joke, and the phrase he would use again, fatefully, about Your Name four years later. He was hopeless at video games, drove the wrong way round Rainbow Road on Kenta's Wii, slowly, apologising to the other drivers, but he would sit beside her while she played and ask polite questions about the story and actually listen to the answers, which in her experience made him rare among men.

At Christmas, a couples' night in Japan, they went to the **Kobe Luminarie**, the arches of light put up every December since 1995 in memory of the earthquake. Mizuki had been under the kotatsu with Fumiko during that earthquake when she was four, and walking under the lights she went quiet. He didn't ask why; he held her hand a bit tighter. She photographed the arches with the FM2 braced on his shoulder, and one of those frames hangs in her flat in Sydney. Afterwards they had kushikatsu with her second cousin Daisuke, who lived in Kobe and had played Final Fantasy VII while seven-year-old Mizuki watched. He shook Takuya's hand and said, "So you've met the audience." Takuya didn't understand, and Daisuke refused to explain.

### Takuya meets the family

He met the Tanakas properly in October 2012, at the counter of the shop, which is the only way anyone meets the Tanakas. Mizuki had warned him: her mother would talk, her father wouldn't, Kenta would look at his phone, and her grandmother would feed him to death. All true. Yumiko asked him eleven questions in two minutes (his family, his father's job, whether his mother made her own pickles, his blood type) and decided he was "a very good boy, a bit pale". Fumiko, eighty-five, came round the corner especially and put three mikan in his jacket pockets without explanation.

Then Hiroshi, at the hotplate, asked his only question. "Tigers?"

Takuya, honest to the end, said, "I'm sorry. I don't really follow baseball."

The silence that followed was, Mizuki says, "the longest of my life, and I was in the 1995 earthquake". Her father turned and flipped an okonomiyaki very precisely. Her mother took a menu from under the counter and hit Hiroshi gently on the back of the head. "Say something nice." Hiroshi thought, then put an extra helping of pork on Takuya's okonomiyaki. Mizuki knew what that meant, and somehow so did Takuya. He ate every bite and said it was the best he'd ever had, and her father nodded once. Takuya was in. Over the years he learned to say "mada mada" at the right moments and even went to Koshien with Hiroshi, without ever understanding the rules. "He's quiet," her father told her once. "Good." From him, that was a speech.

### "Futsu!"

The disaster was Mr Ishida's. In September 2012 Takuya came into the ramen shop to surprise her at the end of a shift, sat at the counter, and when she asked how he wanted his noodles said, clearly, in front of Mr Ishida and a counter full of regulars: "Futsu, please." Normal.

In a Hakata shop, noodle firmness is close to a religion. Real customers order barikata or katamen, very firm or firm, and the brave order harigane, "wire". Futsu is allowed, but it is what tourists and children order. Mizuki was so embarrassed she pretended not to know him, took his order like a stranger's and spent twenty minutes very busy at the far end of the counter. Mr Ishida looked at Takuya, then at Mizuki, then at Takuya, and cooked the noodles futsu without comment, which was itself a comment.

When she finally went over to refill his water (she would never, boyfriend or not, let him wait for water), he asked mildly, "Did I do something wrong?" She whispered, "Futsu! In a Hakata shop!" He thought about it. "But I like them normal." She had no answer then and has none now. "Futsu!" is still her on-air cry of despair at soft noodles. The next day Mr Ishida asked, not looking up from the stockpot, "The tall one. Yours?" She said yes. He grunted. "He said thank you for the water. Good."

### Aya, and the monthly rule

**Aya Morimoto**, her best friend since the first day of junior high, was studying pharmacy at a different university in Kyoto. Pharmacy had become a six-year degree in 2006, so while Mizuki was finishing, Aya was only two-thirds through and drowning in organic chemistry. "You're leaving me alone in school," she said, more than once, not entirely joking. They still rode the Keihan together when their timetables matched, sharing earphones and a bag of sweets. Aya was still the loud one, still devoted to Kanjani Eight, still carrying a Togepi keyring on her pencil case "because it looks innocent and it isn't".

When Mizuki first mentioned Takuya, Aya demanded a full report (age, height, blood type, family business, Tigers or Giants) and then a meeting. In a café in Kawaramachi she interrogated him for forty minutes like a detective in a TV drama. He answered everything calmly, including how many girlfriends he'd had ("one, for three weeks, in high school; she moved to Nagoya"). Verdict: "Approved. With conditions." Mizuki asked what the conditions were. "I'll tell him if he breaks them."

That autumn the two friends had their first real wobble in years. Newly in love and free of job hunting, Mizuki cancelled two Sunday shopping trips in a row and forgot to answer a message from Aya, who had failed an important chemistry test and wanted to talk. When Mizuki finally rang, Aya was cold, polite and very Kyoto about it, which from the loudest girl in Osaka was terrifying. They met at their usual takoyaki stand in Amerikamura, and Aya said it straight, the Osaka way: "You're allowed a boyfriend. You're not allowed to disappear." Mizuki apologised properly, without a joke, and they made a rule: whatever happens, one day a month is just the two of them. They have more or less kept it ever since, across an ocean, these days mostly as an evening standing together somewhere in Eorzea.

Their Sundays went on: the covered Shinsaibashi-suji arcade end to end, then Amerikamura's vintage shops and Triangle Park. Mizuki's rule: try everything on, buy one thing, come back next week for the second. Aya's: "If you're thinking about it, it's already yours." And Aya kept saying what she'd been saying since 2010. Square Enix was rebuilding the failed original Final Fantasy XIV from scratch as A Realm Reborn. "When it comes back, we're playing it together." Mizuki, who had quit the 2010 version after four nights in a Namba manga café, said "We'll see." Aya said, "No. We'll play."

### What she watched, heard and played

**Music.** Perfume were the sound of her commute: "Laser Beam" in 2011, "Spring of Life" in spring 2012, on shared earphones on the Keihan. Kyary Pamyu Pamyu's "PonPonPon" (July 2011), with its floating eyeballs and dancing bread, she watched about fifty times trying to decide if she liked it, and concluded "yes, but I'm worried about myself". Utada Hikaru had gone on a break from music at the end of 2010, and Mizuki mourned her like a friend who'd moved abroad, until November 2012, when Utada's "Sakura Nagashi" played over the end of Evangelion 3.0. She sat very still through it while Takuya waited for it to be over, and still calls it the most beautiful thing Utada ever made, "worth Takuya's whole complaint". In summer 2013 Southern All Stars, her father's favourite band, returned from a five-year break, and for the first time anyone could remember Hiroshi turned the shop radio up instead of down.

**Television.** In 2013 the Tanakas watched what the whole country watched. NHK's morning drama "Amachan", about a shy Tokyo girl who becomes a free diver in a small Tōhoku town, made the nation say "jejeje", and Yumiko started saying it at the till. Then that summer came "Hanzawa Naoki", the banking drama with its famous promise to pay back wrongs "double". Hiroshi, who hadn't followed a drama since the 1980s, watched every episode in silence and once said "baigaeshi", double payback, to a supplier who was late with the cabbages. The supplier still talks about it.

**Films and anime.** In May 2013 Makoto Shinkai's short film **The Garden of Words**, about a schoolboy and a woman who meet in a Tokyo garden only on rainy mornings, came out, and she saw it alone in a small cinema during her first months at the hotel. "Every frame is a photograph of rain. I came out wanting it to rain, and it was sunny, and I was angry with Osaka." Shinkai's Your Name would knock her flat three years later, but this came first. In spring 2013 Kenta got her to watch the first episode of **Attack on Titan**, which everyone at his university was talking about. She watched through her fingers, then from behind a cushion, then from the next room. "It's very good. I will never watch it. Giants eating people. No, thank you." It is still her standard example of a brilliant thing refused on grounds of gore.

**Games.** She played Final Fantasy XIII-2 (December 2011) on Kenta's PS3 over New Year, liking the time travel more than the battles. Mostly, though, these were retro years. Den Den Town, Osaka's electronics and games district around Nipponbashi, was a few streets from Mr Ishida's, and before shifts she dug through baskets of second-hand Super Famicom cartridges the way she dug through vintage denim in Amerikamura. The family Super Famicom, Daisuke's old one, still lived at Fumiko's, so in 2012 she bought a battered second-hand console of her own in Den Den Town for her bedroom, plus a scuffed Chrono Trigger and her own copy of Final Fantasy VI, and played them at midnight with the sound low. "New games were already getting too long," she says. "Old games respect your evenings."

### Influenza, and a lost roll of film

In November 2012 she lost a roll of film: Tofuku-ji's red valley at its absolute peak, shot with Takuya on a cold, perfect morning. She'd put the exposed roll in her coat pocket, and somewhere between Tofuku-ji and Tennoji it vanished. She retraced the journey twice and asked at the Keihan lost-property office, where a kind man searched a box of gloves and umbrellas very seriously and found nothing. "Thirty-six photographs nobody will ever see. Probably they were bad, and in my head they're beautiful forever." She has kept exposed film in a zipped inner pocket ever since and checks it with her hand, without thinking, every time she stands up.

In February 2013, two weeks after handing in the toilet thesis, she got influenza: proper flu, a swab at the clinic, thirty-nine degrees and aching bones. Six days quarantined in her room in a mask, banned from both the shop downstairs and Mr Ishida's. Her mother left trays of rice porridge with umeboshi outside the door like offerings at a shrine. Takuya sent a box of lemon honey drinks from Kyoto with a note, "Please don't play games, rest," which she ignored, playing Chrono Trigger with a fever until the time-travel plot made more sense than it ever has since. Fumiko, not allowed in, stood in the street below her window and waved.

Over all these years, something was growing above the neighbourhood: Abeno Harukas, the new skyscraper over the Kintetsu station next to Tennoji, which reached its full three hundred metres in August 2012 and became the tallest building in Japan. Mizuki photographed it every few months from the same corner outside the shop, a little sequence of a tower climbing out of Abeno like a plant. Her father looked at it for a long time and said "Big." Fumiko said she preferred the sky.

### March 2013: hakama

Mizuki graduated in the second half of March 2013, on a cool, bright day with the cherry blossoms still in tight buds.

Like many Japanese women at university graduation, she wore a hakama, the pleated skirt over a kimono top that Meiji-era schoolgirls wore, which turns every ceremony into a sea of colour. Hers was rented from a kimono shop in Tennoji: deep navy, over a pale pink kimono top with small white plum blossoms, with brown lace-up boots. She had chosen it in December after an hour of trying things on with her mother and Aya, and gone back for it three times, because of her rule. Her hair was done at a salon that opened at five in the morning for half the graduating women of Osaka, and she sat half asleep while a stylist pinned in a cluster of artificial plum blossoms. When she got home at seven her father, opening the shop shutters, saw her in the street and stood completely still.

Her parents closed the shop for the afternoon, which almost never happened. Yumiko's sign on the shutter read: "Closed today. Our daughter is graduating. Sorry. Please come tomorrow." Several regulars came anyway, read the sign and nodded, and one left a bag of oranges against the shutter.

At the university gate Hiroshi borrowed back the FM2, the camera he had bought with his first proper wages in 1982, and shot a roll of her: by the gate, under a tree, with her diploma tube, with her mother, with Kenta in a suit and complaining about it. It's the only time she has seen her father nervous holding a camera. He kept saying "Stand still" though she was standing still. When the film came back half the frames were slightly out of focus and all of them were perfect.

Fumiko, eighty-six, came in her best grey coat and a brooch from her own wedding, and held Mizuki's arm all the way down the steps from the hall, very slowly. Halfway down she said, "Your grandfather would have whistled." Mizuki didn't trust herself to answer. Her other grandmother, Chiyo, admired the hakama, asked what the rental had cost, and said, "Too much. But it suits you." Chiyo has never paid full price for anything, and Mizuki took it as the compliment it was.

Takuya graduated the same day in a dark suit, and the two families met outside the hall. His quiet Kyoto mother bowed and said Mizuki's accent was "so lively", and Mizuki, after four years in Kyoto, knew exactly how to take it, and so did Yumiko, who smiled very widely and said nothing, which from her is a weapon. Then Hiroshi and Takuya's father, two men with nothing to say, stood side by side looking at a tree for five minutes in total contentment. "The best part of the day," Mizuki says. "Two fathers looking at a tree." Aya came between pharmacy classes with pink tulips and a handmade sign, "Congratulations, you finally left school", and insisted on a photo of the three of them, Mizuki in the middle, Takuya looking like a man waiting for the dentist. It's one of her favourite pictures.

### Okinawa with Aya

Japanese students take a "sotsugyō ryokō", a graduation trip, before working life swallows their holidays. Mizuki's was with Aya, who wasn't graduating and announced: "You're graduating, I'm graduating vicariously. Okinawa."

They went for four days in the last week of March 2013, flying from Kansai Airport on one of the new budget airlines, the one with the pink planes, for less than a bullet train to Tokyo. Mizuki had never been further south than Wakayama. At Naha the air was soft and warm and smelled of the sea, twenty-three degrees in March, and Aya stepped off the plane, spread her arms and shouted "Summer!"

They stayed two nights in a cheap guesthouse off Kokusai-dori, Naha's main street of souvenir shops and bottles of awamori with snakes inside, which Aya photographed and Mizuki refused to look at. The owner, an elderly woman with a laugh like a seagull, sent them to Makishi Public Market, where you buy fish downstairs and they cook it for you upstairs, and they ate fish in colours Mizuki hadn't known fish came in, bright blue and parrot green. They climbed to Shuri Castle, the red palace of the old Ryūkyū kings, and she photographed red against blue until the roll ran out. (When it burned in 2019 she was in Sydney, and she looked at those photos for a long time that night.)

The food was a revelation. **Okinawa soba**, thick wheat noodles in light broth with a soft slab of pork rib, she judged on its own terms and approved, though she noticed nobody refilled her water. **Taco rice**, taco filling on rice, born near the American bases, was "the most delicious mistake in Japan". They ate hot **sata andagi** doughnuts out of paper bags in the street and popped **umibudo**, "sea grapes", a seaweed like tiny green bubbles. **Goya champuru**, stir-fried bitter melon, she called "a vegetable that is angry with you personally", and finished anyway out of stubbornness; Aya gave up after one bite. Every day there was Blue Seal ice cream, purple sweet potato for Mizuki, salt cookie for Aya, and an argument about which was better.

On the third day Aya, who had got her licence the summer before and drove with total confidence, rented a tiny white kei car and took them two hours up the west coast to the Motobu peninsula, Mizuki navigating badly from a paper map and Kanjani Eight on the stereo the whole way, because the driver chooses the music. They crossed the long, low bridge to Kouri Island over water so clear and turquoise that Mizuki made Aya pull over just so she could look at it. The sea at Shirahama, the Wakayama beach of her childhood summers, was lovely but green-blue. This was a colour from a computer screen.

### The whale sharks

Then the **Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium**, the reason for the whole trip. You walk down through coral reefs and tropical fish, deeper and darker, then turn a corner and the whole wall in front of you is the sea: the Kuroshio Sea tank, one of the largest in the world, named after the warm current that runs past Okinawa, with an acrylic window blue all the way up. And in it, enormous and unhurried, the **whale sharks**.

Mizuki stopped dead. It's the only time she has forgotten she was holding a camera. The biggest, a male the staff call Jinta, who had lived with the aquarium since the nineties, was close to nine metres long and spotted like a night sky, and moved past the glass like a whole bus of a fish, with manta rays wheeling above him like birds. Aya, who talks through everything, said nothing for five minutes, which Mizuki says is still the record.

They stayed for the afternoon feeding, when the sharks rise and hang upright in the water, mouths wide open, gulping like gentle vacuum cleaners, which was funny and enormous and strangely moving all at once. Then they sat in the café beside the tank for over an hour, nursing one drink each. When she finally remembered the FM2, she shot a roll through the glass. Underwater light through acrylic is hard on film and half of it came out murky, but one frame, a small boy in a yellow cap with his hands flat on the window and Jinta passing behind him, came out perfectly, and it hangs in her Pyrmont flat.

"That's when I understood the word 'awe'," she says. "I knew it from class. In the aquarium I learned what it means: you are small, and it's fine." She has loved whale sharks ever since and is still slightly resentful that Sydney's aquarium doesn't have one.

### The sunburn

Afterwards they walked down to Emerald Beach below the aquarium park, white sand and impossible water. It was too early in the year for most people to swim, but the sun was out, and they lay on their towels talking about whale sharks and the future and Aya's chemistry exams until they both fell asleep.

Mizuki woke an hour and a half later on her left side. By evening she was badly burned on exactly half of her body: right cheek, right arm, the back of her right leg, the left side perfectly white. Aya, who had slept on her back under a hat, was barely pink and laughed so hard at dinner she had to leave the table. "You look like a tanuki, but only on one side." Okinawa's March sun, Mizuki learned, is stronger than Osaka's in August. She lay very still that night with a cold towel on her face while Aya read her the guesthouse's entire sunscreen pamphlet in a newsreader's voice.

The timing was the worst part. A week later she stood in the hotel's company entrance ceremony in a brand-new uniform with one cheek peeling under a layer of concealer. It is the root of a near-religious commitment to sunscreen that reached full flower in Sydney, where she puts it on the tops of her feet.

On the last morning they walked the fukugi tree lane in Bise, the village beside the aquarium: tall, dark-leaved trees planted centuries ago as windbreaks, with sunlight coming through in coins. Mizuki, peeling, photographed Aya walking ahead of her, small under the trees. It's still her favourite picture of her best friend.

She brought home beni-imo tarts for Fumiko, who said they were too sweet and ate four; a bottle of awamori, without a snake, for her father; and a pair of small ceramic shīsā, the lion-dog guardians of Okinawan rooftops, one with its mouth open and one closed, for her mother. Yumiko put them beside the till, the open-mouthed one on the left "to scare off the people who don't pay", and they are still there. Regulars pat them on the way out.

### The last shift at Mr Ishida's

Her last shift at Mr Ishida's was a Friday at the end of March 2013. Four years after a nervous first spring, she knew every scratch in the counter and every regular's order, and they came: the taxi driver who always ordered harigane, the two old brothers who came every Friday and never spoke to each other, the student who had once come in counting coins and found an extra slice of chashu in his bowl that nobody mentioned. Her father sat under the Hawks newspaper, folded his Tigers towel into its square and ate in silence. Takuya came too and, with great dignity and visible effort, ordered katamen. The whole counter applauded. Mizuki hid behind the stockpots.

She did the job exactly as always: loud greetings, a wiped counter, every glass full. At eleven, after the last customer, Mr Ishida came out from the back with an envelope, her last pay and a little extra, handed it over without looking at her, and said, "You never let anyone wait. Good." Then he went back to the pots. It was the only time in four years he praised her out loud.

She bowed, said "otsukaresama deshita", folded her apron onto its shelf, and made it to the Midosuji line before she cried, quietly, all three stops home to Tennoji, holding the envelope. Her mother met her at the back door, sniffed, and said what she had said a thousand times: "You smell like a pig's onsen." Then she hugged her anyway. Mr Ishida has sent a brush-written New Year card every year since, usually with one line of news about the soup. The first, in January 2014, said: "The new girl lets the water go to half. I am teaching her."

### April 2013: the hotel in Umeda

On 1 April 2013, the day company life begins for every new graduate in Japan, Mizuki put on the black suit one last time and rode the Midosuji line north to Umeda in a carriage full of other young people in identical black, all terrified. She thought, very seriously, "Today I am a member of society." Japanese has a word for it, shakaijin, a working adult, literally "a society person", and she felt it like a coat. Her new shift manager, **Mr Hamada**, looked at her peeling Okinawa cheek for one long, polite second and said nothing, which she later learned was how he said everything. The bows, the scarf and the keigo belong to the next part of her life.

She turned twenty-three on 10 April, in training, and didn't tell anyone at work. That evening her mother made a cake shaped, more or less, like a hotel. Takuya, two weeks into his own new job in Kyoto, came down on the Keihan in his new suit and fell asleep at the counter, upright, mid-sentence. Her father let him sleep and kept his okonomiyaki warm at the edge of the hotplate, which in the Tanaka family is an act of love.

### Summer 2013: a PlayStation and a knock on the door

With her first real salary, after a box of good sweets for her parents and a bottle of sake for Fumiko's altar, she bought a **PlayStation 3** of her own from an electronics shop in Namba and set it up on the chest of drawers in her old bedroom, beside her Den Den Town Super Famicom. For the first months it mostly played DVDs, because she was too tired for anything else. The early shift meant an alarm at twenty to five; the late shift brought her home at eleven with aching feet, and she fell asleep with the TV on. "Four months into being a member of society, I was a very sleepy member of society."

Meanwhile A Realm Reborn was coming. Aya had played the beta, sent screenshots every day, and finally sent the launch date, 27 August, with "!!!!!" and nothing else. Mizuki, who remembered the 2010 game as "a beautiful restaurant where the waiter takes ten minutes to bring the water", said she would "think about it".

Aya is from Osaka. She knows that "I'll think about it" in Osaka means no. So one Saturday in late August she turned up at the shop with the PS3 game in a carrier bag and a box of takoyaki, said hello to Yumiko and Hiroshi, walked upstairs as she had done a thousand times since she was thirteen, and put the disc into Mizuki's PlayStation without asking.

"You're playing."

"I'm tired."

"Healers can play tired. You'll be a healer."

"How do you know?"

"Because I know you," said Aya. "Make the character."

### August 2013: A Realm Reborn, and Mizu Nyan

The launch was chaos. So many people wanted in that the servers buckled: long login queues, error codes Mizuki can still recite like a hated poem, and a stretch when Square Enix stopped selling the digital version because there was no room for new players. They spent much of that first weekend staring at a loading screen and eating cold takoyaki. But within an hour of actually getting in, she knew everything had changed. The menus answered. The fields looked like places. When she pressed a button, something happened. "It was the same restaurant," she says, "but somebody had fixed the waiter."

She made a **Miqo'te**, the cat-eared, tailed race, because she had wanted to be one since 2010 and "if you can have ears, why not have ears?" Of the two Miqo'te clans she chose the **Keepers of the Moon**, the night-loving one: for Sailor Moon, and because shift work had made her a creature of odd hours. She gave her short **blue hair** for Sailor Mercury, her favourite since she was six, and spent forty minutes on the face while Aya fell asleep on her bed.

Then the name. Miqo'te have proper naming conventions in the game's lore, with clan surnames and apostrophes, and Aya had helpfully read them all out. Mizuki ignored every one and typed **Mizu Nyan**, "nyan" being the sound a cat makes in Japanese, and laughed so hard she nearly fell off the bed, waking Aya, who looked at the screen and said flatly, "You'll regret that." She hasn't. When anyone teases her about it, the answer is always: "I was twenty-three, leave me alone." Her mother called up the stairs to ask if they were all right. "I'm a cat!" Mizuki called back. "Good," said her mother, and went back to the till.

### Why White Mage

She started as a **Conjurer** in Gridania, the forest city, the same healing class she had abandoned in a Namba manga café three years before, and this time it clicked. At level 30, after also levelling Arcanist to 15, which she grumbled about loudly (a book, and a floating pet she found "too cheerful"), she unlocked **White Mage**: the white hooded robes trimmed with red triangles that Final Fantasy fans have known since the very first game. She put them on and had a feeling she describes as "putting on a uniform that fits". She has played White Mage ever since, and has given different reasons on different days, all true:

- **Someone had to keep Aya alive.** Aya chose Gladiator, sword and shield, on the way to **Paladin**, the knight who stands at the front and takes every hit ("because I like running into things first", which was true of her whole life). Paladin required Aya to level Conjurer to 15, and she complained so much that Mizuki felt square about the Arcanist. If her best friend was going to charge at everything in Eorzea, Mizuki wanted to be the one deciding whether she got up again.
- **The water glass.** A health bar is a glass at Mr Ishida's counter. Never let it run low; never make anyone ask. A good healer tops people up before they notice they were thirsty.
- **Her heroines.** Aerith in FFVII and Yuna in FFX were healers with backbone, and Sailor Mercury was the clever one at the back who kept the team together. She always liked the one who looked after people more than the one shouting at the front.
- **No blood, please.** The healer stands at the back watching health bars, not monsters' insides. It is the Battle Royale problem solved.
- **The okonomiyaki shop.** She grew up watching her mother keep a whole room happy at once, noticing who needed what before they asked. White Mage felt like running the front.
- **Omotenashi, with a staff.** That September, a few weeks after Mizu Nyan was born, Tokyo won the 2020 Olympics and "omotenashi", wholehearted hospitality, became the word of the year. Mizuki, bowing to strangers by day and healing strangers by night, thought it described her new hobby perfectly.

### First dungeons

Her first dungeon was **Sastasha**, a seaside smugglers' cave and the first dungeon nearly every player meets, at level 15. Aya tanked; two strangers from the Duty Finder made up the four. Mizuki was so nervous she healed everyone to full after every single hit, like a waiter refilling a glass after every sip, and ran out of mana in the first room. She got the coral puzzle wrong twice (a note tells you which colour of coral opens a hidden door) and typed "sorry" in the chat four times in twenty minutes, the most Japanese thing she has ever done in a video game. As the party broke up, one stranger typed "nice heals". She took a screenshot, and still has it.

**Tam-Tara Deepcroft**, a catacomb full of hooded cultists, scared her in the good way, the Ringu way. **Copperbell Mines** taught her to stand out of things: watching Aya's health bar, she stood in a bomb blast and died, and Aya had to explain to two strangers why the healer was on the floor. "The healer is being a carpet," Aya typed. Mizuki has never forgotten it.

Her first real rescue came in the **Thousand Maws of Toto-Rak**, a dripping prison full of giant insects that she hated on principle ("a dungeon made of things that should stay under a rock"). Aya pulled far too much at once and fell, and Mizuki, under real pressure for the first time, cast **Raise** and healed her back up in a desperate burst, and they won. She shouted "In the name of the moon!" at the television loudly enough that her mother called up the stairs again. Her father, at the hotplate, reportedly said, "She's a cat," as if that explained it.

And then **Haukke Manor**, the haunted mansion near Gridania at level 28: candlelit corridors, ghostly maids, portraits whose eyes follow you, and a vain lady of the house waiting at the end. Mizuki loved it instantly. It was Fumiko's kind of story, sweet and creepy with the fright saved for last. She walked through it slowly, looking at the paintings, while Aya waited impatiently at every door, and she still tells new players to go slowly and look at the paintings. She described it to Fumiko the next Saturday at Kuromon market, and Fumiko listened carefully and said, "Ah, an old one. The ghost who won't leave is always a woman who was too proud." Mizuki thought that was a better story than the game's.

### Autumn 2013: her place

By the end of 2013 she was logging in most nights. On late shifts she played in the morning with coffee and the shop's breakfast noises coming up through the floor; on early shifts she played in the evening until she fell asleep holding the controller. The smell of okonomiyaki sauce drifted up through the old floorboards, and on the screen Mizu Nyan's blue ears bobbed through the forests of the Black Shroud.

Next door, Kenta stayed loyal to Dragon Quest X, and the oldest rivalry in the family, Dragon Quest against Final Fantasy, went online. They took to knocking on the wall whenever either of them levelled up, one knock for him, two for her. "It was the most we had talked in years." Daisuke, on the phone from Kobe, heard she played White Mage and laughed: "Still the audience, then. Keeping everyone alive while they do the fighting." She told him he was wrong, thought about it on the train, and decided he was a little bit right and she didn't mind.

Takuya tried. One Sunday he watched her and Aya run Haukke Manor from the end of the bed, asked thoughtful questions ("Why is the lady angry?" "Why does the girl in armour keep running away from you?"), and concluded kindly that it seemed "a bit complicated". "Is he still there?" said Aya over voice chat. "He's always still there," said Mizuki, and meant it as the nicest thing she could say.

Eorzea had become what it still is: her place. "I bowed to strangers all day," she says, "then came home and kept strangers alive. It's the same job. One has a scarf."

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes Perfume on the Keihan** because those songs were her and Aya sharing earphones, half asleep, on the morning train to Kyoto.
- **Likes cities with some of the lights off** because the setsuden summer of 2011 taught her that half-lit streets photograph better; it is the root of her night photography.
- **Likes water refilled without asking** because it got her a job: it was her entry-sheet answer and her final-interview story.
- **Likes calm, quiet people (a surprise to her)** because Takuya showed her that silence with the right person isn't empty.
- **Likes the Kamo River's "equal-distance" couples** because that's where she realised she liked Takuya, and because the joke turned out to be true.
- **Likes whale sharks, and the feeling of awe** because Jinta at the Churaumi Aquarium made her feel small and fine about it; Sydney's aquarium having none is a grievance.
- **Likes Okinawan food (taco rice, sata andagi, umibudo, purple sweet potato ice cream)** because of four warm days with Aya; Okinawa soba is judged as itself, not as ramen.
- **Likes Wolf Children, The Garden of Words and "Sakura Nagashi"** because they are quiet, aching and beautiful, her favourite kind of thing, and each came with someone she loved.
- **Likes old games from Den Den Town baskets** because "old games respect your evenings".
- **Likes White Mage, Haukke Manor and the word "Nyan"** because Aya, Mr Ishida's counter and Fumiko's ghost stories all ended up in one character.
- **Dislikes black recruit suits and identical crowds** because shūkatsu made everyone try to be the same; she always looks for the one person who forgets to be.
- **Dislikes "praying mail"** because she got about twenty and can still spot a polite rejection from its first line.
- **Dislikes new pumps** because of the scar on her right heel, "the shūkatsu medal".
- **Dislikes soft noodles ("Futsu!")** because of the night Takuya ordered them at Mr Ishida's counter.
- **Dislikes goya** because it is "a vegetable that is angry with you personally", though she'll finish it out of stubbornness.
- **Dislikes falling asleep in the sun** because of the half-face Okinawa sunburn and the peeling cheek at her company entrance ceremony; hence the sunscreen fanaticism.
- **Dislikes gore, Attack on Titan above all** because "giants eating people, no, thank you", however good everyone says it is.
- **Dislikes losing film** because of the vanished Tofuku-ji roll; exposed film lives in a zipped pocket and she checks it every time she stands up.
- **Dislikes launch-day queues and error codes** because A Realm Reborn's first weekend was mostly a loading screen and cold takoyaki.
