## Chapter 12: The closed years (2020 to 2021)

These were the two years when the world shut its doors and Mizuki found herself on the wrong side of all of them. She was twenty nine when they began and thirty one when they ended, and in between she did not go home once. She lost a trip, a house, two housemates to Brazil, most of her shifts and, for a while, a good deal of her cheek. She gained a flat of her own, a pot of shiso, her mother's okonomiyaki recipe in writing for the first time in family history, a PlayStation 5 she had told everyone she didn't need, a promotion, and the one game ending she says "saved her life, a little bit, in a normal way". On air she talks about these years rarely and briefly: one quiet sentence, then she changes the subject to food. This chapter is the long version.

### January and February 2020: the smoke lifts

The year began under the same orange sky that had ended 2019. In early January the bushfire smoke still came and went over Sydney with the wind; some mornings the sun over Glebe was a flat red coin, and the shop at World Square smelled faintly of campfire under the broth. Mizuki had stopped photographing it. She kept her FM2 in its bag, wore one of the P2 masks Fiona had brought home from the hospital supply cupboard ("don't tell anyone, they're the spare spares"), and checked the fire maps on her phone the way people in Osaka check the typhoon forecast.

On Australia Day, Sunday 26 January, the Glebe house held its second Hottest 100 party in the backyard, because Rafa considered any national event an excuse for a barbecue and Mizuki considered any countdown an excuse to make a spreadsheet. She had picked her top ten in a notebook, with scores. She got two right. Billie Eilish's "bad guy" won, which Fiona had predicted on the first of January, and Fiona was insufferable about it until March. Mizuki's own small victory was that Tame Impala made the list several times, and she announced to the backyard that this was "music for walking home in the heat", a phrase she still uses. Three weeks later, on 14 February, Tame Impala released *The Slow Rush*, and she listened to it on repeat on the walk home from work up George Street and over the hill to Glebe. Valentine's Day in Japan is the day women give chocolate to men; in Australia it seemed to be the day everyone bought roses from a bucket at the train station. She bought herself a Tim Tam and called it cultural research.

The rain came in the first week of February. It came all at once, the way Sydney rain does: days of it, gutters overflowing on Glebe Point Road, the Blackwattle Bay end of the street turning into a small river. Most people in the shop complained. Mizuki went out in it on purpose, in the navy raincoat she had bought after her first Sydney storm, and stood under the frangipani in the front yard with her face up like a child. On 13 February the news said all the fires in New South Wales were contained. She took the FM2 out of its bag for the first time in weeks and photographed a puddle with the sky in it. "Blue," she wrote on the back of the print. "Finally."

Behind the rain, though, another story was starting. Lunar New Year had fallen on 25 January, and Haymarket had been as loud and red as ever, with lion dancers outside the Dixon Street shops. But by February the crowds were thinner. The Chinese tourists who usually filled the food courts weren't coming; students from China couldn't get back for the university year. People in Chinatown wore masks, and this time it was not the smoke. Mr Kondo watched the news on his phone during the quiet hour, frowning, and began to hum.

Mizuki, at that point, had other things on her mind. She had a plan.

### The plan

Her plan for 2020 was simple and she had been making it for a year. She would fly home in early April for her thirtieth birthday, her first trip back to Osaka since she'd left with one suitcase in March 2018. She had saved for it since Melbourne. She had booked the flights in November, to Kansai with one change, two and a half weeks in total, and Mr Kondo had approved the leave in his roster book with a little drawing of a plane.

Yumiko had planned the menu. It came by message in instalments over the summer, numbered, like a battle plan: (1) pork and kimchi okonomiyaki at the counter, third seat from the left, on the first night, "no arguments"; (2) a visit to Fumiko's grave at the temple with incense and Fumiko's favourite rice crackers; (3) Grandma Chiyo's house, where Mizuki would be told she was too thin and given money she was not allowed to refuse; (4) hanami at Osaka Castle with Aya and her parents, if the blossom held; (5) karaoke, "your mother will sing first, this is not negotiable"; (6) the birthday itself, with a strawberry shortcake from the bakery in Tennoji where Mizuki had worked Saturdays in high school. Hiroshi's contribution to the plan, relayed by Yumiko, was one line: "Tell her the Tigers are starting the season at home."

Mizuki had a list too. Presents: Tim Tams for her mother, who had decided from photos that they were "a dangerous biscuit"; a jar of Vegemite for Kenta, as a prank, with a note saying it was a delicacy; a tin of macadamias for Chiyo; a pair of sheepskin slippers for her father's cold feet in the shop's back room; a koala keyring for Aya's bag; Lily's recommendations of Australian lollies for Daisuke's two sons. She had already bought most of it and packed it in a cardboard box under her bed in Glebe. That box stayed under the bed for fifteen months.

### March 2020: the world shuts

March happened very fast, and afterwards she could never quite put it in the right order.

First, the toilet paper. In the first week of March, supermarket shelves in Sydney were stripped bare of it, and people fought in aisles over the last packs. Mizuki rang her mother in disbelief, and Yumiko laughed and told her about 1973, the oil shock, when housewives in Osaka had queued round the block for toilet paper after a rumour, and Fumiko, then in her forties, had refused to join the queue "on principle" and used old newspaper for a week out of pride. "Your grandmother would have been fine," said Yumiko. "She was always fine. It was the rest of us who suffered." Mizuki and Fiona did a careful inventory of the Glebe bathroom cupboard and found eleven rolls, which Fiona declared "enough for a small war", and Rafa built a little pyramid of them on the landing as a monument.

Then the shop. Office workers started working from home, and World Square, which at lunch was normally a river of lanyards, went quiet in a fortnight. Mizuki, who had spent a year arranging the water jugs so that every table was four steps away, now stood at the counter with nobody to give water to. On a Tuesday in the middle of March they served nine bowls in the lunch hour. Mr Kondo hummed all afternoon, a long, wandering tune with no end, which the staff had learned meant he was doing sums in his head.

On 20 March Australia closed its borders to everyone except citizens and residents. On 23 March restaurants and cafés were told to go takeaway only, and within a few days the whole of New South Wales was told to stay at home except for work, shopping, exercise and a few other reasons. The Japanese government, on the other side, was turning back foreign visitors and asking its own people abroad to think hard before travelling. Mizuki, holding a Japanese passport and an Australian work visa tied to a ramen shop, sat on her bed in Glebe and read the rules for an hour and understood less at the end than at the beginning.

The visa was the part that frightened her, and she didn't tell her mother. Her visa, which Mr Kondo had sponsored in 2019, was attached to the shop. If the shop closed, or let her go, she would have sixty days to find another sponsor or leave the country. When the government announced a wage subsidy to help businesses keep staff on the books, she read the fine print twice: people on temporary visas like hers weren't covered. That night she lay awake doing her own humming.

The next morning Mr Kondo sat everyone down at the counter, with a towel round his head as usual and his hands flat on the wood. He said the shop was staying open. He said nobody was being let go. He said everyone's hours would be cut and he would pay what he could, and that this was not a speech, because he didn't do speeches, and then he went into the kitchen and banged a stockpot for a while. Mizuki went from five shifts a week to two. It was enough for rent, just, and noodles, and nothing else. She never forgot that he'd kept her on. When people ask her on air why she has stayed at the same shop for so long, the answer is that week.

### Takeaway ramen and the cat called Menma

Ramen, Mizuki will tell anyone, is the worst takeaway food on earth. It is a dish that is perfect for about four minutes. The noodles keep cooking in the soup; the soup cools; the fat sets; the egg goes grey; by the time it reaches somebody's kitchen table in Ultimo it is "a sad warm puddle with spaghetti in it". For Mr Kondo, who had spent nearly fifteen years in Sydney perfecting a miso broth, sending it out in plastic containers felt like sending his children to school in pyjamas.

So the two of them made a project of it, standing at the pass during the long empty afternoons. They packed the broth separately, hot, in a sealed tub. They cooked the noodles thirty seconds firmer than usual and packed them in a separate box with a little oil so they wouldn't stick. Toppings went in a third, smaller container: chashu, bamboo shoots, spring onion, the egg cut in half at the very last moment, nori in its own paper sleeve so it wouldn't go soft. Mizuki, remembering Mr Ishida's rule that the customer never waits, timed the walk from the counter to the delivery riders' bench by the escalator: forty seconds. She moved the bench.

The packaging needed instructions, and that was where Lily came in. Lily was nineteen that year, in her second year at UTS, studying from her bedroom on a laptop with a cracked corner, and bored out of her mind. She drew a cat on a round sticker for the lid: a fat, round-faced cat with a towel round its head like Mr Kondo, holding chopsticks. Under it she wrote the steps. "1. Heat the soup until it's angry. 2. Noodles in the bowl. 3. Soup on the noodles. 4. Toppings on top like a crown. 5. Do NOT microwave the egg (Menma is watching)."

The cat was named Menma, after the bamboo shoot topping, because Lily said it was "brown, chewy and underrated", and Mizuki said that was the cruellest thing she had ever heard about a cartoon. Lily drew a new Menma every week: Menma slurping, Menma in a mask, Menma lying flat on the floor saying "stay home", Menma in a Tigers cap (that one was for Mizuki), Menma crying at a microwave. Customers began keeping the stickers. One regular, a young nurse from the hospital up the road, told Mizuki she had a row of them on her fridge, and asked if there was a Menma calendar. There wasn't, but Mr Kondo, without saying anything, had a stamp made for the takeaway bags. Mizuki still has a sheet of the original stickers in her journal from that year.

The regulars changed shape. The tradie in hi-vis who ordered tonkotsu with extra garlic every Wednesday kept coming, because building sites never closed, and he started ordering two, one for "the missus, who says your egg is better than mine". The quiet Japanese businessman who used to eat alone and leave exact change disappeared; Mizuki assumed he'd gone home, and she missed him in a way she couldn't quite explain, as if a clock had stopped. The elderly woman who came every Monday for the shoyu rang the shop instead, and Mizuki, on her own initiative, walked her bowl to her flat in Ultimo on the way home and left it on the doorstep with a knock. "Too thin," said the woman through the door. "Thank you," said Mizuki. "Both," said the woman.

### 10 April 2020: thirty in the Glebe house

Mizuki turned thirty on Friday 10 April 2020. It was Good Friday, so Sydney was shut anyway, and it was also, by coincidence that she found almost too much to bear, the day *Final Fantasy VII Remake* came out around the world: the game she had watched her cousin Daisuke play over his shoulder at Fumiko's in 1997, remade, on the birthday she was supposed to spend in Osaka.

The cancellation email from the airline had come ten days earlier, polite and full of apology, offering a credit she could use "when travel resumes". She photographed the email on her phone screen with the FM2, which is a silly thing to do with a film camera and exactly why she did it. She calls that print the saddest photo she owns. It is grey and slightly blurred, with her own reflection faint in the screen, and she keeps it in an envelope rather than an album.

The house would not let her be sad on the day. Bia, who had been told the date by Fiona, decorated the kitchen with paper streamers that Rafa hung slightly too low, so everybody hit their head on them all day. Fiona, who was on a run of night shifts, set an alarm for two in the afternoon to be up for it and came downstairs in her dressing gown and a paper crown left over from Christmas. Rafa grilled sausages and picanha in the courtyard as if it were a festival. And together, with no strawberry shortcake from the Tennoji bakery available within nearly eight thousand kilometres, they made one: a slightly lopsided sponge, cream that Bia whipped by hand because the mixer had died, and strawberries arranged on top in the shape of a "30". The 3 was good. The 0 was "more of an egg". Mizuki says it was perfect, and it is the happiest photo she owns, right next to the saddest one.

At six in the evening Sydney time, five in Osaka, her family called on the laptop, propped on the kitchen bench against the fruit bowl. They had closed the shop early, which her father never did. Her mother, father and Kenta sang "Happy Birthday" in a kind of round, because the connection lagged and nobody started at the same time. Her mother then said, "You're not late this time. You're just not coming," and laughed, and then had to leave the frame for a minute. Her father held up a single strawberry shortcake slice from the Tennoji bakery to the camera, and then, deadpan, ate it in front of her. "For you," he said, with his mouth full. It was the funniest thing he had done in years, and Mizuki laughed until she cried, and then just cried, and Fiona passed her the tissues without a word, Scottish nurses being experts in timing.

Aya called later, at ten, from her flat in Osaka, still in her hospital lanyard after a long shift at the pharmacy, and they had a can of chu-hi together over the screen. Aya's present, which arrived by post in June because the international post had slowed to a crawl, was a set of purikura-style stickers she had printed of the two of them at fifteen, with "THIRTY: STILL BOKE" written across the top.

Kenta's present was different and very Kenta. He bought *Final Fantasy VII Remake* on the day it came out and, over the following weeks, played it on video call while she watched from Glebe, phone propped on her pillow. He said nothing much; he just played. It was exactly how she had first met Final Fantasy, behind Daisuke at Fumiko's kotatsu, and both of them knew it without saying so. She complained about the menus, the loud parts and the length ("they made one disc into a whole game, and it's only Midgar"), and then, when the music from the original came up in a new arrangement, she went completely silent. When Daisuke heard, he sent a one-line message from Kobe: "The audience is back." She printed it and stuck it inside her journal.

### Why she didn't go home

People sometimes ask, years later, why she didn't simply fly home in 2020, since she was Japanese and Japan would always let her in. She answers carefully, because the honest answer is a tangle.

Flights in and out of Australia that year became rare and very expensive, and the few that ran were often cancelled. Arriving in Japan meant two weeks of quarantine. Coming back to Australia was the hard part: her visa allowed her to leave, but returning needed an exemption, there were caps on how many people could fly in each week, and arrivals had to spend fourteen days in hotel quarantine, which before long people had to pay for themselves. And her job, which Mr Kondo was holding onto with both hands, would not wait for her for months. If she went home, she might not get back to her room, her shop, her visa or the life she had built a piece at a time. On the news, a politician said that visitors who couldn't support themselves should think about going home. She understood the logic and felt the words like a cold draught all the same.

So she stayed, and her mother, who had been brave all through March, was not brave in May. On a Sunday evening call Yumiko said, half joking and half not, that Mizuki should "just come home, there's a room, there's a hotplate, there's work". Mizuki, tired from a week of counting coins, snapped that it wasn't that simple and her mother didn't understand anything about visas. Her mother said she understood perfectly well what it was like to have a daughter on the other side of the world in a pandemic. There was a silence of the kind that happens only on video calls, when both people are frozen and neither can tell if the connection has dropped. Then Hiroshi's hand appeared in frame, put a cup of tea in front of Yumiko, and withdrew. The call ended soon after.

Mizuki sent her mother a photo of a folded crane the next morning, without words, which in the Tanaka family is the same as an apology. Her mother replied with a photo of the okonomiyaki flour bag. The next Sunday, the lessons began.

### Sunday nights: okonomiyaki by video call

Yumiko had been making okonomiyaki for over thirty years, and Hiroshi longer, and neither of them had ever written the recipe down. It lived in their hands and in phrases like "a bit of this", "enough", and "until it looks right". When Mizuki was small she had watched from the third seat at the counter; at university she had made it on the shop hotplate under supervision. But alone in Sydney, in a share-house kitchen with a borrowed electric frying pan and Australian cabbage, she realised she couldn't do it. Her versions were "fine", which is the worst word an Osaka person can say about food.

So every Sunday evening, starting in late May 2020 and carrying on for most of the year, they had a lesson. Sydney was an hour ahead of Osaka in winter, two hours from October, and Mizuki got it wrong at least three times, once turning up at the kettle an hour early and once an hour late ("you're late", said her mother, of course). She propped her phone against the kettle on the Glebe kitchen bench; her mother propped hers against the till at the shop, before evening service in quiet weeks, or at the low table upstairs on Sundays when the shop was shut.

It was hard to turn "a bit of this" into numbers, and Mizuki made her mother do it anyway, with a notebook open beside the bowl. The rules that came out of those Sundays she can recite on air without looking:

- **Nagaimo.** Grated mountain yam in the batter, for fluffiness. She found it frozen at the Japanese grocer in Crows Nest, at a price that made her say a word her mother pretended not to hear.
- **Dashi, not water.** The batter is thin, made with cold dashi, and rested in the fridge while you cut the cabbage.
- **The cabbage.** Cut fine, but not too fine; Hiroshi said shredded cabbage was "coleslaw", which in his mouth is an insult. About a centimetre, roughly, and not the core.
- **Tenkasu** (crunchy tempura bits) and a little red pickled ginger, folded in at the last moment, gently, "like you are tucking it in to sleep".
- **Mix with air.** Not stirring; lifting.
- **Pork on top.** Thin slices of pork belly laid on the raw side so they cook crisp when it's flipped.
- **The flip** is one movement, with two spatulas, and confidence. Hesitation is how you end up with half on the floor.
- **Never press it down.** "Your father will cry," said Yumiko, and in the background her father said, "I will," without looking up.
- **Sauce, mayonnaise, aonori, katsuobushi,** in that order, and the katsuobushi should dance in the heat "like it's happy to see you".

Hiroshi passed behind the screen most weeks and said one thing. "Too much cabbage." "Pan's not hot." "That's a good one." Once, after she'd made a particularly ugly one that fell apart on the flip: "Tastes the same in the dark." Mizuki was so pleased by "that's a good one" the first time he said it, in July, that she rang Aya straight afterwards to report it. Aya, who had known Hiroshi for seventeen years, said that was the longest compliment she'd ever heard him give, and that she would like it on a T-shirt.

The Glebe house were the test audience. Rafa, who had been a sceptic of "Japanese pizza" since 2018, ate three on the first Sunday and declared it "a churrasco for vegetables". Fiona, coming off a shift, ate hers standing at the bench, half asleep, and said it was "a hug with sauce". Bia took notes in Portuguese.

The lessons were also how Mizuki learned what was happening to the shop at home. Osaka had its own state of emergency in April and May, and restaurants were asked to close early; the shop, which had served at its counter since 1964, started selling okonomiyaki to take away in little paper boxes for the first time in its history. Hiroshi hated the boxes for exactly the reason Mizuki and Mr Kondo hated the ramen tubs: the steam made everything soft. Father and daughter, two cooks in two cities, spent one whole Sunday call complaining about steam, and Yumiko said it was the most she'd heard Hiroshi talk since their wedding. Later in the year the regulars came back, slowly, in masks, sitting at alternate seats with plastic screens between them. Grandma Chiyo, still in her own house in Abeno that year, phoned the shop every week to complain about the price of masks, compare it to the price of masks in 1957, and ask whether Mizuki was eating.

By the end of 2020, Mizuki's okonomiyaki was officially "almost acceptable", in her mother's words. In the Tanaka family this is a gold medal. She has made it on Sunday nights ever since, never presses it down, and keeps the notebook from those calls, with its spattered pages and her mother's numbers, in the kitchen drawer in Pyrmont next to the spatulas.

### Takarazuka on two screens

The other Sunday ritual, from the middle of 2020, was her mother's idea. The Takarazuka Revue had suspended its performances in the spring, and Yumiko, who had been a fan since she was a teenager and had taken Mizuki to her first show at the Grand Theatre in 1999, was beside herself. She had a cupboard of DVDs. So did Mizuki, now: before she left Osaka her mother had given her a box of copies, "for emergencies".

They watched them together, apart. On a Saturday night once a month they would each put the same DVD in, Mizuki on her laptop on the bed in Glebe and Yumiko on the television upstairs from the shop, with the phone between them on speaker. Yumiko would count down in the voice of a stage manager, "san, ni, ichi," and they would both press play. It never quite worked; one of them was always two seconds ahead, and whoever was ahead would get told off for spoilers. *The Rose of Versailles* was first, because it was her mother's first love, then *Elisabeth*, with its dark, beautiful figure of Death, which Mizuki thought was the most romantic and frightening thing she had ever seen at nine years old and still thinks so now.

Her mother sang along to every number, in key, because Yumiko is the karaoke queen of Abeno and has never sung a song quietly in her life. Mizuki would hear Hiroshi in the background saying "neighbours", and Yumiko saying "the neighbours are fans", which was true. They cried at the same moments, nearly eight thousand kilometres apart, and laughed at each other for crying. Mizuki ironed her work shirts while they watched, because her mother always ironed during Takarazuka, and she found she couldn't watch it without an iron in her hand. Even now, she says the smell of a hot iron makes her hear a forty-person chorus.

### The Glebe house, very quiet

The house on Glebe Point Road had always been loud. In 2020 it learned to tiptoe.

Fiona worked at a big hospital in Camperdown, and that year her shifts were long, strange and hard. She did nights more often than before, slept in the day with a towel taped over her window, and came home in the grey morning with marks on her face from her mask. She did not talk about work, and the house learned not to ask. What Mizuki did instead was leave food. Most mornings before her own shift, she'd put two onigiri on a plate in the fridge, wrapped in cling film, with a sticky note: salmon on the left, ume on the right. Fiona's notes back became a sort of conversation on the fridge door. "THE UME ONE ATTACKED ME. 10/10. F." "Salmon please. I am not brave this week. F." "WHO TOOK MY YOGHURT. Not you. I know it wasn't you. You're too tidy for crime. F."

The rest of the house carried on as best it could. Rafa's job at a Brazilian steakhouse in the city had disappeared in March, when the restaurant closed its doors, and he filled his days by barbecuing in the courtyard with what he could afford, which turned out to be a lot of sausages. Bia's English course went online, and she did her classes at the kitchen table in a blouse and pyjama bottoms, waving to Mizuki off-camera. Like half of Australia, the house went through a baking phase. Fiona discovered banana bread, made it four times in a fortnight, and announced on the fridge door that she was "now 40 per cent banana". Mizuki, who had spent her Saturdays in high school at the bakery in Tennoji, made melon bread from memory instead, with the crackly sugar crust, and got it right on the third attempt. She refused, on principle, to start a sourdough. "I already have one thing in a jar to keep alive. It's me."

On her days off she walked. Exercise was one of the reasons you were allowed out, and she exercised furiously, with the FM2 round her neck. She walked round Blackwattle Bay and Wentworth Park, under the fig trees where the fruit bats hung in the day, through Glebe's back streets to Annandale and back, and down to Pyrmont, which she didn't know well then. She photographed empty streets: a closed café with its chairs stacked on the tables, a bus with nobody on it, the light rail sliding past an empty stop, a hand-written sign in a shop window that said "Back soon x". She shot mostly black and white that year, she says, because the colour felt wrong. Those rolls are some of the best photos she has ever taken, and she doesn't like looking at them.

### Rafa and Bia go home (winter 2020)

By June it was clear to Rafa and Bia that they couldn't stay. Rafa's restaurant wasn't reopening any time soon, Bia's student visa was costing money they no longer had, and their families in São Paulo were worried in the way families are when they can only see you on a phone. They found seats on a long, expensive route home through the Middle East and booked for the middle of July.

They had a farewell churrasco in the courtyard on the last Sunday, in coats, on a cold blue winter afternoon, with exactly as many people as the rules allowed. Rafa cooked everything left in the freezer. Bia made her potato salad with raisins one last time, the one Mizuki had been complaining about since their first Christmas, and Mizuki ate a full bowl of it, with a straight face, as a gift. Bia noticed and cried. Fiona, who had swapped a shift to be there, made a toast in Glaswegian so thick that only Mizuki, after two years of training, could follow it.

The presents were small. Rafa gave Mizuki his barbecue tongs, ceremonially, "because you are the only one who cleans them". Bia gave her a Brazilian ribbon to tie on her wrist for a wish, which she wore until it fell off in the shower in December. Mizuki gave each of them a folded paper crane, the way Fumiko had taught her, and wrote a wish inside each wing. She didn't tell them what the wishes were. They have both kept the cranes; Bia sends a photo of hers, on a shelf in São Paulo, every year on Mizuki's birthday.

The morning they left, Mizuki got up at five to make onigiri for the plane, wrapped them in paper and wrote on each one what it was, in English and Portuguese, the Portuguese copied carefully from her phone. She hugged them at the front gate under the frangipani, which had dropped all its leaves for winter, and then she went inside and cleaned the whole kitchen, which is what she does instead of crying.

Their room stayed empty. Nobody was moving to Sydney that year. The house, which had been a little United Nations of noise, became two women on different body clocks: Mizuki up at eight, Fiona asleep until three, passing each other in the kitchen like ships, with the fridge door as their noticeboard. The landlord dropped the rent, slightly, and Mizuki wrote him a thank-you card, which he didn't know what to do with.

Rafa and Bia still message on her birthday every year, without fail. Rafa's message is always a photo of a barbecue.

### Nana, the friend who says yes

Nana Yoshida, her friend from Kobe whom she'd met in a Japanese grocery near Town Hall in 2018 while complaining out loud about the price of curry roux, worked as a nail artist in Chatswood. In late March the nail salons were closed, and Nana, who had never been still for more than an hour in her life, was suddenly at home in a small flat with a lot of nail polish and nobody's hands to paint.

Their K-pop dance class in the city had gone online, with the teacher on a laptop in her living room and twenty little squares of students crashing into their furniture. Nana and Mizuki did it together on video call on Thursday nights. When TWICE's "More & More" came out in June, Nana learned the chorus choreography in two days and sent Mizuki a video of herself doing it in her kitchen, in socks, between the fridge and the oven, with the title "YOUR TURN". Mizuki learned it in the Glebe hallway, the only space long enough, and Fiona came out of her room at four in the afternoon, sleepy, and sat on the stairs to watch, as the audience. She clapped at the end and went back to bed. Mizuki and Nana kept up the video swaps all year: TWICE's "I Can't Stop Me" in the autumn, BTS's "Dynamite" because Nana insisted, and one, in a moment of madness, to Morning Musume's "Love Machine", for old times' sake. They have the whole collection in a shared folder called "Kitchen Idols". It is not to be shared with anyone, ever. Mizuki has threatened to retire if Scott asks.

Nana is the friend who says yes to everything, and in 2020 that meant saying yes to the small things that were allowed. In June, when the rules loosened, she said yes to a picnic on a blanket at Wentworth Park, each with their own food two metres apart, sharing nothing but the view of greyhounds being walked. In the spring she said yes to Mizuki's suggestion of a jacaranda walk in Kirribilli at seven in the morning, before the crowds, and yes again to eating takoyaki from a food truck after it, though they both agreed it was too doughy and had no octopus to speak of. She played *Animal Crossing* all through the first lockdown, and sent Mizuki screenshots of her island, which had a shrine, a torii gate and, after a request, a small okonomiyaki stall. Mizuki didn't own a Switch, so she visited the island the only way she could: through Nana's photos, and gave each one a score.

### Aya, Kenta and the faraway faces

Aya, at home in Osaka, was busier than she'd ever been. As a hospital pharmacist, she worked long days in 2020 and 2021, and on some calls she was so tired she fell asleep sitting up. Mizuki let her. On other nights they watched Japanese dramas "together", pressing play at the same moment the way Mizuki did with her mother, and Aya, as ever the tsukkomi, provided running commentary on every bad decision any character made. They watched the *Hanzawa Naoki* sequel in the summer of 2020, because Aya said everyone at the hospital was watching it, and Mizuki became addicted to the furious shouting in suits. When Aya's shiba, Mame, wandered into frame, the call stopped while Mizuki said "kawaii" eleven times.

Aya was the one who noticed, in the second half of 2020, that Mizuki's jokes had gone thin. She said so, as only a best friend of seventeen years can: "You're allowed to be sad. You're not allowed to be boring." Mizuki laughed, which was the point, and then talked for an hour, which was the other point.

Kenta, in Osaka, did what Kenta does: he sent photos without words. Dotonbori in April 2020, almost empty, the Glico running man shining over a bridge with nobody on it. The Midosuji line at rush hour with six people in the carriage. The shop counter with plastic screens between the seats. A cat on the Shitennoji wall where Mizuki had fallen off her bike in 1999. And one in May of the family's spare room, with the bed made up, which she understood as a message from her mother delivered by her brother, and which she didn't answer for two days.

The family group chat, still named "Mizuki's barefoot country" after her first week in Sydney, became the place where the whole family talked every day. Her mother sent photos of food; her father sent the Tigers' results, which were not good (the 2020 season started late, in June, and ended with them in second place, a long way behind Tokyo's Giants). Kenta sent a photo of an engagement ring at New Year 2021, without a word, and the whole chat exploded. Mizuki, at one in the morning in Glebe, called him immediately. He picked up and said, "Yeah." That was the whole announcement. Miho, in the background, was laughing.

Daisuke sent New Year greetings from Kobe, his two sons in matching jumpers. Mr Ishida's New Year card, brush-written as always, arrived at the Glebe house in the middle of February 2021, six weeks late, because the post from Japan was so slow. It said: "Keep the broth hot. Ishida." She stuck it on the fridge next to Fiona's notes.

### Eorzea holds the line

Every night that year, straight after dinner, Mizu Nyan logged in. Tsukimi Noodles, the little Free Company of Japanese people in Australia and Australians learning Japanese, became the one place where Mizuki spoke her own language every day, about nothing in particular, with people who laughed at the same things. Kazu, the leader, a Japanese dad in Sydney's north-west who played a Roegadyn tank, was homeschooling two children through the lockdowns and logged on each night with the voice of a man who had explained fractions eleven times. He still logged off at nine sharp on school nights, lockdown or not, "because rules are rules, even when there is no school".

They didn't do much that was difficult. They ran old raids for the fun of it, did treasure maps, and sat on the dock in Limsa Lominsa fishing together in silence for an hour, which Mizuki says is the most Japanese thing you can do in a video game. When patch 5.3 came out in August 2020 and closed the main story of Shadowbringers, the whole FC played it on the same night and met in the house afterwards, in the Lavender Beds, to sit in a circle without saying much. Mizuki redecorated the house that winter as a winter onsen, with a steaming pool, lanterns and a sign over the door saying "No shoes, no tank stance". Kazu said it was the best thing anyone had done for him all year. She thinks he might have meant it.

### Spring and summer: small doors opening

Through the second half of 2020, Sydney opened a little at a time. Dine-in came back at the shop in limited numbers in the winter, with tables spaced out and a QR code for checking in at the door, which Mizuki explained to customers so many times she began to do it in a song. Her shifts went from two to three. The office workers did not come back; World Square at lunch, in the spring of 2020, still had the feeling of a school in the holidays.

In September she caught a bad cold, the ordinary kind, with a sore throat and a nose like a tap. In any other year she'd have worked through it with a mask, Japanese style. In 2020 it meant a test at a pop-up clinic in a car park, a queue of sniffing strangers in the drizzle, a long cotton bud that made her eyes water, and staying in her room until the negative result came through by text the next day. She spent the day embarrassed, sneezing, and apologising to Mr Kondo by message for no reason, as if a cold were a personal failure. He replied with a photo of a bowl of miso ramen and the words "Get well. Then argue with me." She was back on Friday.

In November, on her own, she did her yearly jacaranda walk, the purple hanami, through the streets of Glebe and Annandale where the trees met over the road. The jacarandas didn't know anything had happened. That was the thing she liked about them that year.

Christmas 2020 was small and uncertain. A cluster of cases on the Northern Beaches just before Christmas brought new rules for Greater Sydney, and everyone's plans shrank again. Mizuki and Fiona had Christmas in the Glebe courtyard, just the two of them, with Rafa's tongs, cold prawns from the fish market and a strawberry shortcake from the Japanese bakery in the city, carried home on the bus on her knees like a newborn, as tradition required. Fiona, who hadn't been home to Glasgow in two years either, rang her own family at eleven at night, and Mizuki heard her laughing through the wall for an hour, then quiet. In the morning there was a note on the fridge: "Thanks for the cake. Thanks for the year. F."

On New Year's Eve she made toshikoshi soba, the noodles for seeing out the year, and watched the Kōhaku song contest from Japan on her laptop at the kitchen table, with the harbour fireworks banging faintly over the roofs. For the first time, it was held with no audience in the hall. She thought that was the saddest and most fitting thing she'd seen all year.

### Early 2021: the house breaks up

The Glebe house ended quietly, as the year had made everything end. The lease was coming up, the empty room couldn't be filled, and Fiona, worn out by the trip across town after night shifts, wanted to live near her hospital in Camperdown. They decided over a pot of tea in January: they would both move out in the autumn.

That summer had its good days. On Australia Day 2021 they did a Hottest 100 for two, and Glass Animals' "Heat Waves" won, which both of them had voted for, so for once nobody was insufferable. In late February Mizuki went to see *Demon Slayer: Mugen Train* at a cinema in the city, the film that had become the biggest film in Japanese history while she was stuck in Sydney. She went with Lily, who had never seen any Demon Slayer and was given a summary on the bus so detailed it took the whole ride. Mizuki loved Rengoku, cried at the end, and watched the fights through her fingers, as she does with anything violent. Lily, who had cried more, denied it.

In March the rain came again, harder than the year before, with floods out west of the city and the big dam overflowing. In Glebe the back door, which never quite shut, finally let in a puddle that reached the shoe rack. Mizuki's old canvas trainers, which she had bought in Amemura at twenty-three, sat in the water overnight and never recovered. She held a small funeral for them on the doorstep, with incense, which Fiona photographed.

For her thirty-first birthday, Saturday 10 April 2021, Sydney was open again, and Nana, who says yes to everything, said yes to the most Mizuki plan possible: an afternoon at a karaoke box in Haymarket, just the two of them, singing Utada Hikaru, TWICE and, for her mother, a Takarazuka number down the phone. Mizuki did the full "Love Machine" choreography on the sofa. Nana filmed it. It went into the Kitchen Idols folder.

### The PS5, "for work"

Mizuki has always said modern games are too long, too loud and have too many menus. She has never owned a new console at launch in her life, and when the PlayStation 5 came out in Australia in November 2020 at $749.95, she said loudly in the staff room that she didn't need one, it was for teenagers and people with money, and her old PS4 was fine.

Her old PS4 was not fine. She had bought it second-hand from a shop on George Street at the end of 2019, when the laptop from Japan began to overheat in every big fight (her Osaka PS4 was still in her old room above the shop), and by 2021 it sounded like a small aircraft taking off whenever Mizu Nyan walked into a busy city. Loading into Limsa took long enough to make a cup of tea. And in April 2021, Square Enix opened the beta of a proper PlayStation 5 version of Final Fantasy XIV, with faster loading and sharper graphics, ahead of its full release in May.

The trouble was that nobody could buy a PS5. They sold out in minutes every time a shop got stock, and resellers were asking twice the price. It was Lily who turned it into a mission. For three weeks, on their breaks, she refreshed shop websites on her phone and sent Mizuki screenshots of "SOLD OUT" with Menma crying underneath. Then, on a Thursday morning in what Mizuki's Japanese calendar insisted was spring and Sydney insisted was autumn, a stock alert came through while Mizuki was carrying two bowls of tonkotsu. Lily took the bowls out of her hands, gave her the phone, and said, "Go." Mizuki bought it at the counter in ninety seconds with her hands shaking, at full price, and then served the tonkotsu, which was now slightly cool, with a deep bow and a free egg.

She calls it her "work console". She told her mother it was "for the FC". She has, she will admit, also used it to play old games, which is the joke: she bought the most powerful machine in the house to play a game that came out in 2013 and to replay the PS1 classics she grew up with. When Mizu Nyan first loaded into Limsa Lominsa in two seconds, she made a noise that her neighbours probably heard. She kept the box, in the Japanese way, "in case", and the box has moved house with her ever since.

### May 2021: the Pyrmont flat

She found the flat in April. It was in Pyrmont, up the hill from Darling Harbour, in an older brick block a few streets back from the water: one bedroom, a kitchen just wide enough to turn round in, a bathroom with a stubborn hot tap, and a balcony just big enough for one folding chair. If she leaned out at the right angle, she could see a slice of the harbour. She took it on the spot, partly because of the balcony and partly because, when she stood on it, a lorikeet landed on the rail and looked at her as if to say, "Well?"

It was the first time in her life she had lived alone. She had grown up in two rooms above the shop with her parents and Kenta, lived at home through university in Kyoto, and shared the Glebe terrace for three years. She was thrilled, and also, on the first night, slightly terrified by the silence.

The move was in the middle of May. Fiona came straight from a night shift and carried boxes in her scrubs. Nana came in a hired van she had never driven before and drove it "like a taxi in Osaka", which was a compliment and a warning. Lily came for the last two hours and directed. Rafa's tongs, the Takarazuka DVDs, the cardboard box of presents for Osaka still unopened under everything, the PS5 in its original box, her father's FM2 in her hands the whole way. The only casualty was her gachapon shelf: a box of tiny capsule-toy food came open in the van, and her miniature takoyaki boat, a gift from Aya in 2008, was never found. She looked for it for a week. She still mentions it. "Somewhere in that van, there is a very small takoyaki that is lonely."

She furnished it the way her family had furnished the rooms above the shop: nothing wasted, everything within reach. A low table she sat at on a cushion, because she never got used to sofas. A shelf with the FM2 in the middle, like a little family altar, with her Vulpix keyring hanging off its strap. The framed print of Fumiko's hands shelling peas, the photo that won her a prize in 2008. A rice cooker that played a tune when it finished, "the only flatmate who never complains". Shoes off at the door, for everyone, with a smile that made it clear this was not a suggestion. Fiona was the first guest, and took her shoes off without being asked, which Mizuki says is the moment she knew Fiona was a friend for life.

Pyrmont suited her. She could walk to work over the old timber deck of Pyrmont Bridge and through Darling Harbour to World Square in twenty-five minutes. And the Sydney Fish Market was a short walk away at Blackwattle Bay: the nearest thing she had found to Kuromon and her Saturday mornings with Fumiko. She started going early on Saturdays to buy one good thing, the way her grandmother taught her: a dozen oysters, a piece of salmon, prawns, sea urchin when she felt rich. She had about five Saturdays of it before the city shut again.

### June to October 2021: the long lockdown

On Saturday 26 June 2021, Greater Sydney went back into lockdown. It was supposed to last two weeks. It lasted until 11 October.

It was harder than 2020, and she is honest about that. In 2020 she had been in a loud house with people in it. In 2021 she was in a small flat on her own, a month after moving in, with the boxes not all unpacked. The shop went takeaway again, and her shifts went back down to two. Mr Kondo hummed for most of July. Lily, studying online again, brought Menma back in a sequel series, starting with "Menma 2: The Return" and a cat looking very tired in a mask, and the regulars who'd kept the old stickers asked for the new ones. By August there was a rule that you couldn't go more than five kilometres from home for exercise or shopping, and Mizuki measured her five kilometres on a map with the seriousness of a surveyor. It included the fish market, World Square, the Chinese Garden of Friendship and Fiona's street in Camperdown. It did not include Chatswood, so Nana was out of range, and they went back to dance videos, now from two kitchens.

She built her days so they held together. One walk a day at sunset around Darling Harbour with the camera, photographing the empty promenade, the closed restaurants, the lights on the water, and the ibis, who had the whole waterfront to themselves and behaved like landlords. Dinner on the balcony every night, in her one chair, whatever the weather, even in a coat. Sunday okonomiyaki, now on a proper hotplate she'd bought for the new kitchen, with her mother on the phone against the rice cooker. Saturday Takarazuka. A Thursday call with Aya. FC nights. And twice a week she walked to Fiona's flat in Camperdown, inside her five kilometres, and left a bag on the doorstep with onigiri and a can of green tea, knocked, and waved through the window. Fiona was working nights again, long ones. The fridge-door notes had become messages on the phone. "UME AGAIN. You are a terrible influence. F."

On Friday 23 July she watched the opening ceremony of the Tokyo Olympics on her laptop at the low table, a year late, with no crowds in the stadium. When the athletes started to walk in and the music turned out to be from video games, she sat up straight. The first piece was the overture from Dragon Quest. Before it had even finished, a message arrived from Kenta, her Dragon Quest brother, in Osaka: a single thumbs up. When Final Fantasy music came on later, she sent him a single thumbs up back. It was the most competitive conversation they'd had in twenty years. She watched every night of the Games after work, and she says it was the strangest Olympics in the world and she loved it anyway.

Her vaccinations came in August and September, at a big hub, with a long queue of masked strangers who all said "thank you" to the nurses in turn. She sent a photo of her plaster to the family chat and her father replied, "Good," which is his version of a parade.

In September, needing something alive in the flat besides herself, she planted shiso on the balcony, from seed she'd bought in Crows Nest before lockdown. Shiso is the taste of summer at home: on cold tofu, with sashimi, torn into somen. She watered it every morning and talked to it, which she denies. When the first tiny leaves came up, she sent a photo to the family chat. Her mother replied with eleven exclamation marks.

There was one bad stretch, in the middle of August, which she mentions only in a sentence, if at all. For a week or so she didn't turn her camera on for calls, didn't take the FM2 out, and ate standing at the bench. Aya noticed first, from Osaka, and stayed on the phone with her on a Thursday night for three hours without making her talk much, just watching a drama together. Fiona, on her day off, came and sat on the balcony floor with her, one chair being one chair, and they shared a bag of hot chips. Nana sent a new dance video every day that week, each worse than the last on purpose. Mizuki says she was lucky in her friends. She says it lightly. She means it heavily.

### Late October 2021: Kenta and Miho

Sydney came out of lockdown on 11 October, and two and a half weeks later, on a Saturday at the end of October, her little brother got married in Osaka.

Kenta and Miho had kept it small and careful, the way weddings were in Japan that year: a ceremony at a shrine in south Osaka with close family only (and Aya, who had been at every Tanaka family occasion since she was twelve), then lunch at a restaurant. Miho, a nursery teacher from Sakai, calm and kind and two years younger than Kenta, wore a white kimono and looked, Mizuki said, "like the snow in Otaru". Kenta wore a black haori and looked, Mizuki said, "like Kenta in a costume". Aya was there as Mizuki's stand-in, and held the phone.

Mizuki watched on that phone, from Pyrmont, via Aya. She dressed for it. From the waist up she wore her best dress, a navy one she'd bought in Shinsaibashi for a hotel staff party in 2016, with Isamu's Seiko watch and lipstick; from the waist down, pyjama trousers with sheep on them. She set her phone on the low table on the balcony, propped against the shiso pot, with a glass of sparkling wine and a box of tissues within reach. Her mother had made Kenta's best man promise to point a phone at the important moments, and he did, sometimes at the right angle.

It was the strangest and most beautiful thing she watched that year: her little brother, the boy who played the sprite in *Secret of Mana* and stole her snacks, bowing to his new in-laws while she waved at a screen from a balcony nearly eight thousand kilometres away. At lunch, Aya carried the phone round the tables so Mizuki could greet everyone, like a small travelling guest. Grandma Chiyo told her she looked too thin "even on the internet". Hiroshi made the shortest wedding speech in the history of Osaka, a single sentence: "Look after each other, and come to the shop on Sundays." Yumiko cried, then hit Hiroshi with the programme for making her cry. And at the end, Kenta took the phone from Aya, looked straight into it, and bowed to his sister. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

She sent Australian wine and a koala tea towel. Miho sent back a handwritten thank-you card, with a little drawing of a koala in an apron, which has been on Mizuki's fridge ever since. On air, if the wedding comes up, she says it was beautiful, and that is all she says.

### Shift lead

When the city reopened, the shop came back faster than anyone expected. The office workers trickled back, then poured. Mr Kondo took on two new casual staff for the evenings, both students who had never worked in a Japanese kitchen and thought ramen came in a cup.

In November, at the end of a long Friday, over staff noodles at the counter, Mr Kondo told Mizuki he was making her shift lead. He said it the way he said everything important, looking at his bowl. She would do the rosters, open and close on his days off, train the new people, and hold a set of keys. There was a small pay rise, and a new name badge, which she photographed on the counter like a newborn. She bowed at exactly thirty degrees, in honour of Mr Hamada at the hotel in Umeda, and then ruined it by laughing.

She found out she was good at it. She trained the new staff the way she'd been trained: Mr Ishida's water rule, her mother's rule of remembering every order, Mr Hamada's bow, and Fumiko's rule of saving the best part for last (in a ramen shop, the egg). She taught them to say "irasshaimase" when people came in and to mean it. Lily, who had worked there longer than the new boss had been boss, called her "Boss" in a voice that was half respect and half revenge, and the Wednesday tradie, who had always called her "boss", said he'd been right all along.

### December 2021: Endwalker on the balcony floor

On Friday 3 December 2021, early access opened for *Endwalker*, the expansion that would finish the story she had been following since 2013. It had already been delayed by two weeks, and the launch was famously chaotic, with queues of thousands of players and an error code that the whole community learned by heart. Mizuki queued for two and a half hours on her new PS5 with half of Tsukimi Noodles on voice chat, complaining in two languages, eating chips and swapping queue positions like a stock market. She says it was the most fun she'd had all year.

For two weeks she played the story every night after work. It was about despair, and about hope anyway: about the end of the world seeming certain, and people finding reasons to keep walking towards it with their friends. She thought it felt written for that year, and for everyone stuck at home with a phone full of bad news. Square Enix, overwhelmed, briefly stopped selling the game to new players because the servers couldn't fit everyone in, and Kazu said this was "the first time in history a game was too popular to buy", which is not true but has become FC law.

She finished the main story at two in the morning on a weeknight, sitting on her balcony floor with the controller, the door open, the laptop speaker beside her, the shiso pot at her elbow and the harbour lights over the rooftops. She cried, but not the way she had at the end of Shadowbringers, when Fiona had knocked with a cup of tea. This was relief. "Endwalker is the reason I survived that year," she says, gently and completely, and it's one of the few sad things she says on air without a joke after it. Then, because she is herself, she adds the joke a minute later: the healers kept everyone standing to the very end, and everyone talks about the tank. "Nobody thanks the healer. Typical."

### The end of the closed years

By Christmas 2021 the borders were beginning, slowly, to move. Australia had started letting its own people come and go, and visa holders like her were promised they could return, but then a new variant arrived at the end of November and Japan shut its doors to foreign visitors again. She could have gone home now, in theory, through quarantine at both ends. In practice she had just been made shift lead, the shop was finally busy, and flights were full and ruinous. She sent her parents a photo of the cardboard box of presents, which had finally been opened in the Pyrmont flat: the Tim Tams were long gone and replaced, but the Vegemite for Kenta was still in perfect condition. "Some things last," she wrote. Her father replied with a photo of the shop hotplate, cleaned and shining, with nothing on it. She understood that too.

Christmas Day was a long lunch at Fiona's in Camperdown, the start of what became their monthly Sunday brunches, with Nana, a strawberry shortcake carried on her knees on the bus, and a bowl of Bia's raisin potato salad that Mizuki made herself from Bia's recipe as a joke, which nobody ate. She had a flat of her own, a balcony, a pot of shiso, a promotion, a shop family in Mr Kondo and Lily, an online family in Tsukimi Noodles, and friends who had kept her standing. She still hadn't been home. Her nickname for Australia was still "the barefoot country". But somewhere in those two years, a little against her will, Sydney had stopped being the place she was visiting and become the place she lived.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes Mr Kondo, permanently**, because he kept her on in March 2020 when he didn't have to. It's why she has stayed at the shop so long.
- **Hates takeaway ramen** ("a sad warm puddle with spaghetti in it"); if you must, broth, noodles and toppings in separate containers, and never microwave the egg.
- **Loves Menma**, Lily's cartoon cat on the takeaway stickers; keeps a sheet of the originals in her 2020 journal.
- **Loves Sunday-night okonomiyaki** made exactly to her mother's numbered rules from the 2020 video calls (nagaimo, dashi, cabbage "not coleslaw", never pressed down); treasures the spattered notebook.
- **Loves watching Takarazuka DVDs** "together" with her mother; *The Rose of Versailles* and *Elisabeth*; can't watch without ironing; a hot iron smells like a chorus to her.
- **Loves melon bread; refuses sourdough** ("I already have one thing in a jar to keep alive").
- **Loves Tame Impala** (*The Slow Rush*, February 2020) as walking-home music; YOASOBI's "Yoru ni Kakeru" and TWICE's "More & More" are lockdown songs.
- **Loves Nana's "Kitchen Idols" dance videos**, and will never let anyone see them.
- **Dislikes the word "fine" about food**: the worst thing an Osaka person can say.
- **Hates the silence of an empty shop** at lunchtime; World Square in 2020 "felt like a school in the holidays".
