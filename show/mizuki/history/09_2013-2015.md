## Chapter 9: The front desk in Umeda (2013 to 2015)

### April 2013: a uniform, a name badge and a bow

Mizuki started work on Monday 1 April 2013, the day Japanese company life begins for new graduates, nine days before her twenty-third birthday. She still had the last of an Okinawa sunburn peeling off her right cheek from the graduation trip with Aya, and on her first morning she covered it with concealer and hoped. She took this as a sign. She was not yet sure of what.

The job was on the front desk of a business hotel in Umeda, in Kita, the north side of central Osaka. She never names it, then or now. "It was a normal hotel," she says. "Normal is very hard to do well." It was tall, narrow and sensible, built for salarymen on two-night trips: about two hundred small, spotless rooms, each with a good bed, a desk facing a wall, a kettle, a trouser press and a little card explaining the trouser press in four languages. There was a breakfast buffet in the basement with miso soup beside the scrambled eggs and a rice cooker the size of a washing machine, and a lobby that smelled of coffee and carpet shampoo. It was about ten minutes' walk from the station, which in Umeda can mean ten minutes or forty, depending on which exit you come out of and how brave you are.

She had picked the hotel during the long job-hunting season of her third and fourth years, in a black recruit suit like every other student in Japan, sitting in group interviews where twelve young people in identical suits said identical things about teamwork. What she remembered from the hotel's final interview was that one of the two managers asked her what she did at Mr Ishida's ramen shop, and she said, "I make sure nobody waits for water," and he wrote something down and underlined it. She found out later he'd written "water" and nothing else.

Umeda was changing around her the month she arrived. Grand Front Osaka, the huge glass complex beside the north side of Osaka Station, opened on 26 April 2013, three and a half weeks into her career, and for the whole of Golden Week the crowds were so thick she could barely walk from the station to work. Half the guests that week asked her how to get to Grand Front. The other half had just come back from Grand Front and wanted to tell her about it. She went herself on her first day off, rode the escalators, looked at a robot, bought nothing and decided she preferred the old Umeda underneath it, the low, smoky lanes of yakitori stands under the railway arches. "New buildings are like new shoes," she said. "Everybody loves them. Nobody is comfortable yet."

The uniform was a navy jacket, a white blouse, a navy skirt, low black heels and a small blue-and-white scarf tied at the neck in a precise knot. She hated the scarf with her whole heart. It itched in summer, it came undone whenever she bowed, and it had to be retied in the staff-room mirror about nine times a shift. By the second week she had solved it with a tiny safety pin hidden behind the knot, a piece of engineering she still considers among her best work, and she passed the secret on to every new woman on the desk afterwards like a family recipe.

Her name badge said TANAKA in Roman letters and 田中 underneath. Within a month an American guest had read it as "Tanaka-san" and called her "Mrs Tanaka" for three days, and she hadn't the heart to correct him. Her mother, when she heard, was delighted. "Mrs Tanaka is me," Yumiko said. "So I've been working in a hotel in Umeda without knowing it. Very good. Bring my wages."

For the first month Yumiko told every regular at the okonomiyaki counter that "our Mizuki works at a hotel in Umeda now, in a uniform, with a name badge", in a voice that suggested she had joined the Cabinet. Her father said nothing at all about it for a week, then one evening, when she came through the back door in her navy jacket, he looked up from the hotplate, bowed very low, and said in a flawless, formal shop voice, "Irasshaimase." Then he did it every night for three months, until she started bowing back.

### Mr Hamada

Her shift manager was **Mr Hamada**, and for five years he was the most important person in her working life, though he would have been embarrassed to hear it.

He was in his fifties, slim, grey at the temples, with the neatest hair she has ever seen on a living person and a navy suit that seemed to be the same suit every day, though she suspected he owned five identical ones. He had worked in hotels since before she was born, first in Kobe and then in Osaka, and he moved through the lobby without a sound, so that guests and staff alike would turn round and find him already there. The staff called him "the ninja", but only very quietly, and only when he was definitely on another floor, which was never certain.

He bowed at exactly thirty degrees, every time, as if he had a protractor inside him. In her first week he taught her the bows, standing in the back office with his hands flat on his thighs: fifteen degrees, the eshaku, for a greeting or passing a guest in a corridor; thirty degrees, the keirei, for thanks and welcome; forty-five, the saikeirei, for a sincere apology. He made her do them against the wall until her back was right. "And for a really big mistake?" she asked. "There will not be a really big mistake," said Mr Hamada, which she found terrifying and very reassuring in equal measure.

He had sayings, though he never said them as if they were sayings. "The guest does not see the desk. The guest sees you." "If the guest has to ask, we are already late." (She told him that was Mr Ishida's rule about water, and he said, "Then Mr Ishida is a hotel man," which she passed on to Mr Ishida, who was enormously pleased and pretended not to be.) And, about the lobby: "Everything that happens here, happens in front of people. So nothing happens here." Problems were walked, gently, into the back office. Voices went down, never up.

He carried a small black notebook in his inside pocket and wrote in it with a propelled pencil, and the staff speculated endlessly about what was in it. Mizuki decided it was a list of everyone's mistakes. Years later she saw it open by accident and discovered it was mostly guests' names and small notes beside them: "Mr K., Sendai, prefers high floor, allergic to buckwheat", "Mrs O., wedding anniv. in June". It was Fumiko's way of remembering people, she realised, written down by a man in a suit. She has kept a version of it ever since: first a little notebook at the desk, now the notes she keeps about Scott.

He ate the same lunch every day, a bento his wife packed, always with a single pickled plum in the exact centre of the rice, and he ate it in eleven minutes. He drank hot canned coffee from the staff vending machine in winter and cold canned coffee in summer, and the day the machine switched from cold to hot every autumn, he said "Ah, autumn," the only remark about the weather anyone ever heard from him.

He praised nobody. When the businessmen who came every month from Hiroshima and Sendai began asking for "the Osaka girl with the scarf" by name, Mr Hamada noticed, wrote something in the notebook, and said nothing, which from him was a medal. Once, in her second year, after she had calmed a sobbing French student who'd lost her passport and walked her to the police box and back in her lunch break, he passed her at the desk and said, very quietly and without stopping, "Good." She went to the staff toilet and grinned at the mirror for a full minute.

### Keigo, and a tired mouth

Then there was keigo, the honorific Japanese that every Japanese person learns a little of and almost nobody uses perfectly. At the hotel there were three layers of it, and she had to switch between them without a seam: sonkeigo to raise the guest up, kenjōgo to lower herself and the hotel down, and teineigo, the plain polite layer, to hold it all together. You did not say "wakarimashita", I understand. You said "kashikomarimashita", a word so long and smooth that she said it a hundred times a day and still says it in her sleep. You did not say "chotto matte", wait a sec. You said "shōshō omachi kudasaimase", please be so kind as to wait a little moment. The hotel's opening line on the phone took her four seconds to say, and she timed it.

Osaka people talk to everyone, loudly, with jokes. Osaka-ben, her home dialect, is quick and round and full of punchlines, and it does not bow easily. "Akan" (no good) had to become "itashikanemasu" (I am afraid we are unable to); "honma?" (really?) had to become "sayō de gozaimasu ka". In the first month she let a "meccha" slip out to a guest from Tokyo ("this umbrella is meccha strong") and the guest laughed so much she was nearly forgiven. Mr Hamada, behind her, said nothing, which was worse.

For three months she went home with a tired mouth. Not a tired voice; a tired mouth, as if her lips had been doing gymnastics. She'd come up the back stairs, throw herself on the tatami, and talk to Kenta in the broadest, rudest Osaka-ben she could manage for ten minutes "to let the air out". Kenta, who was at university studying information engineering and spoke about four words an hour, put his headphones on.

It was the hardest part of the job, and it taught her the thing she still uses every day: how to be warm inside a polite shape. Hotel Japanese did not allow jokes, so she learned to let the joke live in her eyes instead, in a tiny pause before a punchline that never came, in the way she said "kashikomarimashita" to a guest who had asked for something faintly absurd, so that the guest knew she knew. Regulars noticed. A grey-haired engineer from Sendai told her on his tenth visit, "You are the only person in Japan who can say 'kashikomarimashita' sarcastically." She bowed fifteen degrees and said "Kashikomarimashita," and he laughed all the way to the lift.

She still says it now, to Scott, whenever he suggests something she considers his worst idea of the week. He has learned what it means.

### Living above the shop, and the Midosuji line

She still lived at home, upstairs from the okonomiyaki shop in Abeno, in the same narrow room she'd had since she was small, with a futon, a low desk, a bookshelf of manga and photography books, Fumiko's prize photo of her own hands shelling peas on the wall (Fumiko had insisted Mizuki keep a print), and a window that looked across the lane at a wall of air-conditioner units. Most young Japanese people with jobs in their own city live with their parents until they marry, and nobody thought it odd, least of all Mizuki, who had never paid for her own laundry powder in her life.

Her mother refused to take rent. Then, having refused, she charged her in dishwashing: three nights a week, whatever shift Mizuki had worked, there was a stack of okonomiyaki plates and spatulas waiting for her in the shop sink after closing. "That's rent," said Yumiko. "In Umeda you'd pay a hundred thousand yen for a room the size of a cupboard. Here you pay plates." Mizuki did the sums once and decided it was a very good deal, and also that her mother had done the sums first.

The family's evenings had a shape. The shop closed at ten. Her father scraped the hotplate in long, even strokes, a sound she can still hear if she closes her eyes. Her mother counted the till and wrote the day's takings in a ledger in tiny handwriting, which **Grandma Chiyo**, her father's mother, came in to inspect every Sunday afternoon with her reading glasses on the end of her nose. Chiyo lived on her own in the house with the bonsai, three streets away by the Hankai tram line, walked with a folding umbrella in all weathers whether or not it was raining, and could find a two-hundred-yen mistake in a month of figures in under a minute. "Mizuki-chan," she'd say, "a hotel girl should know the price of everything." Then she'd ask what the hotel charged for a single room on a Saturday and tut for a long time.

Yumiko still said "you're late" instead of "welcome home", whatever time Mizuki came in. Arrive at eleven at night from a late shift: "You're late." Arrive at half past two in the afternoon from an early shift: "You're late." Once, on a day off, Mizuki came downstairs at seven in the morning, before her mother had even opened the shutters, and Yumiko looked at her and said, "You're late," and they both laughed so hard her father came out of the back with a spatula to see who had died.

The commute was the Midosuji line, the red line, the main artery of the Osaka subway, which runs straight up the spine of the city from Tennoji to Umeda in about a quarter of an hour: Tennoji, Dobutsuen-mae, Daikokucho, Namba, Shinsaibashi, Hommachi, Yodoyabashi, Umeda. She knew every station by its smell and its sound. Namba smelled of sweet bread from the bakery near the gates. Shinsaibashi was the station where the girls with good handbags got on. Yodoyabashi was where the bankers got off, all at once, like a flock of very tidy birds. She liked having grown up in Minami, the south, and working in Kita, the north. "Like a home and a holiday house in the same city," she said. "And the holiday house has more escalators."

The morning train was full of people pretending not to be asleep. She learned the commuter arts: how to sleep standing up with one hand on the strap and wake exactly at your stop; how to read a paperback folded in one hand; how to make yourself thin. On the platforms at Umeda in the morning rush the station staff in white gloves really did help push the last passengers into the carriages on the worst days, and she watched a foreign guest of the hotel photograph it once with real joy, as if it were a festival. "It is a festival," she told him. "The festival of being late."

Shift work meant three rhythms. The early shift began at six, which meant an alarm at four forty, the first train, the city still grey, and her mother already awake in the kitchen downstairs, because her mother is always already awake. The day shift was civilised. The late shift ran until eleven and finished with a sprint for the train, a seat if she was lucky, and the smell of everyone's after-work beer. About once a month she covered a night shift, the long quiet one, doing the night audit at the computer, answering the phone to drunk guests who had lost their room numbers, and watching the lobby at four in the morning, when it looked like a stage after the actors have gone home.

Kenta, by then at university, was the one person in the house who was awake at strange hours too. On the nights she came in from a late shift they sometimes overlapped in the kitchen, him making instant ramen, her eating whatever her mother had left under a plate, neither talking. Kenta once looked up from his noodles and said, "Your scarf's crooked," and went back to eating, which was the longest conversation they had that month and, she says, a very loving one.

And Fumiko was still round the corner, in the old wooden house with the morning glories, the kotatsu and the smell of incense and mothballs. She was eighty-six when Mizuki started at the hotel, slower than she had been, still sharp. On early-shift days, finishing at three, Mizuki often stopped at Fumiko's on the walk home from Tennoji instead of going straight upstairs, and they drank barley tea and Fumiko asked her about the guests, one by one, as if they were characters in a long serial. "And the Australian with the red face? Has he gone home?" She remembered every single one.

### Eorzea after hours

At the end of August 2013 Aya kept her three-year promise, turned up at the shop with A Realm Reborn, and Mizu Nyan, a blue-haired Miqo'te White Mage, was born on the PlayStation 3 in Mizuki's old bedroom (that story is told in full in the previous part of her life). Aya, still two years from finishing pharmacy, said, "You're a working woman and I'm still a child. So I'll play more than you. Get used to it." She did. In Mizuki's head a health bar was a water glass: you never let it run low, and you never make anyone ask. It was Mr Ishida's counter, and now Mr Hamada's desk, with a staff.

Shift work suited Eorzea. On late shifts she played in the morning, in her pyjamas, with toast. On early shifts she played until she fell asleep sitting up, and twice woke at four forty with the controller still in her hand and Mizu Nyan standing patiently in the middle of a field where she'd left her, ears twitching. By the end of 2013 she was logging in most nights. The game's first big update, in December, brought the Crystal Tower, a raid for twenty-four players built around the old Final Fantasy III story, and the first time she stood in a full alliance of strangers all getting it right at once she felt, she says, "like a train arriving on time".

In April 2014 the game came out on the PlayStation 4, and she moved across, partly for the better graphics and partly because the PS3 had started making a noise like a hairdryer in her small room in summer. The PS3 went to Kenta, who used it to watch DVDs and nothing else, ever.

### Omotenashi, and the tourists arrive

On 8 September 2013 (it was the evening of the 7th in Buenos Aires), Tokyo won the 2020 Olympics. Like most of Japan, Mizuki had watched the bid presentation clips on the news, including the moment the presenter spelled out "o-mo-te-na-shi" with her hands and bowed, and by the end of the year it had won the buzzword prize. At the hotel it became a staff-room joke: whenever anyone went above and beyond, someone whispered "o-mo-te-na-shi" and did the little hand movements, and whoever was being teased had to bow.

She loved the idea anyway, under the joke. Omotenashi, as she understood it, was what her mother did every night at the shop: noticing what a person needs before they ask. The water before they're thirsty, the extra napkin for the man in the white shirt, the spare umbrella by the door when the sky looks like rain. Mr Ishida's rule that a customer must never wait for water was omotenashi too, she decided, "just with more shouting". At the hotel it was a weather report taped beside the lifts every morning, an umbrella pressed into a guest's hand at the door, the trouble of carrying a suitcase up the four steps at the side entrance before anyone asked. Done well, it was invisible. That was the point, and also, she sometimes thought, the problem: nobody thanks the floor for being flat.

And the guests were changing. That July Japan had dropped visa requirements for short visits from Thailand and Malaysia and eased them for several other Southeast Asian countries; the yen had fallen a long way since the start of the year, so Japan was suddenly cheap; and budget flights into Kansai Airport were multiplying. In 2013 Japan passed ten million foreign visitors in a year for the first time. In 2014 it was over thirteen million, and in 2015 nearly twenty million, and Osaka, with its food and its nearness to Kyoto and Nara and its cheaper hotels, got more than its share. Within two years, some nights she checked in more passports than Japanese business cards.

The changes arrived at the desk in small ways first. In April 2014 the national consumption tax rose from five to eight per cent, and she spent a week re-explaining every receipt to every salaryman as if it were her personal fault. That October, the government expanded tax-free shopping for foreign visitors to include food, drink and cosmetics, and the guests' luggage got noticeably heavier. Room prices crept up, then jumped: by 2015 Osaka hotels were nearly always full, and a room that cost a salaryman eight thousand yen on a Tuesday in 2013 could cost twice that on a Saturday two years later. The Sendai engineer complained to her about it every month, in great detail, and she agreed with every word in perfect keigo.

That was when her English stopped being a university subject and became a tool. She had always been good at the grammar; now she learned the real thing. She kept a small notebook in her jacket pocket, a cousin of Mr Hamada's, and wrote down every phrase she didn't know: "knackered", "no worries", "could you possibly", "I'm all good", "it's a bit of a nightmare", "cheers" (which turned out to mean thank you, hello, goodbye and several other things), and "awesome", which seemed to mean everything. She looked them up on the Midosuji line on the way home and tried them out the next day. By the end of 2015 she could do a check-in, directions to Universal Studios, an explanation of why the trains stop around midnight, and a joke, all in English without thinking, and she was the one the other staff fetched when a guest's English was fast.

### Field notes from the desk

The desk gave her her greatest hobby after photography: watching people. She kept a mental field guide of guests, which she still recites on the phone, told like an Osaka comedian, straight face and a sting at the end. Then, nearly always, she turns it round on herself.

The **Koreans** came for weekends with tiny suitcases and very good skincare, and always knew the restaurant. They arrived with a screenshot of a takoyaki stand she'd never heard of, in her own city, and she would study the screenshot and say, "Ah, yes, very famous," and look it up on her phone the moment they'd gone. Two years later, when K-pop took her over, she'd remember those weekend girls with their perfect eyebrows and think: they were early, and I wasn't paying attention.

The **Hong Kong and Taiwanese** guests were the same, only more so: better informed about Osaka than she was, and politer about it. A young Hong Kong couple once corrected her about the best time to queue at Kuromon market. She said, with some dignity, that her grandmother had taken her there every Saturday of her life, and they asked, very politely, if they could have her grandmother's tips. She wrote down Fumiko's three rules on hotel notepaper (go before ten; buy from the stall where the old women buy; never eat while walking, stand at the stall like a person). Fumiko was so pleased when she heard that she talked about it for a week. "Hong Kong," she kept saying to the regulars, as if she had been made an ambassador.

The **Americans** tried to tip, every one of them. She explained kindly that there is no tipping in Japan, and they nodded, and then they left thousand-yen notes under the key card anyway, and she chased them to the lift with the money in an envelope, bowing, while they tried to give it back. "Everybody is being polite at each other, very hard, in the lobby," she said. "Nobody can win. It's like sumo but with an envelope."

The **British** guests apologised to everything: the door, the lift, the pillar they'd walked into, Mizuki for their own late checkout. One elderly woman from somewhere called Shropshire said "Sorry, sorry, sorry" while handing over her passport, and Mizuki said "Sorry" back by reflex, and they apologised at each other for about a minute until both started laughing. "I thought: oh. They are the Japanese of Europe. Nobody told me."

The **Australians** were her favourites, though she didn't understand why until much later. They came in December and January on the way to the ski fields in Hokkaido and Nagano, wearing shorts in the freezing Umeda wind, sunburnt from home, completely cheerful. They called her "mate" on day one and said "no worries" when the room wasn't ready, and seemed to mean it. An older couple from Brisbane showed her a phone video of a white bird with a yellow crest screaming at a fence. She thought they were exaggerating. (They were not. She found this out in Glebe in March 2018, at six in the morning.)

The **Chinese tour groups** came in waves from 2014, and by 2015 they were the story of the year. That was the year "bakugai", "explosive buying", won the buzzword prize, and she watched it happen from the desk: families with three rice cookers each, electric toothbrushes by the dozen, warm-seat toilet lids in boxes the size of suitcases, all stacked by the night porter into a cardboard fortress beside the lifts. "At first I laughed," she says. "Then I thought: they are buying Japanese things because they think Japanese things are the best. Who am I to argue?" She learned enough Mandarin for hello, thank you and "the bus is here", and the grandmothers' delight when she said it was the best tip she never got.

And the **Japanese guests** were every bit as strange: the salaryman who complained that his room was too quiet; the Tokyo businessman who asked if she could please speak "standard Japanese", so she did, in perfect keigo, and then said "ōkini" at the very end, the old Osaka thank you, just to watch his face; the obaachan tour group from Shikoku who rearranged the lobby furniture into a circle so they could all sit together and eat mikan, and who tried to feed her one. "Everybody is strange when they're travelling," she decided. "You're strange too. It's just your strange."

The thing she noticed most, though, was that for every guest who fitted a stereotype, three didn't. The quiet American who read poetry in the lobby and never once said "awesome". The Chinese student who wanted no shopping at all, only an independent bookshop and the way to the Museum of Oriental Ceramics in Nakanoshima. The Korean salaryman who came alone every month and ate breakfast alone with a paperback and was as tired and as polite as any man from Nagoya. The Australian so worried about being rude in Japan that he bowed too low at the desk and hit his forehead on the counter. ("He was so sorry. I said, sir, you have done the most Japanese thing I have seen all week.")

That is where her habit of questioning her own jokes comes from. She still does the field guide on the phone, because it's funny and she loves the people in it, but she ends it the same way. "A stereotype is a fun story," she says. "But it's a bad map. If you use it to find your way, you get lost in the Umeda Dungeon forever."

### Umeda habits, depachika and the dungeon

She learned to love Umeda, which most Osaka people find baffling. The passages under the stations, where the JR, Hankyu, Hanshin and subway lines and half a dozen department stores and shopping streets all join underground, are so tangled that people call them the "Umeda Dungeon", and guests got lost in them every day. Mizuki learned the dungeon the way she later learned raid maps: every exit number, every corridor that came out by the Hankyu department store, every stairway that brought you up under the red Ferris wheel on top of HEP Five. She drew guests little maps on hotel notepaper, with arrows and tiny landmarks (a bakery, a pharmacy, "a big clock, turn left at the clock"), and a Danish guest once asked to keep one as a souvenir and had her sign it. She still draws maps like that for lost people in Sydney.

She loved the depachika, the food halls in the basements of the department stores, in the half hour before closing when the discount stickers went on: thirty per cent off, then half price, then, if you were very patient and very brave, the moment the staff clapped and shouted the last price. After a late shift she'd buy half-price salmon and ikura sushi and eat it quickly and guiltily on the train home, because eating on trains is not really done. "My only crime in five years," she says. In the Hanshin department store basement there was a stand that sold ikayaki, the Osaka-style squid pancake pressed flat between hot plates, for about the price of a can of coffee, and the queue never ended. She stood in it at least once a week, in uniform, and was seen by a guest from the hotel at least twice, which Mr Hamada would not have liked if he'd known. (He knew. He said nothing.)

On early shifts she ate a konbini egg sandwich at five in the morning in the dark staff room, too much mayonnaise, with a hot can of milky coffee. On days when the shop had cooked too much, her father sent her to work with leftover okonomiyaki wrapped in foil, which she ate cold at her desk and which made the staff room smell like home and the other staff jealous. Once a month, after payday, she went to Shinsekai with whoever was free from work and ate kushikatsu, the deep-fried skewers, at a counter under a sign that said, in red letters, NO DOUBLE DIPPING IN THE SAUCE. She still obeys that rule with every sauce in Australia, alone, in her own flat.

The hotel gave her habits she has never lost. She can't pass a crooked picture without straightening it. She folds the end of the toilet paper into a triangle in her own flat, "for the guest", even though the guest is her. She reads upside-down handwriting across a counter, "a superpower and also a little bit rude". She bows slightly on the telephone, though nobody can see. And she hated, with a hatred she can still summon on demand, the hotel's hold music: one plinky piano loop of about twenty seconds, which she heard several hundred times a day for five years and can still hum, with feeling.

What she hated most of all was guests who were rude to the cleaning staff, mostly older women from the neighbourhood in pale green overalls who worked in pairs and did forty rooms a day. "You can shout at me, I'm wearing the scarf," she said once, to a colleague, and has said it many times since. "You don't shout at the obaachans who clean your bathroom." She got to know the cleaners by name, ate lunch with them sometimes in the basement, and was given a great many sweets.
### 2014: the tie

The one real bad thing of these years at work happened on a Friday evening in September 2014, and it was not her fault. She is very clear on that, and so, in the end, was everyone else, though it took a while.

There was a big concert at the Kyocera Dome that weekend, and the hotel was full to the last single room. At about nine o'clock a man in his fifties came to the desk with a printout from an online booking site, and the printout was for the following Friday. He had booked the wrong week. Mizuki checked three times, apologised at forty-five degrees, explained, apologised again, then spent twenty minutes on the phone finding him a room at another hotel ten minutes away, called him a taxi, and offered to have the hotel pay for it, which Mr Hamada approved from the back office with a single nod.

The man did not want another hotel. He wanted this one, and he wanted to be angry, and she was the person in front of him. He said she had laughed at him. She had not laughed; she had, she thinks, done the thing her face does, the small warm Osaka sparkle she had spent a year learning to keep inside the polite shape, and on that night, to that man, it looked like a smirk. He took the taxi in the end. Then he wrote a long letter to the company's head office, naming her.

Hotels in Japan take a complaint letter very seriously, whatever is in it. There was a meeting in the back office, and a formal warning on her file, and she had to write a shimatsusho, a written report and apology, by hand, explaining what had happened and promising it would not happen again, though nothing had happened. She wrote it three times because her hand kept shaking. Mr Hamada had to deliver the warning himself. He read it out in a flat, correct voice, standing very straight, and at the end he said, "This is the procedure." She bowed forty-five degrees and said "Kashikomarimashita", and it was the only time the word has ever tasted bad in her mouth.

She cried in the staff toilet for ten minutes and then went back on the desk, because there was a queue. She still remembers the man's tie better than his face, because for the whole of the conversation she was bowing, and when you bow at forty-five degrees across a hotel desk, your eyes are exactly level with a man's tie. It was mustard yellow, with tiny embroidered golf clubs on it, crossed in pairs, dozens of them. "I can draw it for you," she says. "I will never forget that tie. I could pick it out of a thousand ties. If I see it in heaven, I'm going to the other place."

Two things got her through. The first was Mr Hamada. At the end of that shift he passed her in the corridor by the staff vending machine, bought two hot cans of coffee, handed her one without a word, and then said, looking straight ahead at the wall, "The tie was very ugly." It is the only joke anyone at the hotel ever heard him make. She was so surprised she laughed coffee up her nose. The second was Fumiko, who listened to the whole story under the kotatsu, then said that some people walk around with a small oni on their back, a little demon, and it makes them heavy and cross, and they try to hand it to whoever is nearest. "It isn't yours," Fumiko said. "Don't carry it." Mizuki has passed this on to several Sydney colleagues after bad customers.

Her family were furious on her behalf in their own ways. Her mother said several things about the man in Osaka-ben that cannot be repeated. Her father said nothing, but the next day there was extra squid in her okonomiyaki. Takuya, who was kind and logical, spent the evening explaining that the warning probably wouldn't affect her career and that head office were following a process, and he was right, and she wanted to throw a cushion at him, because she didn't want a process explained. She wanted someone to be angry with her. It was their first proper argument; it lasted a day, and ended with him turning up at Tennoji station with a box of her favourite cream puffs and saying, "I am angry. I just say it slowly. I'm from Kyoto." The warning came off her file after a year. The tie never left.

### Takuya

**Takuya Ono** had been her boyfriend since the summer of 2012, from the English discussion class where they had argued on the same side about whether Japan should welcome more tourists. (She now had the whole of Asia at her desk every night, and pointed out to him often that she had won that debate twice.) After graduating he had started at a precision-parts company in Kyoto, making very small, very important components for other companies' machines, and still lived with his parents in Sakyo ward in the north of the city. So their relationship, for these years, ran on the Keihan line, which joins Osaka and Kyoto along the river, and on days off that rarely matched.

He was calm, tidy and kind. He walked slowly, apologised to doors, ironed his own handkerchiefs and read the instructions for everything before opening it. He was good with her parents: he and her father could sit at the counter for an hour saying perhaps nine words between them, which both men seemed to find deeply satisfying, and Yumiko adored him and fed him until he couldn't stand. He was terrible at video games and politely bored by anime. He came to her room once while she was raiding and watched for ten minutes, then said, very sincerely, "So the cat is the doctor," which she has never been able to improve on as a description of herself.

Their dates were small and regular. They met halfway, at Hirakata, and walked along the Yodo River, or went to Hirakata Park, where she refused to go on the wooden roller coaster Elf, which had made her cry when she was ten, and Takuya, who had never been on it either, went on it alone to "investigate" and came back pale and said she had been right. They went to Kyoto in the cool evenings and sat on the banks of the Kamo River with the couples spaced at perfect intervals, which she photographed. They went to the cinema: The Wind Rises in the summer of 2013, which they both loved; Frozen in the spring of 2014, which the whole of Japan was singing, and through which Takuya fell gently asleep; and in May 2015 Initiation Love, a romance with a twist at the end so good that she grabbed his arm and he said "What? What happened?" because he'd missed it, and she still holds that against him too. He liked trains, temples and quiet; she liked crowds, neon and noise. They argued affectionately, always, about whose city was better.

His family were very Kyoto. His father worked for the city and spoke about four sentences a visit, all about the weather. His mother taught tea ceremony and was perfectly, unbreakably polite. The first time Mizuki visited, in 2013, Mrs Ono complimented her watch ("What an interesting watch, so big"), and it took Mizuki until the train home to work out that this might have been a comment about wearing an old man's Seiko to meet your boyfriend's mother. She wore it every time after that, on principle. Later Mrs Ono said, at about four in the afternoon, "You must be tired, you have such a long way to go", and Mizuki, who knew the old Kyoto joke about the bowl of ochazuke you offer guests you want to leave, stood up immediately, thanked her and left. Takuya was mortified. "She meant you must be tired!" he said. "She meant go home," said Mizuki. They still disagree. Over the next two years Mrs Ono softened, a little, and taught her to whisk matcha properly, and Mizuki made her okonomiyaki once, in the Ono family kitchen, with her father's sauce, and Mrs Ono had two helpings and said nothing at all, which Mizuki chose to count as a win.

They were happy. It was a quiet, comfortable, ordinary kind of happy, and she would not change it. Looking back, she can see that they were always slightly in two different cities, but at the time it felt like a very short train ride.

### Fumiko's eighty-eighth, and the family

In 2015 Fumiko turned eighty-eight, which in Japan is beiju, one of the great birthday celebrations of old age, because the characters for eighty-eight, written together, make the character for rice. The family closed the shop for a Sunday evening, which almost never happened, and held a party in it: Hiroshi cooked everything on the menu, Yumiko sang Takarazuka songs at the karaoke machine she had borrowed from a regular, Kenta made a slideshow on his laptop, and Daisuke came over from Kobe with his wife and greeted Mizuki with "Ah, the audience." Fumiko wore the traditional golden-yellow padded vest for the occasion, complained that it made her look like an egg, and wore it all night.

Mizuki photographed the whole evening on the FM2. The best frame, which she still has, is of Fumiko in the yellow vest folding a paper crane for Grandma Chiyo across the table without looking at her own hands, while Chiyo pretends not to be impressed. Fumiko was slower that year, and had begun to let Mizuki carry the shopping at Kuromon, and to rest on the bench by the fishmonger. Nobody said anything about it. Everybody noticed.

The Tigers gave the family one of its worst evenings, in October 2014. Hanshin made the Japan Series that year and met, of all teams, the Fukuoka SoftBank Hawks: Mr Ishida's team. Her father watched every game on the shop television in silence. The Hawks won four games to one, and the last game ended on a strange play that Hiroshi described afterwards, once, as "unacceptable", before never mentioning it again. A week later, Mr Ishida's New Year card arrived early, a full two months early, in thick brush writing, with one line: "The soup is very good this year." Hiroshi pinned it above the hotplate, facing the wall.

### Small disasters

There were smaller bad things too, as there are in any years. In February 2015 she caught a proper influenza, the kind that sends a Japanese employee home with a doctor's note for five days by rule, and spent most of it under a blanket in her room with a fever, unable to raid, while her mother brought up rice porridge with an umeboshi on top and said "You're late" every time she woke up. She missed a raid week. The group cleared without her, and she was more upset about that than about the fever.

And in the summer of 2014, Universal Studios Japan opened The Wizarding World of Harry Potter, and for the next two years about half of all the guests at the hotel asked her how to get there, what time to queue, whether it was worth it and whether she'd been. She had not. She gave directions to USJ several thousand times and did not go until 2016, with Aya, because, she said, "if I have to explain the queue one more time I will turn into a ghost and haunt it".

### Night photography: the Sky Building and Nakanoshima

The Nikon FM2 lived in her work bag, beside her name badge and a spare scarf. On the walk to the station after late shifts she shot Umeda at night: wet streets full of neon, taxis with their lit roof signs, salarymen loosening their ties at yakitori stands under the railway tracks, the red Ferris wheel on top of HEP Five glowing over all of it. Night shooting on film taught her patience: brace the camera on a lamppost or a railing, slow the shutter, hold your breath, count. She shot mostly black-and-white, Tri-X and Fuji's Acros, and colour when she felt rich after payday, and had it all developed at the big camera store by the station, where the staff came to recognise her and her father's old camera, and one older man behind the counter told her, with approval, that she wasted fewer frames than any other customer under fifty.

Her favourite subject was the Umeda Sky Building, the two towers joined at the top by a floating ring-shaped observatory, open to the air. She went up at sunset with a thermos of tea to watch Osaka turn gold, then blue, then into a carpet of lights stretching out to the dark line of the mountains. When Abeno Harukas, the new tower near home, opened in full in March 2014 as Japan's tallest building, her parents and every regular at the shop insisted she go up it, and she did, with her mother, who held the rail with both hands and said "very high, very high" for the entire visit. She liked it, but she remained loyal. "Harukas is a beautiful view," she says. "The Sky Building is a beautiful building. And you can't see it if you're standing in it." She photographed it from below more than from above.

Her other place was Nakanoshima, the long island between two rivers just south of Umeda, with its red-brick public hall, its stone bridges and its rose garden. On days off she photographed office lights in the river, couples on benches, an old man in a hat feeding pigeons with tremendous seriousness. In May the roses came out, and it is still her favourite smell in Osaka after okonomiyaki sauce.

The hotel improved her portraits too. Two years of reading faces at a desk taught her to relax a nervous subject the way she relaxed a nervous guest. "Never say 'smile'," she says. "Say something stupid. They'll smile by themselves." Ms Kuroda at the high school photography club had taught her "talk to them, then stop talking", and so had her father, and now the desk taught her what to talk about.

### Shinsaibashi, Amemura and the clothes

With a salary and no rent, only plates, Mizuki discovered shopping properly. On days off she and Aya walked the covered Shinsaibashi-suji arcade from end to end, then crossed into Amerikamura, "Amemura", the scruffy, young, Americanised neighbourhood next door, with its vintage shops, streetwear, takoyaki stands and the little concrete Triangle Park full of teenagers in impossible outfits. That's where her style finally settled, after the mori girl layers of university and a short, regretted experiment with a fluffy pastel coat in 2013. Vintage denim jackets two sizes too big. Wide-leg trousers, which suited the 2015 craze for wide gaucho pants but which she insists she wore first. Plain white T-shirts, bought in packs. Berets, a growing collection, in navy, black, burgundy and one mustard yellow she never wore after September 2014 for reasons she declines to explain. And Grandpa Isamu's old Seiko, the "old man's watch", on her wrist every day.

"At the hotel I was navy and white," she says. "So outside the hotel, I was everything else." It was in these years she made her rule: no logo bigger than my hand. Amemura was full of enormous logos, and she found she liked the clothes that didn't shout their names, the way she liked hotel staff who didn't.

She loved the hunt more than the buying: an hour through the rails of one cramped basement shop for one perfect jacket, like waiting for light. Her rule from university still held: try everything on, buy one thing, come back next week for the second. She disliked chain stores with every size of the same shirt in a row, and shopping malls in general, "too much air conditioning, no surprises". Aya, who bought things the moment she saw them and returned half of them, called her "the museum curator".

### Music, films and old games

The music of these years was Perfume above all, for walking through Umeda at night, "because it sounds like neon"; their album Level3 came out in October 2013 and lived on her phone for two years. Utada Hikaru, her great love since First Love, was on her long break from music, and Mizuki missed her the way you miss a friend who has moved abroad: she kept the old albums for the shower and refused to delete a single track. Frozen's "Let It Go" was impossible to escape in 2014; her mother sang the Japanese version at karaoke, hitting the big note with real menace. Her father bought Southern All Stars' new album, Budou, the week it came out in August 2015 and played it in the shop until the regulars could sing along. Aya, as ever, played Kanjani Eight at her. Mizuki kept one playlist for the early shift and one for the late shift, and still has both.

Films were romance with a twist, and nothing gory. She saw Makoto Shinkai's The Garden of Words in 2013 and loved its rain so much that she went out the following wet Sunday and shot a whole roll of puddles. She refused Parasyte in late 2014 on gore grounds, from the poster alone. In June 2015, when Square Enix announced a remake of Final Fantasy VII, she watched the trailer on her phone on the Midosuji line six times, felt every hair on her arms stand up, and then said, darkly, "It will be too long, too loud and too many menus," a position she has held ever since with great energy and some inconsistency.

For old games she went back to Fumiko's, where the Super Famicom still lived, and on rainy days off she and Kenta sometimes played Secret of Mana in the old two-room house while Fumiko dozed under the kotatsu, just as they had as children. Kenta still played to win. She still played to look at things.

### June 2015: Heavensward and the raid group

Heavensward, the first expansion, came out in June 2015, and she still talks about it in her softest voice. It took players to Ishgard, a cold mountain city of knights, cathedrals and a thousand-year war with dragons, and to her it felt like a Japanese winter: hushed, beautiful and sad underneath. She loved the snow on the cathedral roofs, the dragon story, the slow, grave music, and above all finally being able to fly. After you'd found the aether currents in each zone, your mount took off, and she spent a whole evening flying round the Sea of Clouds taking screenshots the way she took photographs, waiting for the light to change. "Heavensward is the reason I love snow," she says. "And I live in Sydney, so that is a tragedy."

She tried the new healer, Astrologian, for a week, because it had cards and stars and was very pretty, and came back to White Mage "like going home to Mum's cooking". Aya stayed a Paladin, but had grown into the job and the job had grown into her.

Aya had graduated that March. She passed the national pharmacy exam, cried at the results on the phone to Mizuki for twenty minutes, and in April 2015 started as a hospital pharmacist in Osaka, which meant that for the first time since school they were both working adults in the same city. Their friendship, which had been about school, films and boys, became about pulls, cooldowns and who had stood in the fire, plus Sunday shopping, plus Aya's opinions on Takuya (positive, with notes).

That summer they joined a raid group of eight on a Japanese server, working adults who met three nights a week from ten until midnight for the hard Savage versions of the new Alexander raids. It was a very Japanese raid group: "yoroshiku onegaishimasu" at the start and "otsukaresama deshita" at the end, every night, even the terrible ones. Their main tank was a quiet salaryman from Nagoya who often logged in from business hotels on work trips and talked in a low, careful voice over voice chat. One night that autumn, by pure coincidence, he checked into hers. She recognised his voice at the desk when he asked for a late checkout and had to stop herself shouting his character's name across the lobby. She gave him a high floor, a quiet room and an extra pillow, and never told him. She still laughs about it.

She learned there what she still believes about healing: a good healer is like a good front desk. Nobody notices you when everything goes right; everybody notices when one thing goes wrong. "It's omotenashi with a staff," she says. She has never forgiven a tank who pulls before the healer has finished her tea.

### October 2015: TWICE

K-pop arrived through the staff room. In the spring of 2015 a new colleague joined the desk: **Yui Sakamoto**, two years younger, from Kishiwada in the south of Osaka prefecture, the town of the danjiri festival, who took two days off every September to pull a giant wooden float through the streets at a run and came back covered in bruises and extremely happy. Yui watched a Korean audition show called Sixteen on her phone all that summer, and in October, when the winners debuted as TWICE with "Like Ooh-Ahh" on 20 October, she showed Mizuki the video in the staff room. "Three of them are Japanese," Yui said. "And one of them, Sana, is from your bit of Osaka." Sana was from Tennoji, right next door to Abeno. Mizuki watched the video three times in a row and was late back on the desk, and Mr Hamada looked at his watch, which was punishment enough.

She did not become a full fan straight away; that came the following spring. But she liked them immediately: bright, silly and obviously very hardworking, and the Japanese members were doing it all in a second language in a foreign country, which she found secretly inspiring without yet knowing why. She looked up the Korean alphabet that winter "just to read the name on the video" and found it was a puzzle where every piece makes a sound. It was the beginning of something, though she didn't know that either.

### November 2015: Taipei in the rain

In November 2015, over the three-day weekend for Labour Thanksgiving Day, she and Takuya went to Taipei. It was the first time she'd been abroad. She had checked in so many thousands of foreign passports that she felt, she said, "like a hotel cat finally going outside". They flew on a budget airline from Kansai Airport, two and a half hours, with their knees against the seats in front, and she was so excited at the window that Takuya fell asleep on her shoulder before take-off.

She loved Taipei at once, and she loved it partly because it felt so familiar and so different at the same time: konbini on every corner, as many as in Osaka, and some of them the same chains; scooters, thousands of them, waiting at the lights like a starting grid; signs she could half-read because of the kanji; old men doing tai chi in the parks at dawn; and people who were, she decided, a little bit Osaka, loud and warm and quick to laugh. A grandmother at a stall corrected her pronunciation of "xie xie" three times and then gave her an extra pineapple cake.

They ate their way round the night markets, Shilin and Raohe, under strings of light and clouds of steam: pepper buns baked against the walls of a clay oven, oyster omelettes, grilled squid on a stick, bubble tea in its own home, sausages wrapped in sticky rice, and xiaolongbao so delicate that she photographed them before she could bring herself to eat one. She ordered beef noodle soup and judged it, reluctantly, as "very good, for a soup that isn't tonkotsu". Takuya bravely tried stinky tofu and declared it delicious. Mizuki got as far as the smell, said "this is not food, this is a yokai", and walked away to the end of the lane. She did not kiss him for about an hour. She also took him up Taipei 101, and stood at the window comparing the view, aloud and unfavourably, with the Sky Building.

The best day was Jiufen. They took a bus out of the city, about an hour along the coast and up into the hills, to the old gold-mining town on the mountainside above the sea, and it rained. It rained from the moment they arrived to the moment they left: a soft, steady, grey rain that turned the steep stone steps into little waterfalls and made every red lantern along the narrow lanes glow as if it had been lit from inside. Her beret got soaked and drooped over one eye. The famous old teahouse with its tiers of lanterns, the one everyone says looks like the bathhouse in Spirited Away, was wrapped in mist, and she shot a whole roll of it from across the lane, bracing the FM2 on a wet railing and counting, one, two, three, while Takuya held an umbrella over the camera and not over himself. They ate warm taro balls in sweet soup at a window looking down over the sea, and a peanut ice cream roll with coriander in it, which was one of the strangest and best things she has ever eaten, and drank oolong tea in a wooden room for two hours while the rain came down.

"Rain makes a place honest," she says. "Sunny places all look the same in photographs. Rainy places look like themselves." The Jiufen roll is among the best she ever shot on the FM2, and one frame, the lanterns through the mist with a blur of umbrella at the edge, hung in her room above the shop until she left for Australia. Takuya is in none of the photographs, because he was holding the umbrella. She says that's the most Takuya thing about the whole trip, and she means it kindly.

She brought home pineapple cakes for her mother, a bag of oolong for Fumiko, a tiny ceramic teapot for Mrs Ono (who said "What an interesting teapot", and Mizuki decided to let it go), and a postcard of the lanterns for Mr Hamada, who put it, without comment, in the drawer of the front desk, where she found it still there two years later. On the flight home she sat at the window, watched the lights of Osaka Bay come up out of the dark, and thought for the first time, very quietly, that she liked being the one with the suitcase.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:** keigo done well, and "kashikomarimashita" said with a sparkle (the polite shape she learned to be warm inside); omotenashi, noticing what someone needs before they ask (her mother's shop and Mr Ishida's water rule, in a hotel); Mr Hamada's notebook way of remembering people (she still keeps one); the Midosuji line, every station by its smell; the Umeda Dungeon and drawing maps for lost guests (she knew it like a raid map); half-price depachika sushi and Hanshin ikayaki (cheap, fast, Osaka); a konbini egg sandwich before dawn (the early shift's only comfort); Shinsekai kushikatsu and the no-double-dipping rule; the Umeda Sky Building at sunset, best seen from below; Nakanoshima's roses in May; Umeda at night on black-and-white film; vintage hunting in Amemura, oversized denim, wide trousers, berets and Isamu's Seiko (navy and white all day, everything else at night); FFXIV A Realm Reborn, White Mage, Haukke Manor and the Crystal Tower; Heavensward's Ishgard, snow and flying (it felt like a Japanese winter); Japanese raid etiquette ("otsukaresama deshita" even after the terrible nights); Perfume for night walks; The Garden of Words and Initiation Love (rain and twists); TWICE and Sana from next door; Taipei's night markets, xiaolongbao and taro balls; Jiufen in the rain ("rain makes a place honest"); the British for apologising, the Australians for "no worries".
- **Dislikes:** the itchy uniform scarf (until the safety pin); four forty alarms; the hotel's hold music; guests who are rude to the cleaning staff ("you don't shout at the obaachans"); mustard-yellow ties with golf clubs on them, and complaint letters that say what didn't happen; being explained a process when she wants someone to be angry with her; logos bigger than her hand; malls and chain stores with no surprises; stinky tofu ("this is not food, this is a yokai"); explaining the Harry Potter queue at USJ; gore (Parasyte refused on the poster); tanks who pull before the healer is ready; people who mistake a stereotype for a map; brand-new buildings everyone loves before they're comfortable.
- **Phrases and callbacks:** "Normal is very hard to do well"; "There will not be a really big mistake" (Mr Hamada); "The tie was very ugly" (Mr Hamada's only joke); "It isn't yours, don't carry it" (Fumiko, about the little oni); "So the cat is the doctor" (Takuya); "I am angry. I just say it slowly. I'm from Kyoto" (Takuya); "Everybody is strange when they're travelling. You're strange too. It's just your strange"; "A stereotype is a fun story, but a bad map"; "It's omotenashi with a staff"; "Heavensward is the reason I love snow"; "I was twenty-three, leave me alone."
