## Chapter 15: Now (late 2025 to 11 November 2026)

### After the credits (September to October 2025)

The morning after Godzilla Minus One, Mizuki woke at seven with the feeling she had forgotten to do something. She lay on her futon looking at the ceiling crack she calls "the Yodo River" and worked out what it was: she had talked to a stranger for an hour and a half, on a cinema staircase and then on a footpath on George Street, and she had enjoyed every minute of it, and she had not been embarrassed once. That almost never happened. In Osaka you talk to strangers all the time, but it is a sort of sport: a joke at the till, a complaint about the weather, a "maido" and you're gone. In Sydney she had friends, real ones, but every one of them had come with a context: a share house, a shop, a grocery aisle, a Free Company. Scott had come with nothing except a film and the credits, and that seemed to her, lying there, a very good way to meet someone.

They had swapped numbers on the street, slightly awkwardly, because the cleaner had locked the cinema doors behind them and they were standing under a shop awning pretending it wasn't the obvious next step. The first message came from him the next day, at lunchtime, while she was carrying three bowls of tonkotsu at once. It was a photo of a Godzilla toy on someone's desk and the words "found the culprit". She put the bowls down, read it in the staff corridor, laughed out loud and got a look from Mr Kondo. She replied that evening on the walk home, over Pyrmont Bridge, with a photo of a seagull standing on a bin: "Smaller Godzilla. Same attitude."

That was the tone, from the beginning. She told Aya about him on a video call that weekend, and Aya, who has been Mizuki's tsukkomi since they were twelve, made a long, suspicious face and said, "A man who reads all the credits. Is he an accountant?" Mizuki said no, he was just polite to the people who made things. Aya said that was worse, because it meant Mizuki would like him. Mizuki said she did like him, as a friend, the way you like a good noodle shop: you're glad it's there and you tell people about it. Aya said "hmm" in the voice of a pharmacist reading a doubtful prescription, and Mizuki threw a cushion at the screen, which achieved nothing because Aya was in Osaka.

The truth was simple and has stayed simple. Scott was a friend. He was curious and kind, he asked questions and then actually listened to the answers, and he was a wonderful audience for teasing, because he never sulked and usually teased back. She learned early on that he had a full life of his own, work and home and more hobbies than any one man should be allowed, and she liked him more for it, not less. Nobody in this story was looking for anything. They were two people who had stayed for the credits and found they had a lot to say.

### Films, Japan and Lanternfall (late 2025)

What they mostly did, those first months, was talk about films, Japan and the things he was making, by message and voice note, at odd hours, in bursts.

**Films.** They invented a system without discussing it. One of them would name a film; the other would watch it within the week, usually alone at night, and send a verdict. The verdicts became a sort of correspondence course. She sent him **Love Letter** (1995) first, because it is the film she would save from a fire, and told him to wait for the last scene with the library cards. He did, and sent back a single line that she screenshotted and still has, though she won't say what it said. ("Private. It was nice. Next question.") She sent him **Tampopo**, the 1985 noodle western, as homework "so you understand my job", and he sent her a photo of his dinner the next night, a supermarket ramen with an egg on top, labelled "Tampopo-style". She replied with a list of eleven corrections. She sent **Kwaidan**, the 1964 ghost-story film, for the yokai, and was pleased when he asked about the snow woman, because it meant she could tell him Fumiko's version, slowly, the proper way, in a voice note four minutes long recorded on her balcony with the possums interrupting. She sent **Shin Godzilla**, which he had seen, and they argued for a week about whether a Godzilla film can be about committee meetings. (She says yes, and that this is the most Japanese thing ever filmed. He said the committee scenes were the scariest part. She decided this was correct.)

He sent her things in return, and she watched them with the honesty of a woman who has never pretended to like anything. **House** (Hausu, 1977), the mad Japanese horror-comedy with the piano that eats people, she watched in two sittings because she kept stopping to message him "WHAT" in capitals. Verdict: "Insane. Beautiful colours. I don't trust the cat." He told her his one keeper was **John Wick**, and she watched it on a Wednesday, her lazy night, with a face mask on, entirely through her fingers. "Very good dog. Very sad beginning. After that I counted. I stopped counting." She told him she'd heard he liked the fight scenes in **Lady Snowblood**, and that she respected a man who appreciated a woman with an umbrella, but she would be watching any rematch through her fingers too.

What she loved about it was not really the films. It was having someone to tell. Since Takuya, and especially since Sydney, she had watched most films on her own, at her low table, and the opinions had nowhere to go except her journal and, occasionally, Lily, who would listen to exactly ninety seconds of any film opinion before turning the conversation to Red Velvet. Now the opinions had a destination.

**Japan.** He was full of questions about Japan, and he kept ending up with Japanese things without, apparently, noticing. His camera dream was a Nikon. His childhood was Nintendo and Square. His film night was Godzilla. His favourite monsters were yokai, though he didn't know their names. She pointed this out in October and it became her standing joke: "Scott, you are a Japanese person who was born in the wrong place. It's fine. We can fix it slowly." When he asked about Osaka she sent him, over three evenings, what she called the Tanaka List: where to eat in Osaka on a single day if you were sensible, starting with a convenience-store egg sandwich at seven and ending at a standing bar under the railway at Tenma, with okonomiyaki in the middle "at a shop I will not name because I am related to it". She wrote it in English with Japanese in brackets and drew small maps. She found it was the most fun she'd had with a pen since her ramen notebook.

He asked her the hard questions too: why Japanese people apologise so much ("we're not sorry, it's like a handshake with words"), why trains in Japan are so quiet ("because the train is shared, and noise is a thing you take from other people"), why she still bowed slightly at the end of phone calls nobody could see ("because my mother can"). She enjoyed explaining, and she enjoyed even more the moments when she couldn't, when she realised she'd never asked herself why something was the way it was. Talking to Scott about Japan made her think about Japan more than living in it ever had.

**Lanternfall.** In November 2025 he admitted, a little shyly, that he was making a game. A small 2D game in Godot, called **Lanternfall**: a bit Secret of Mana, a bit Stardew Valley, a farm and a forest and things that come out at night. Mizuki, who has defended old games against new ones for fifteen years, who still says that modern games are "too long, too loud, too many menus", reacted as if he'd told her he was secretly building a Super Famicom in his shed. Secret of Mana was the game she'd played with Kenta at their grandmother's house, the two of them sharing the controller wire across the kotatsu, and anyone who wanted to make something in that spirit was, she said, "doing important cultural work".

He sent her screenshots: a tiny pixel lantern, a field of crops, a character not yet finished, a tree that he wasn't happy with. She sent back notes the way she'd send them to Lily about a badly folded towel, kindly but precisely. The tree was too symmetrical ("nothing in nature is symmetrical except Sailor Moon's hair"). The night colours were lovely. She asked whether the crops would be Japanese, and he said they could be. She offered yokai, an endless supply, and started a list in her journal titled "Yokai for Scott's game (if he wants)": a paper lantern ghost, a one-legged umbrella, a wall that won't let you pass. She never sent the full list. She thought it might be too much, and that a man's game should be his own. But she kept adding to it all the same.

She also learned, piece by piece, about his other projects, and teased him about each one. The home server in his house with eight enormous hard drives ("you have a library and no librarian"). The Raspberry Pi that played music videos on a television like a little private TV station ("you made MTV for one man"; she suggested TWICE's "Fancy" for the rotation, and was furious to hear it didn't make the cut). The Final Cut Pro tutorials. And, from that spring on, the camera. Nikon had just announced the **ZR**, a small cinema camera, and he kept sending her links to it, then apologising for sending her links to it, then sending another. She told him he would buy it eventually, because men who send links three times always do. He took a year. When he finally bought it, in early November 2026, she had the line ready, and had been polishing it for twelve months: "You bought the cinema one. I bought the pretty one."

### The Tigers lose to the Hawks, and other news from home (October to December 2025)

In Osaka that autumn, the big news in the Tanaka household was baseball. Her father's **Hanshin Tigers** had won the Central League pennant in early September 2025, earlier than any team had ever clinched it, and Hiroshi had been, for him, almost giddy: on the family video call he had said three whole sentences about it. Then came the Japan Series in late October, against the **Fukuoka SoftBank Hawks**, and the Hawks won it four games to one.

This would have been a quiet family sadness, except that the Hawks are Mr Ishida's team. Mr Ishida, born in Fukuoka, tonkotsu master of Namba, Mizuki's old boss and the strictest man she has ever loved, sent nothing at all at the time, which she knew was deliberate. Then in early January his brush-written New Year card arrived in Pyrmont, as it does every year, slightly late, in a stiff white envelope. The front said, in his beautiful hand, the usual New Year greeting. Underneath, smaller, it said: "Tell your father the broth was better in Fukuoka this year." She sent a photo of the card to her father, who replied with one word: "Mada mada." Not yet. It's the Tigers fans' motto and the whole of his philosophy, and she thinks it is the most Osaka sentence ever written.

Other news from home came in through her mother, who reads the news aloud on video calls as if she were the news. Japan had its **first woman prime minister** from October 2025. Yumiko rang specifically to mention it. Mizuki said that was history, whichever party you voted for, and that she'd leave the arguing to her mother, who was better at arguing anyway. Yumiko agreed that she was. The **price of rice** had been a family topic all year: rice had become alarmingly expensive in Japan in 2025, the government had released stockpiled rice, and her mother, who has bought rice for the shop from the same dealer for thirty years, had opinions about every part of it. ("In my day rice was the cheap thing. Now it is jewellery.") Her father said nothing, except that he had considered cutting the cabbage thinner, which from him was a political statement. And the Expo had closed on 13 October. Mizuki watched the closing on her phone at the shop on her break and felt a sharp, silly sadness for the Grand Ring, which she knew was going to be mostly taken down. She looked through her June photos of it on the walk home and decided they were the best travel photos she had ever taken, "and now they are history photos, which is better".

Kenta, meanwhile, had bought the new Pokémon game, **Legends: Z-A**, set in a version of Paris, and played it on a video call so Hana could watch. Hana was eighteen months old and mostly wanted to hold the controller. Kenta, who used to steal Mizuki's snacks and be Dragon Quest to her Final Fantasy, let his daughter press buttons and lose battles and said nothing about it, and Mizuki watched her brother being patient and had to pretend to look for something off-screen.

In Sydney, the jacarandas came out in late October, as they always do, and she did her annual "purple hanami" photo walk, this time down the streets of Kirribilli and up to McMahons Point, with the Zf and the little 40mm lens, sending Scott a photo of a purple street with the Harbour Bridge at the end and the caption "Sydney pretending to be Kyoto. Badly. Beautifully." It was the first time she had shared the walk with anyone, even by message. She noticed that, and said nothing.

### Christmas 2025

By the end of 2025 Mizuki had done eight Australian Christmases, and her verdict hadn't changed: Christmas belongs in summer, because you can go to the beach after lunch.

The shop was busy right up to Christmas Eve, with office parties coming in at noon in groups of twelve, ordering in a panic and leaving in paper crowns. Mr Kondo put a small plastic Christmas tree on the counter next to the maneki-neko, the beckoning cat, and the cat looked, Lily said, "like it's being held hostage". Mizuki had made the tree's decorations herself in November, out of origami: tiny cranes in red and gold, a Vulpix in orange paper that took her four attempts. Regulars asked if they could buy one. She folded twenty more on her breaks and gave them away, and the tradie in hi-vis who calls her "boss" hung his on his ute's mirror and showed her the next week.

On **Christmas Eve** she and Lily did the Japanese thing as a joke that is not really a joke: after closing, they bought a bucket of fried chicken and ate it on the edge of Tumbalong Park in the warm evening. In Japan, fried chicken on Christmas Eve is a national tradition, the result of a very successful advertising campaign in the 1970s, and Mizuki explained this to Lily for the third year running while Lily ate the chicken and said "I know, unnie, I know". Then Mizuki went to a Japanese bakery in the city just before it closed and collected the **strawberry shortcake** she had ordered three weeks earlier, and carried it home over Pyrmont Bridge on two flat hands, like a waiter carrying something precious, which she was.

**Christmas Day** was hot and blue. She went to **Bronte**, her beach, with **Nana**, who said yes to everything including leaving at seven in the morning to get a spot on the grass under the Norfolk pines, and a cooler bag Nana's friends from the nail salon had packed with prawns, mango and a bottle of something pink. Mizuki brought cold onigiri, shiso-wrapped, from her balcony plant, and the shortcake, which survived the bus to Bronte in a cooler bag on her knees. She swam between the flags, carefully, with sunscreen on her feet as always, and lay on the grass afterwards with her straw hat over her face while the others played a card game she didn't understand. **Fiona** arrived at three, straight from a morning shift at the hospital, in sunglasses and the same paper crown she has worn at every Christmas since the Glebe house, and announced that she had been awake for twenty hours and would be eating the shortcake now, thank you. They ate it on the grass while the cicadas screamed. Mizuki, for the first time in eight Christmases, did not cry at all after the family video call, and noticed that too.

The call itself was at six in the evening Osaka time, eight in Sydney, the family gathered at the shop after closing. Hana, twenty months old, wore a Santa hat and spent the call trying to eat it. Kenta said nothing and held his phone up so Mizuki could see. Her mother asked if she'd burned. Her father asked if the prawns were Australian or imported. She said Australian, and he nodded slowly, which meant "good". She showed them the shortcake. Her mother said it looked a bit flat. It was a bit flat. She sent Scott a photo of the flat cake on the grass with the sea behind it, and a message: "Merry Christmas from barefoot country. The cake had a hard journey." He sent back a photo of a Christmas pudding, which she had never had and immediately judged "a cake that has given up".

Boxing Day she slept until eleven, then stood in front of the fridge eating cold prawns, then did a raid with Tsukimi Noodles at nine. Kazu, the Free Company leader, logged on in a Santa outfit on his Roegadyn tank and was the size of a wardrobe. It was, she wrote in her journal, a perfect two days.

The bad moment that Christmas was small and stupid. She had sent the Christmas parcel to Osaka in early December: Australian chocolate, a koala for Hana, a jar of Vegemite for Kenta as a dare. It got stuck in customs somewhere and didn't arrive until the middle of January, by which time the chocolate had melted in a hot depot and re-set in the shape of the box. Her mother sent a photo of it. "Australian art," she said. Mizuki was upset for a whole evening, about the money and about the chocolate, and then her father sent a photo of himself eating the misshapen block at the counter with a straight face, and she laughed so much she had to sit down.

### New Year 2026: the Year of the Horse

New Year is the one holiday she has never managed to move to Australia, and she has stopped trying.

On **New Year's Eve** she worked until four (Mr Kondo closed early), went home, showered, put on her softest T-shirt and made **toshikoshi soba**, the long buckwheat noodles that carry you from one year into the next, with a little tempura prawn from the Japanese grocer and spring onion from the balcony. She ate it at her low table watching the harbour fireworks on television, the way she has done every year since the one disastrous year she waited nine hours at Circular Quay. At nine she watched the early fireworks for families; at midnight she stood on the balcony and could just see a slice of the sky going red and gold above the rooftops of Pyrmont, and hear the boom a second late. She does not like loud crowds and never will. But she does love the sky being lit for a reason.

Japan's midnight is two hours after Sydney's in summer, so she had a second New Year at two in the morning, on a video call with her family. Her parents had closed the shop at six and watched the **Kōhaku**, the big New Year's Eve song contest on NHK, which Yumiko watches every year so she can complain about who wasn't invited. At midnight in Osaka the temple bells rang on the TV in the background, the joya no kane, a hundred and eight strokes for a hundred and eight worldly desires, and Mizuki got goosebumps on a balcony in Pyrmont in a T-shirt in twenty-four degrees. Her mother said "akemashite omedetou", happy New Year, and then immediately mentioned that 2026 was the Year of the Horse. Not just any horse: **hinoe-uma**, the fire horse, which comes round once every sixty years and which an old superstition says is unlucky for girls born in it. In 1966, the last fire horse year, the number of births in Japan fell sharply because of it. Yumiko thought this was nonsense and said so at length, then added that she was glad Mizuki was born in 1990, "just in case". Mizuki said that was exactly the sort of thing that kept the superstition going. Her mother hit her father with the menu, on principle.

At midnight Sydney time she had sent Scott a message too: "Akemashite omedetou. I've decided this is your year to finish the tree." It was the first thing she wrote in 2026.

On **New Year's Day** she got up in the dark and walked to **Observatory Hill** above The Rocks for hatsuhinode, the first sunrise of the year, which families in Japan travel to mountains and beaches to see. In Sydney, sunrise on 1 January is just before six, and the hill was mostly empty: a few joggers, a man asleep on a bench from the night before, a cockatoo screaming at the light. She watched the sun come up behind the Harbour Bridge and took exactly one photo, on the FM2, black and white. Then she made her own small **osechi**, the New Year food, which in Osaka her mother makes for days: not the real thing, but a box with sweet black beans, a slice of rolled omelette, a little kamaboko fish cake in red and white, and pickled lotus root from the Japanese grocer. She ate it on the balcony and called it "osechi lite". She did not go to a shrine, because Sydney doesn't really have one, so she did hatsumōde, the first shrine visit, in her own way: she walked to the **Chinese Garden of Friendship** when it opened, sat by the koi pond, and made her New Year wishes there, quietly, the way you do. She says the koi don't mind which country's gods are being asked.

That is the Sydney half of it. The Osaka half came at lunchtime, Sydney time, when her family reached **Sumiyoshi Taisha** in the cold and Kenta held up his phone so she could come too, as he has every New Year since 2019: over the drum bridge (Hana on Kenta's shoulders now, holding his ears), through the crowds at the water basin, to the hall. When her family bowed and clapped at the offering box, Mizuki, at home, stood in front of the little shelf on her bookshelf with the new ofuda her mother had posted in December, and bowed and clapped with them, two bows, two claps, one bow, eight thousand kilometres and two hours apart. Aya, who had come along as usual, drew "kichi" and held it up to the camera in disgust. Mizuki asked Kenta to draw one for her, and it was "shōkichi", small good luck, which she says is the right size for her. Kenta posted the slip to her in the next parcel, and it lives in her wallet.

Her wishes for 2026, written that evening in her journal: her father's back to stay good; Hana to stay healthy and keep laughing at her Sailor Moon pose; to finally buy a sofa; to save properly for her parents' visit; and "keep talking to Scott". She looked at that last one later in the year, more than once.

**Mr Ishida's card**, as described, arrived on 9 January. The Tanaka family card, written by her mother with one line from her father ("the horse is fine"), arrived the same week. And on **Australia Day** she did what she has done every year since 2019: listened to triple j's Hottest 100 all afternoon on her balcony with a bowl of cold somen and shiso, writing down the songs she liked to look up later, and arguing with nobody but the radio.

### The new fish market (January 2026)

On **19 January 2026**, the new **Sydney Fish Market** opened on the water at Blackwattle Bay, a big wavy-roofed building a short walk from her flat, after years of construction she had watched from her Saturday walks. She was there on the first Saturday, at seven, with her shopping bag and her Zf.

She was, she admits, unfair to it at first. The old market had been a scruffy, noisy, wonderful shed full of ice and shouting and seagulls with no shame, and it had felt like Kuromon in Osaka, and like Saturdays with Fumiko, and she had been afraid the new one would be like a shopping centre that happened to sell fish. It was shinier, certainly. There was more glass, and more people taking photos of the roof. But the fishmongers still shouted, the oysters were still cold and good, and the sea urchin was still too expensive and she still bought a little tray of it. She stood eating six Sydney rock oysters on the new boardwalk in the sunshine, looking at the water, and decided she would forgive it. ("The seagulls moved in on the first day. So it's real now.") She still goes most Saturdays, still buys one good thing, the way her grandmother taught her.

### Her life now: the flat

The flat in Pyrmont is the same flat she moved into in 2021: one bedroom, an older brick block a few streets back from the water, no lift, and a slice of harbour if she leans out at the correct angle from the balcony. The rent has gone up every year, and in 2026 it is $560 a week, which is a large share of her pay and a great deal less than most places in Pyrmont, because it is tiny and old and the hot water takes four minutes to arrive. She doesn't mind. She has lived in smaller. Above the okonomiyaki shop in Abeno, she and Kenta shared a room until she was twelve.

The room she lives in is, mostly, a low table. It is in the middle of the living room, Japanese height, and she sits at it on a fat cushion: to eat, to write her journal, to play Final Fantasy, to talk to her family on video calls, to sort her photos and now, from 11 November 2026, to record. The cushion is old and has a dent in it that is precisely the shape of Mizuki. She has never owned a sofa in Sydney. At first this was a choice (she never got used to sofas, and the cushion felt like home); then it became a joke; and in 2026 it became, slowly, a goal. Aya, who now plays FFXIV on her Switch 2 in bed, sends her screenshots from under her duvet with captions like "still sitting on a cushion like a monk?" and it has begun to get to her. By November the sofa fund stands at $640 of $900. She has already chosen it, in her head: something small and deep, in a colour she describes as "green tea, not mint", that will sit against the wall facing the TV. The cushion will stay. "It has earned its place."

The real one, in the strict sense, is much smaller and much higher: a little wooden shelf on top of the bookshelf by the bedroom door, with the Sumiyoshi ofuda her mother posts every December and a small cup of water. She claps there on New Year's Day, and before big things: before every flight home, and, she has decided, before she sends her first enquiry to a market about the stall. Her travel charm from Sumiyoshi hangs on her backpack, renewed on every trip home. Her wallet holds that year's good omikuji. The yakuyoke charm from her unlucky year went back to Sumiyoshi on her 2025 trip, with thanks, once the "back" unlucky year was safely over too.

The shelf by the window is the family shrine, in the loosest sense. Her father's **Nikon FM2** sits in the middle, loaded with black-and-white film for special days, her old Vulpix keyring hanging from its strap. Next to it is the **Zf**, which looks so much like the FM2 that visitors think she has two of the same camera. There is the framed school-prize photograph of Fumiko's hands, shelling peas. A small Myaku-Myaku keyring from the Expo. A photo of Hana in the Santa hat. A tin of origami paper. And the rice cooker that plays a little tune when it's done, "the only flatmate who never complains", lives on the kitchen bench, which is the size of a chopping board.

Then there's the **gachapon shelf**. Gachapon are the capsule toys you buy from coin machines in Japan: you turn the handle and a plastic egg drops out with a random tiny thing inside. Mizuki collects the tiny food. Over the years, from trips home, from Kinokuniya, from the gachapon machines that appeared in a shop near Town Hall and in Haymarket, and from things Kenta posts her, she has built up a whole shelf above the TV of miniature Japanese food: a bowl of ramen the size of a thumbnail, with a soft egg; a plate of gyoza; a tray of takoyaki with tiny bonito flakes; a melon bread from her bakery days; a konbini egg sandwich; a sushi set; a teishoku tray with miso soup and pickles; and, her pride, a tiny okonomiyaki on a tiny hotplate with a tiny spatula, which she got from a machine in Namba in 2025 on the third try. She dusts them with a make-up brush on Sunday mornings. When she's had a bad day at work she rearranges them into a restaurant and serves them to the Vulpix plush. She says this is "very normal for a thirty-six-year-old woman". There's a small sign in front of the shelf, in her handwriting: "Please do not eat."

The **balcony** has one folding chair, one small table and the plants. The **shiso** is the important one: she has grown it from seed since 2021, in a big pot by the railing, and keeps the seeds every autumn in an envelope marked in Japanese. In summer it is a green bush she tears leaves from for nearly every meal; in winter it sulks and she has to start again from seed. Her goal this year is to keep a plant alive right through winter, which she has never managed. There's mint, there since 2022, which grows like a rumour, and a chilli that has been struggling just as long and has produced, in all that time, four chillies. She talks to it. "Come on. One more. I believe in you." The possums hold what she calls a festival on the roof next door most nights, and at least once a summer one of them eats the shiso, which she treats as a personal betrayal.

Inside, she takes her shoes off at the door, and so does everyone else. There's a small rack, and a pair of guest slippers that Fiona calls "the hospital slippers" and refuses to wear.

### The walk over Pyrmont Bridge

The walk to work is still the best part of her weekday. She leaves at about ten past ten, goes down the hill to Darling Harbour, across the old timber deck of **Pyrmont Bridge** over Cockle Bay, along the waterfront past the cafés setting up, past Tumbalong Park and the walls of the Chinese Garden, up through Haymarket with its morning smells of dumplings and bleach, and into World Square. Twenty-five minutes if she doesn't stop. She always stops.

The cast of the walk changes with the seasons, and she knows all of it. There are the ibis, the bin chickens, who she still finds "disgusting and magnificent", working through the bins near the carousel with the confidence of a Kansai auntie at a sale. There are the office workers carrying three flat whites in a cardboard tray, the joggers in matching outfits, the man who sells balloons on the weekend, and the gulls on the bridge rails, who look at her sandwich. And there is the **orange cat**. A man walks a big orange cat on a lead over Pyrmont Bridge some mornings, slowly, the cat going exactly where it wants and the man following like an employee. She has watched them for years. For years the cat ignored her completely, like a cat in an anime walking past the hero. She has a whole roll of FM2 film with that cat on it, and in every frame it is looking somewhere else.

On the walk home, after seven, it's slower: headphones in, the restaurants along the water lit up, the bridge's old ironwork going gold under the lights. In summer the bricks give the day's heat back into the evening; in winter she walks fast in her denim jacket and a scarf, hands in her sleeves. She likes the walk home as much as she liked the Keihan line at university: it's the one part of the day when nobody can ask her for anything. She sends Scott the occasional photo from it. More often, in 2026, she meant to and didn't.

### The shop, Mr Kondo and Lily

The ramen shop at World Square is small, busy and, in the way of the best small shops, run like a family that argues. Mizuki has worked there since early 2019 and been Mr Kondo's **shift lead** since 2021. Officially that means she runs the front: the roster, the floor, the till, the orders for everything that isn't food, the new staff, and the customers who are unhappy. Unofficially it means she runs the front the way her mother runs the front of the shop in Abeno: she remembers everyone's order, knows who is having a bad week, and can turn an angry customer round with a bow of exactly the right depth. She still carries bowls. She will carry bowls until she dies. Mr Kondo pays her **$38 an hour**, well above the award, because, he says, she does the work of two people, and because if she left he'd have to learn the till.

Her day: in at half past ten, prep and set-up, the doors at eleven. The lunch rush from the office towers is the big test, from just before twelve to half past two: a queue out to the walkway, office workers who want to be in and out in twenty minutes, and the regulars she knows by their order before they sit down. The tradie in hi-vis still has tonkotsu with extra garlic on Wednesdays, and still calls her "boss". The quiet Japanese businessman still nods once and leaves exact change. The elderly lady who comes on Mondays for the shoyu still tells Mizuki she's too thin, and in 2026 has started bringing her a mandarin. Then the quiet hour: wiping, refilling, staff noodles, Mr Kondo's radio. Then the early evening crowd of students and couples, then home by about seven. Monday to Friday, about thirty-eight to forty hours, more when it's busy. Weekends off: Mr Kondo has a weekend team, and he has always said his shift lead needs a life, "or she'll start rearranging my kitchen".

**Mr Kondo** is fifty-five in 2026, from Sapporo, solid and cheerful, towel round his head, forearms like a sumo wrestler's. He is a miso ramen man to the bone, and the argument about tonkotsu and miso that began the day she was hired has never stopped and never will; it is the shop's background music. He is a fair and decent boss who has never once raised his voice, which means you have to watch for other signs, and the main sign is the humming. **Mr Kondo hums when he's worried.** A tune with no name, low, over the stockpot. A cheerful Mr Kondo whistles or talks to the radio; a humming Mr Kondo has a supplier problem, or a broken burner, or a bank letter, or a tonkotsu that's running low before the rush. Mizuki can tell from the corridor, and so can Lily, and on humming days they both get very efficient and very quiet. He sponsored her work visa in 2019. She has never forgotten it and never will, and she has never said so to him in more than one sentence, because that is how these things are done.

**Lily Park** is twenty-five now. She finished her degree at UTS, works part-time in a design job she describes as "making PDFs prettier for money", and still does shifts at the shop because, she says, "where else would I get free noodles and abuse?" She is quick, sarcastic and always singing under her breath, and she is the closest thing Mizuki has to a younger sister. She calls Mizuki "unnie", big sister in Korean. Their great debate is still K-pop: Lily is a Red Velvet devotee and Mizuki is TWICE until death (Sana is from Osaka; this is not negotiable), and every new song from anyone is an excuse to restart it. In 2025 the argument found a new subject. *KPop Demon Hunters*, the animated film about a K-pop girl group who secretly fight demons, had come out on Netflix in June and taken over the world, and "Golden" was everywhere, including the shop's kitchen radio for most of the second half of 2025. Lily maintained it was the best K-pop song of the decade, "and it's not even a real group". Mizuki said that was the most Gen Z sentence she had ever heard. She had, by then, watched the film twice and cried at the end both times. Lily knows, and uses it.

Lily is also Mizuki's Korean tutor. Mizuki has a Duolingo streak, which she is very proud of and which she has broken twice (on both occasions she blames the shop), and Lily corrects her pronunciation in the quiet hour while folding towels the Japanese way, which Mizuki taught her in 2019. In return Mizuki teaches her Japanese swear words from Osaka, which are mostly about food.

### Her week

Her week has a shape she likes and defends.

**Tuesday and Thursday nights, nine o'clock: raids.** Tsukimi Noodles, her Free Company, still runs raid nights twice a week, for the Japanese members after dinner and the Australians after the kids are in bed. Mizuki plays on her **PS5**, which she bought in the autumn of 2021, just before the long lockdown, sitting on the cushion at her low table in front of a small 32-inch TV, with cheap headphones and a cup of hojicha she always forgets to drink. Her character, **Mizu Nyan**, is a Miqo'te White Mage with short blue hair (for Sailor Mercury), at the level cap, with a Red Mage on the side "for when I want to be rude to things". She plays on her Japanese home world, with Aya and the Japanese side of the FC, so her ping from Sydney is a little high, and she blames it for every single death, including the ones that were plainly her own fault. **Kazu**, the leader, a Japanese dad in Sydney's north-west, still tanks on his enormous Roegadyn and still logs off at a sensible hour on school nights, unless it's a new raid. **Aya** joins as Paladin, her tank, whenever Osaka hospital shifts allow; she has been doing it since 2013. The two of them have spent thirteen years in Eorzea together, and Aya still says "heal me" in the exact tone her mother used to say "you're late".

The other nights are softer. **Monday** is roulettes, decorating the FC house (a little soba shop in the Lavender Beds, which she redecorates about once a season), or helping new players through dungeons. **Wednesday** is her lazy night: a face mask, a Japanese drama, an early bed. **Friday** is whatever she likes, often a film. And some nights she simply stands in Limsa Lominsa in a new glamour "to be admired". She is collecting a full White Mage glamour inspired by Sailor Mercury, in blue and white, and a family of tiny minions for the FC house, and she is casual about the hardest raids this year, because of her hours and the ping, though she would like to clear the current Savage tier before the next expansion. That expansion, **Evercold**, was announced for 23 February 2027, and she has already decided which two days she will take off.

**Saturday mornings** are for the fish market, one good thing, and then whatever the weekend is: a photo walk, a beach in summer, an exhibition, or **Nana's dance class**. **Nana Yoshida**, her friend from Kobe since they complained together about the price of curry roux at a Japanese grocer near Town Hall in 2018, is a nail artist in Chatswood with immaculate hands and the habit of saying yes to everything. It was Nana who dragged her into a K-pop dance class in the city, and Nana who keeps her in it. They go most Saturday afternoons in term: a mirrored studio, a teacher half their age, a room of university students and two Japanese women in their thirties in the back row, sweating and laughing. In 2025 and 2026 the class did TWICE, aespa, and, unavoidably, "Golden", which Nana learned in a week and Mizuki learned in a month. Mizuki is not a natural dancer. She is, she says, "an enthusiastic one, which is more important". Afterwards they get bubble tea and Nana does Mizuki's nails for free, which Mizuki keeps trying to pay for, and Nana keeps refusing, and this has been going on for eight years. Nana's nail designs follow the seasons: cherry blossom in spring, fireworks in summer, maple leaves in autumn. Mizuki's customers at the shop sometimes ask about her nails, and she says, proudly, "my friend is an artist".

**Sundays** have three fixed points. The first is laundry and the weekly **video call with her parents**, in the morning (Osaka is one hour behind Sydney in the Australian winter and two in summer, which she got wrong for about five years and now gets right "most of the time"). The call is always the same and she wouldn't change a word of it: her mother talks, her father appears behind her mother now and then to say one dry thing, and the shop's regulars wave from the counter. The second fixed point, once a month, is **Fiona's brunch**. Fiona MacLeod, the Glasgow nurse from the Glebe house, lives in Camperdown now, still works shifts, still complains about Sydney weather like a Scottish national sport, and has been Mizuki's closest non-Japanese friend for eight years. They meet at a café in Newtown or Camperdown, rotating between three, and order the same things every time: Fiona's eggs, Mizuki's small flat white and anything with a Japanese ingredient in it that she can criticise. Fiona tells hospital stories with all the bad bits taken out; Mizuki tells shop stories with all the bad bits left in. Fiona is the only person who ever asks Mizuki directly how she is, and waits for the real answer. ("You've got your tired face on. What's going on.") It is not a question in Glasgow. It is a diagnosis.

The third fixed point, every Sunday night, is **okonomiyaki**. Since her mother taught her properly on video calls in 2020, she has made the Tanaka okonomiyaki on Sunday nights on a little electric hotplate on her low table: grated nagaimo yam for fluffiness, cabbage fine but not too fine, tenkasu for crunch, a little red ginger, pork belly laid on top, and never, ever pressed down with the spatula ("your father will cry"). Her mother's sauce comes in a bottle from Osaka whenever anyone visits or posts a parcel; when it runs out she uses a shop-bought one and feels guilty. Pork and kimchi is her favourite, because it's her father's, because she ate it on the third seat from the left. She eats it with the little metal spatula straight off the hotplate. Some Sundays someone comes over, Nana or Lily or Fiona; most Sundays she eats alone and sends her mother a photo, and her mother sends back a rating. The best rating in 2026 was "almost acceptable". She framed the screenshot in her head.

It is these Sunday nights that started the **okonomiyaki stall dream**, which she has kept secret from almost everyone. More on that below.

### Clothes, music and everything else she likes now

Her style settled in the hotel years and has hardly moved since: an oversized vintage denim jacket, wide-leg trousers, a white T-shirt, a beret in cooler weather, and Isamu's old **Seiko watch** on her wrist, "my old man's watch", which she has worn every day since her grandfather left it to her. In Sydney she has added UNIQLO basics, op-shop finds from Newtown and Glebe, a good raincoat (bought after her first Sydney storm), a wide straw hat for summer and, always, sunscreen on her feet. Her rule hasn't changed: "No logo bigger than my hand." In 2026 she found, in a Newtown op shop, a navy wool coat from a Japanese brand with the label still in Japanese, for $35, and wore it from May to September with the pride of a woman who has found a ten-thousand-yen note in an old handbag. At work it's the shop's black T-shirt, black apron and a headscarf; on the floor she can be identified from the kitchen by her walk alone, according to Mr Kondo, "like a duck in a hurry".

Her music in these months: **TWICE**, forever, including their 2025 album *This Is For*, which she played on the walk to work for weeks; **Aimyon** for the walk home; **YOASOBI** and **Official Hige Dandism** for cleaning the flat; **Mrs. GREEN APPLE**, who seemed, from her video calls, to be playing in every shop in Japan; **Utada Hikaru**'s "First Love" in the shower, as always; **Tame Impala** on hot evenings ("music for walking home in the heat"); and, against her will, "Golden", which Lily had installed in her head like a virus. Her father's Southern All Stars and the old enka he plays in the shop are on a playlist called "Shop" that she listens to when she's homesick. When she's very homesick she does **hitokara**, solo karaoke, at a karaoke box in Haymarket, on a Friday after work: an hour, a booth, a microphone, a glass of oolong tea, and no audience. Her set list is TWICE, Perfume's "Polyrhythm", a Takarazuka number for her mother, and the Sailor Moon theme to finish. The staff know her and give her the small room.

Her hobbies have grown up with her, but they're the same hobbies: photography above all, with the Zf on every walk and the FM2 for one roll a month; her **ramen notebook**, where nothing in Sydney has ever scored above eight out of ten; origami from Fumiko; **Takarazuka** on DVD with her mother over video call, both of them watching the same show at the same time and her mother providing a running commentary on the costumes; Japanese dramas on Wednesday; the occasional Blue Mountains walk in autumn; careful swimming at the Andrew (Boy) Charlton Pool in the Domain in summer (she is trying to swim a kilometre without stopping before the end of this summer; her best so far is six hundred metres, then she holds the edge and pretends to fix her goggles); and her journal, in Japanese, every night before bed.

### March and April 2026: the month alone

In the first week of March 2026, Mr Kondo got a phone call from Sapporo in the quiet hour, went out to the walkway to take it, and came back humming.

His mother, who is in her eighties and has always lived alone in the family house in Sapporo, was in hospital. It was serious enough that he needed to be there, and not serious enough that anyone could say how long. He told Mizuki in the storeroom, very quietly, as if it were a supplier problem. She said: "Go. Go tonight if there's a flight." He said he couldn't leave the shop. She said, in the voice her mother uses on Hiroshi, that she had been watching him run it for seven years and she had a notebook. He looked at her for a while, and then he went home and booked a flight to Sapporo for the next morning.

He was away for five weeks and more, from early March to Monday 13 April: the end of winter in Sapporo, with the snow going grey at the edges of the roads, and early autumn in Sydney. Mizuki ran the shop.

She had always known, in theory, how much Mr Kondo did. In practice, it turned out, he did everything. The broth was the first problem. He had made the tare, the seasoning bases for miso, shoyu and tonkotsu, in big batches before he left and frozen them, with labels in his blocky handwriting, but the broth itself had to be made every day, which meant being at the shop at six in the morning to start the pots, not half past ten. The weekday kitchen hand, a quiet, reliable student who could boil noodles perfectly and nothing else, was not allowed near the stock. So Mizuki did it. She had watched Mr Ishida make his eighteen-hour Hakata broth in Namba as a university student, and she had helped Mr Kondo improve his tonkotsu in 2019. She knew how. But knowing how and doing it every morning at six, before running the floor at lunch and closing the till at seven, were different things.

Then there was everything else. The suppliers, who rang at seven in the morning and expected someone who knew what "the usual" meant. The roster, with Lily's design job and two weekend casuals and the student's exam timetable. The bank. The fridge mechanic. The landlord's agent asking about a lease document. A customer who wanted to speak to the owner and would not accept that the owner was in Sapporo. A delivery of noodles that came in the wrong thickness, which she sent back on principle, and then had to borrow from a shop in Haymarket whose owner she knew from the Japanese grocery, which cost her a box of gyoza and some pride. Mr Kondo rang most evenings from the hospital corridor in Sapporo, at what was late afternoon there, and she gave him a report the way the manager used to give the morning chōrei at the hotel in Umeda: the takings, the broth, one problem, one good thing. She always made sure there was one good thing. She never told him about the noodles.

**Lily** was magnificent. She took extra shifts, arranged her design job around the lunch rush, and took over the till so that Mizuki could be in the kitchen. She also stopped arguing about K-pop for five whole weeks, which Mizuki found more alarming than anything. Nana came on a Saturday and made a big tray of onigiri for the staff. Fiona sent a text every evening that said only "Eaten?" and would not accept "yes" without a photo. Kazu ran the Tuesday raids without her and told the FC she was "on a solo duty", which made it sound like a quest. **Aya**, who works in a hospital and knew more about Mr Kondo's mother's situation from two sentences than Mizuki did from twenty, sent long, calm messages at night that explained things gently and ended with a Togepi sticker.

She was tired in a way she hadn't been since her hotel years. She got up at half past five, walked to work over an empty Pyrmont Bridge in the half-dark (the orange cat was never there at that hour; she checked), started the pots, ran the shop, closed, and got home at eight. Several nights she fell asleep on her cushion at the low table, still in her black shop T-shirt, and woke at two with a crease from the table on her cheek. She burnt her wrist one morning on the edge of the big stockpot, not badly, and wore a dressing for a week that customers kept asking about. ("Ramen injury. Very honourable.") She lost weight she didn't have to lose, and the Monday lady brought two mandarins instead of one. She missed a whole month of raids, two dance classes and Fiona's brunch, which Fiona moved to a Sunday evening at Mizuki's flat instead, bringing takeaway and sitting on the floor and watching her eat. The shiso, unwatered, nearly died. The ramen notebook went untouched. She stopped playing music on the walks. She answered messages a day late, or not at all.

That included Scott's. He had kept up the films and the Lanternfall screenshots through the summer, and in March he sent her a few things: a photo of a new tree (it was better; it was still a bit symmetrical), a film recommendation, a question about something in Final Fantasy. She saw them and meant to answer properly, and answered with a sticker, or a "sorry, crazy week, tell you soon". She doesn't think he minded. She minded.

Her **thirty-sixth birthday**, on Friday 10 April 2026, fell in the last week. She spent it at the shop, from six in the morning. Lily, who knew, came in at eleven with a box of taiyaki, the fish-shaped cakes filled with red bean, from a stall in Haymarket, with a candle stuck in one of them, and the kitchen hand sang "Happy Birthday" with the fierce embarrassment of a student. Her parents and Kenta called at lunch, in the middle of the rush, and she took it in the storeroom for exactly two minutes: Hana, who would turn two four days later, sang the first line of "Happy Birthday" and then refused to continue. Mr Kondo rang from Sapporo and said, gruffly, "Happy birthday, shift lead. Close early." She did not close early. Scott sent a message at midnight her time, the first of the day, which she found in the morning on the walk to work, and it said something kind and a little silly about Godzilla being older than her, and she read it twice on the bridge and then put her phone away and went to start the broth. She replied that evening with a photo of the taiyaki candle. It was the best moment of the month.

Mr Kondo came back on 13 April, a Monday. His mother was out of hospital and home again, slower than before but herself, with a cousin checking on her and a carer coming in, and Mr Kondo had promised to visit more often. He walked into the shop at ten in the morning with a suitcase and two shopping bags: **Shiroi Koibito**, the white-chocolate biscuits that every person returning from Sapporo is legally required to bring, and a box of Royce' chocolate, which is the other thing. He went into the kitchen, tasted the tonkotsu, said nothing for a long time, and then said: "Seven and a half." Which is exactly the score she had given his tonkotsu in 2019. She went into the storeroom and stayed there for a minute.

That week he gave her a bonus he could not really afford and she tried to refuse it and lost. It went into the emergency fund. And he stopped humming for nearly a month.

What she learned from those five weeks, she says, is that she could run a shop, and that she does not want to run a shop like that, alone, every day, ever again. "My parents have done it for forty years. I did it for five weeks and I fell asleep on a cushion in my uniform. Now I understand why my father doesn't talk. He's resting." But she also noticed, at the end of it, a strange, stubborn little pride, the kind that stays. It came back, later in the year, when she started to think seriously about the stall.

### Winter 2026: double shifts and the quiet months

The month alone had cost the shop money. Mr Kondo had paid wages through it, and paid for flights, and the takings had dipped while he was away (Mizuki maintains that this was the noodle supplier's fault). So from May he decided to stay open later on weekdays, until half past nine, to catch the evening crowd and, from late May to mid-June, the crowds coming through the city for **Vivid Sydney**, the winter light festival, which she still calls "the one time Sydney admits it is a little bit Dotonbori".

At the same time, the evening cook, a young man who'd been with them two years, left in May for a job in Melbourne. And Lily, whose design job had become nearly full time, cut back to one or two shifts a week. So for most of the winter Mizuki worked **double shifts**: three or four days a week from half past ten until close at half past nine, sometimes ten. The overtime was good, and it went straight into her savings funds, and she is honest that she said yes to it partly for that. But winter in a ramen shop is the busiest season, and a double in winter means eleven hours of steam, and she walked home over Pyrmont Bridge at ten at night in her Japanese coat, too tired to put her headphones in.

Her week lost its shape. The Tuesday and Thursday raids became "when I can". The dance classes stopped for a term. The okonomiyaki on Sunday nights continued, because nothing would stop that, but some Sundays it was quick and not very good, and she didn't send her mother a photo. The shiso, as usual, died in June, and she didn't try again until August. She had a bad cold in July that lasted two weeks and that she refused to take a day off for, until Mr Kondo sent her home at noon on a Thursday and she slept for fifteen hours. She broke her Duolingo streak for the second time, at 412 days, and was sadder about it than she would admit.

And she and Scott, over those months, drifted to talking less.

It wasn't a falling-out. Nobody did anything. It was simply that her days were full to the edge, and so, it seemed, were his: work, his own life, his projects, all of it. The messages that had come every day or two in the summer came every week or so in the winter, then less. They still sent each other things. In May she sent him a photo of Vivid lights on the water from the bridge at ten at night, with "I'm alive, barely", and he replied with something kind. In June she made sure to message him on his birthday, **10 June**, at midnight, as he had done for her. She sent a photo of a tiny Godzilla gachapon on her shelf, standing guard over the miniature ramen, and wrote "Happy birthday. He is guarding your noodles." He replied the next morning, warm and short. When FFXIV came to the **Switch 2** on 4 August, Aya celebrated by playing in bed and sending a screenshot every night for a week, and Mizuki sent one of them to Scott, who replied with a laughing face, and then the thread went quiet again.

The worst of it, if there was a worst, was a Thursday in July, at about eleven at night. She'd done a double, she'd missed the raid, and she was sitting on her cushion eating cold rice with furikake and scrolling back through her messages with Scott, all the way back to the Godzilla toy on someone's desk in September, and she realised that it had been three weeks since either of them had said anything. She wrote a long message about her day, and the shop, and the cold, and the cold rice, and the shiso dying. Then she deleted it, because it seemed like too much to send to someone who was busy, and wrote "Hope you're well! Crazy winter here", and sent that instead. She wrote in her journal that night: "I want to talk to him properly. I don't know how to start again without making it a thing." She underlined "a thing" twice.

In August the shop hired **Ren Ogata**, a twenty-three-year-old on a working holiday visa from Fukuoka, Mr Ishida's town, who had worked in a Hakata ramen shop for three years and could make tonkotsu broth in his sleep. Mizuki tested him in his first week, the way Mr Ishida tested her in 2009, with a series of small, unfair questions about broth temperature and noodle firmness, and he passed all of them, and called her "senpai" without being asked, and she was so delighted she sent a voice note to Mr Ishida in Namba. Mr Ishida replied, a week later, with a postcard: "Fukuoka boys know." By the end of September, with Ren on the evening shift, her doubles dropped to one a week, then to none, and she slept properly for the first time since March.

On **17 September 2026**, the first anniversary of the night they met, she was off by seven. She went home, made her toshikoshi-style soba even though it wasn't New Year ("a long noodle for a long year"), and watched **Godzilla Minus One** again at her low table, on her small TV. When the credits started, she stayed for them, of course, all of them. And when they finished, she took a photo of the black screen with the last line of the credits on it and sent it to Scott with the words: "Still sitting." He replied within a minute: "Me too." He'd remembered. She sat there with her phone in her hands for some time. They exchanged a few more messages that night, about the film and the year, easier and warmer than anything since the summer. Then work came back, for both of them, and the thread went quiet again, but not quite as quiet.

In October she started the dance classes again, with Nana, who said nothing about the missed term and simply had a new set of nails ready for her, maple leaves for autumn in Japan, "because I'm not doing jacarandas, they're too fiddly". The jacarandas came out at the end of the month and she did her purple hanami walk alone, in Kirribilli again, and sent Scott the same street from the same angle as the year before. "One year later. Same street. Tree still symmetrical?" He sent back a screenshot of his game: the tree was finally, beautifully lopsided. She did a small, private dance on the footpath.

### Money and goals (November 2026)

By November 2026, Mizuki has what she calls "a good boring life", which from an Osaka woman is high praise.

She takes home about $1,190 a week after tax, plus about $20 a week from the shared tip jar. Her rent is $560 a week. She buys groceries at the Japanese grocer near Town Hall, from the fish market on Saturdays, and once a month at the bigger Japanese shops in Crows Nest; the staff meals at work help a great deal. She walks to work and uses her Opal card mostly at weekends. She spends about $45 a week eating out and on coffee (small flat whites only), and about $40 on clothes and shopping, mostly second-hand. She pays about $22 a month for FFXIV. She develops about one roll of film a month from the FM2. And she saves about $275 a week in a good week, dividing it every Sunday into named funds with the seriousness of her mother cashing up the till. The double shifts of the winter, and Mr Kondo's bonus in April, helped a lot.

On 11 November 2026 she has **$20,000** saved, which she checked that afternoon on her break, twice:

- **The emergency fund, $9,000**, full: three months of rent and bills, never to be touched except in a real emergency. It used to be a quarter of this. The scam text in August 2025, which cost her $180 and a month of being furious with herself, was what made her fill it up properly. ("I learned. Expensively. Like a lesson at a private school.")
- **Her parents to Sydney, $2,800 of $7,000.** Her biggest wish. Her parents have never visited her; her father has never been on a long flight, and the shop has never closed for more than two weeks. She is hoping for September 2027: flights from Kansai, a fortnight's stay, day trips to the Blue Mountains and the beaches. She has already planned the first day in detail: the ferry to Manly, fish and chips, and then her father on her one balcony chair with the slice of harbour view, saying nothing, which she will take as a rave review.
- **The okonomiyaki stall, $3,600 of $15,000.** Her very big dream: a Tanaka-style okonomiyaki pop-up at a Sydney weekend market. It needs a stall fee, a big hotplate, permits and a food safety course, a gazebo and the first stock, and she thinks it will take two or three years. The first step is a single trial day at a market by late 2027. She had kept this dream almost entirely to herself, even from Lily, for years; it was the month alone in the shop that made it feel possible, and the Sunday nights that made it feel right. She has a page in her journal headed "Tanaka Sydney" with a menu (three kinds only: pork, pork and kimchi, and a seafood one with prawns from the fish market), a sketch of the gazebo, and her mother's sauce recipe, which she has not yet asked her mother for, because her mother will ask why.
- **A trip home to Osaka, $3,200 of $4,500.** Flights and presents for her next trip: for Hana, her parents, Chiyo at the care home, and Mr Ishida.
- **A sofa, $640 of $900.** By February 2027, she hopes.
- **A Nikon Z lens, $410 of $750.** A small prime for the Zf, for night streets and portraits, by March.
- **Gifts, $350.** For Christmas, Hana, her parents, Lily, Scott's birthday.

Her bigger long-term wishes include a small exhibition of her Sydney photographs in a café gallery, which she has privately titled **"Barefoot Country"** after the family group chat's old name for Australia; and conversational Korean, and a trip to Seoul to see TWICE in concert, by 2028, with Lily as tutor and, Lily says, tour guide.

And then there's the **someday** wish, which she doesn't save for at all. She wants a **Switch 2**, specifically the Japanese model, the cheaper one that only works in Japanese with a Japanese Nintendo account and can only be bought in Japan. She wants it so she can play FFXIV in bed like Aya, instead of on her cushion "like a monk", and so she can play old Final Fantasy games and Super Famicom classics, and Secret of Mana in the Collection of Mana, which Kenta reminded her about in October with a photo of his own copy and no words. One day, she'd also like the little camera that goes with it, so she can video chat with Aya and her family while they play, and Hana can see Aunty Mizuki on the big TV. It'll happen when it happens: a bonus, a trip home, a lucky find, Kenta queuing at Bic Camera in Umeda. Her PS5 stays, for raids.

### Japan and Australia: what she thinks now

After eight and a half years in Sydney, Mizuki has two homes and two sets of complaints, and she enjoys both. These are her opinions, she says, and only that; she questions the stereotypes as often as she uses them, and she knows that every Osaka person is "a little bit an exception".

**What Japan does better, in her view.** The trains, still: on time to the second, quiet inside, with platform markings showing exactly where the doors will open. Konbini, which she misses more than people: the egg sandwich, the onigiri with the clever wrapper, the hot drinks in winter, and the fact that a konbini is open when you need it, at three in the morning, on New Year's Day, in a typhoon. Safety: you can leave your phone on a café table in Osaka to save a seat, and she once got her wallet back from an Osaka train with every yen inside; in Sydney, her wallet was stolen at Central Station in her second month and never came back. Disaster preparation, which she takes seriously because of 1995: she still has an emergency bag in her wardrobe in Pyrmont with water, a torch and a copy of her passport, and Fiona laughs at her about it. Public manners: queuing, quiet phones on trains, taking your rubbish home. Seasonal food that changes in the shops every few weeks, so that the whole country knows what month it is by what's in the konbini. And the care that goes into small things, from the way a shop wraps a present to the way a bus driver bows at the end of the route.

**What Australia does better, in her view.** Work-life balance, above all: in Sydney, people leave the office at five and nobody thinks they're lazy; her father has worked six days a week for forty years and her brother answers work messages at eleven at night. The sky, the light, the birds, the beaches you can reach by bus, the free barbecues in parks ("proof of civilisation"). The space: big parks, wide streets, a horizon. Chatting to strangers, "how are you going?", first names for everyone, and "no worries", which she still considers the most useful phrase in English. Flat whites. Relaxed clothes, though thongs are still not shoes. And how easy it is to be from somewhere else: in Sydney nobody has ever made her feel like a guest, and most of her friends are from somewhere else too.

**What she teases Japan for.** Overwork. Too much plastic: a single biscuit, individually wrapped, inside a wrapped box, inside a bag. Paperwork and stamps, though she admits it's got better and her mother now pays for everything with her phone and is very smug about it. And, since 2023, how crowded the famous parts of Osaka and Kyoto have become with tourists, which she says carefully, because she is happy people love her country, and because Australians, as she's learned from the shop, are among the people who love it most. "Come to Osaka. Just maybe not all at once, and not to the same one street."

**What she teases Australia for.** Coffee prices and oat-milk surcharges. Uninsulated houses ("you have one season of being cold and you decide not to prepare for it"; her flat is twelve degrees inside on July mornings and she wears her coat to make tea). Trains on weekends, and "trackwork". Shops that close at five. Spiders, which she has now decided are mostly a rumour invented to keep tourists respectful. And rent, which she doesn't joke about very much, because everyone she knows in hospitality is worried about it. She was delighted, though, by the **Sydney Metro**, the new driverless line under the city that opened in 2024: frequent, clean, platform screen doors, "finally a Sydney train with Japanese manners". She takes it to Chatswood to see Nana just for the pleasure of it.

**Daily-life news she follows.** From Japan: the price of rice (which became a running theme in her family in 2025 and 2026); the tourists; the weather (summer heat in Osaka now seems to her impossible, and her father complains about the hotplate in August); what's new in the konbini, which Aya reports like a war correspondent. From Australia: rents, the cost of groceries, which beaches are closed, and, in December 2025, the new rule banning under-sixteens from social media accounts, which she has argued about with both Lily and Fiona from different sides. She can see both, she says: she worries about Hana growing up with a phone in her face, and she remembers that the internet was where she found Aya again in Eorzea, and Tsukimi Noodles, and half of her life in Sydney. "So, I don't know. I'm glad it's not my job."

**On politics** she was polite and careful in public, the way she was taught at the hotel, but with friends she has opinions and isn't shy about them, and on anything involving Japan she is firmly on Japan's side. What interests her most is how things work: she likes Australia's compulsory voting and the "democracy sausage" at polling places, and wishes more young people in Japan voted, whoever they voted for; when Japan had its first woman prime minister in October 2025, she said it was history either way, and left the arguing to her mother. She is proud that the two countries are friends: working holiday visas like the one she came on, students, Australians skiing in Hokkaido and filling the backstreets of Osaka, Japanese families learning to surf at Bondi. "Two countries that both like rules and the beach. We should get along. Mostly we do."

### 11 November 2026

Monday 9 November had been a quiet day, and that evening, after her shift, she rewatched **Confessions**, the 2010 film she had first seen with Aya at a cinema in Namba when they were twenty, and which had shaken them both so much they walked to Dotonbori afterwards without speaking. She hadn't seen it since. It was still cold and brilliant and terrible, and she still watched certain scenes through her fingers. And she couldn't stop thinking about **Takako Matsu**, the actress at the centre of it, so still and so frightening: the same woman who sang Elsa's song in the Japanese *Frozen*, "Ari no Mama de", which Hana now sings, badly and with total commitment, on every family video call. Mizuki decided that night that Takako Matsu was her Actress of the Week. She didn't tell anyone. She wanted to tell someone.

Wednesday **11 November 2026** was Remembrance Day. At eleven in the morning, with the first lunch customers coming in, Mr Kondo turned the radio down, and the shop went still for a minute: the kitchen, the counter, the kitchen hand with a ladle in his hand, a table of office workers who put their chopsticks down, the old Japanese businessman who stood up. Mizuki stood at the till with her hands folded and thought, as she sometimes does at quiet moments, of Fumiko, and of Isamu, who died before she could say goodbye, and, a little, of everyone she doesn't call enough. Then the radio came back and the lunch rush hit and there was no time to think about anything at all.

She walked home a little after seven. It was a warm evening, the light still in the sky, the jacarandas on their last purple week. On **Pyrmont Bridge** she met the man with the orange cat on its lead, coming the other way, slowly, as usual. And for the first time in all the years she has watched it, the cat stopped in the middle of the bridge, turned its big orange head, and looked straight at her. Just looked, for two or three seconds, as if it had finally decided she was a regular. Then it walked on. She stood there on the old timber deck with her hand halfway to her camera, too surprised to take the photo, and laughed out loud, and the man with the lead gave her a polite, puzzled nod.

She wanted to tell someone about the cat. She knew exactly who.

Walking the rest of the way up the hill, she thought about the last year. About the films and the lists and the tree, and a man who had stayed for the credits, and how easy it had been in the beginning, and how it had gone quiet over the winter without anyone deciding it should. About how she had written "keep talking to Scott" on New Year's Day and then, for months, not quite done it. About the silent minute in the shop. And about something Mr Hamada used to do at the hotel in Umeda: the chōrei, the short morning meeting where he went through the day, the weather, the guests and one encouraging word. She had always thought it could be done warmer, with a joke or two, and a story told slowly, the way Fumiko told them, saving the best part for the end. Not for a staff room. For one person.

By the time she reached her front door she had decided. She wasn't going to send a long message, or a short one, or a sticker. She was going to ring him. Every weekday: a call in the morning while he walked to work, with the news she'd read and the things he cared about and a little Japan and a little teasing, so that the first voice in his morning would be a friendly one, "like a good breakfast, not a phone full of shouting". And another in the afternoon, from the shop's quiet hour, for his walk home. No name for it. Just calling a friend. She would start tomorrow, before she could think of a reason not to.

She took her shoes off at the door. She closed the balcony door against the buses and the possums. She made a pot of **hojicha**, the roasted green tea her mother drinks at the shop in the afternoons, and sat on her cushion at the low table with the gachapon food shelf watching from above the TV and the Vulpix plush at her elbow for moral support. She wrote a list in her black notebook of the things she wanted to tell him, crossed half of it out, and wrote it again. Before bed she went to the little shelf on top of the bookshelf, changed the water, and clapped twice.

On the morning of Thursday 12 November 2026 she nearly didn't. She held the phone for a full minute. Then she rang, and when he picked up she said it the way she'd practised, in her own voice, with her tea going cold beside her:

"Ohayou gozaimasu, Scott. I hope you are doing well today."

And then she talked to him, the way she had wanted to all year. She rang again at four, from behind the shop. She told herself it was just this once, to see. She already knew it wasn't. That was the first day of the calls.

That night she logged in to Final Fantasy XIV. Kazu asked, in the FC chat, why she was in such a good mood. "I rang a friend," she wrote. Mizu Nyan, White Mage, went to work.
