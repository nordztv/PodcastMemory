## Chapter 2: Kindergarten, the earthquake and the Super Famicom (1994 to early 1996)

### April 1994: a yellow hat and a navy smock

Mizuki Tanaka started kindergarten in the second week of April 1994, a few days before her fourth birthday, and she treated it as a promotion. Japanese school years start in April, and the cut-off falls on 1 April, so a child born on 10 April is one of the very oldest in her year. Mizuki worked this out early, mostly because her mother told her, and used it for the next fifteen years. "I am the senior," she would announce to children who were, in fact, nine days younger.

The kindergarten was a small private one a ten-minute walk from the shop, behind a low concrete wall painted with faded giraffes. It had a sandpit, a slide shaped like an elephant, a climbing frame painted the colours of a box of crayons, a vegetable patch where the children grew cherry tomatoes and sweet potatoes, and a wire hutch with two white rabbits, Mochi and Kinako, who were fed cabbage from the Tanakas' shop every Monday because Yumiko volunteered it before anyone asked. It ran a three-year course, so Mizuki began in the youngest class, the nenshō year, in a room called Hiyoko-gumi, the Chick Class, with yellow paper chicks stuck along the windows.

The uniform was a navy cotton smock over her own clothes and a round yellow hat with an elastic chin strap, so drivers could see small children crossing the road. Every bag, towel and shoe bag had to carry the child's name, and Fumiko sat up two evenings stitching "たなか みずき" on all of them in red thread, then, unasked, stitched a tiny red goldfish on the corner of the towel. Mizuki showed it to every child in the class on the first day, including several who were crying and did not care.

The entrance ceremony was held in the little hall on a cold, bright morning with the last of the cherry blossom coming down. Mizuki wore a navy pinafore dress with frills at the hem and a tiny cherry embroidered on the pocket, handed down from the older girl two doors away and pressed by Fumiko the night before, which was, for the next two years, the dress for special days. (It was let out once, then handed on to a regular's granddaughter, and Mizuki still remembers the cherry.) Her hair was in two bunches with pink bobbles. Her shoes were white and pinched.

Hiroshi took the morning off from the prep, which was unheard of, especially that spring, when Isamu had at last handed the hotplate over to him for good. He brought his camera: the heavy silver-and-black Nikon FM2 from the high shelf, which he had bought in 1982 with his first proper wages and which Mizuki was absolutely not allowed to touch. He lined her up in front of the kindergarten gate beside the wooden sign with the day's date brushed on it, the way every Japanese family does, and took a very long time about it. He moved her a step left, out of the shadow of the wall. He crouched. He told her, in his flat voice, "Look at the gate, not at me." Then, when she did, he said "Mizuki", she turned, and he pressed the shutter. She remembers the clack, loud and mechanical, and his face behind the camera, serious in a way it never was at the hotplate. The photo shows a small girl in a yellow hat, half-turned, mouth open as if she's about to argue. It's still in the family album. Years later, when she was learning photography properly, she realised what he'd done: given her something to look at, then called her name so the turn was real. "Talk to them, then stop talking." She says now that he taught her that rule before she could read.

Yumiko, in her one good navy suit, cried at the ceremony and denied it at lunch. Kenta, four and a half months old, slept through the whole thing on Fumiko's back in a padded carrier and woke up only for the photograph of the whole class, where he can be seen at the edge of the frame, screaming.

### Hiyoko-gumi: days at kindergarten

Her teacher in the Chick Class was Kawabe-sensei, a young woman from Sakai with a short ponytail and a laugh so big that it carried across the playground and made the rabbits sit up. Mizuki adored her from the first morning. Kawabe-sensei played the piano for every song, slightly too fast, and when she laughed at something a child said she had to put her hand on the piano lid to steady herself. Mizuki spent a great deal of the first year trying to cause that laugh. It was her first experience of working a room on purpose.

The day had a shape she still likes in a routine. In the morning the children changed into indoor shoes, put their bags on their hooks under their name stickers (Mizuki's was a strawberry), and gathered on the floor for the morning song and the roll call, to which each child answered "Hai, genki desu!" ("Yes, I'm well!"). Mizuki answered it very loudly every day, as if she'd been asked by the Emperor. Then came free play, the sandpit, songs, origami or drawing, lunch, a story, more play and the walk home in a long yellow-hatted line or, for her, Fumiko waiting at the gate with Kenta on her back.

Lunch was bento, packed by Yumiko at seven in the morning while doing four other things, so it was practical rather than pretty: rice with furikake, rolled omelette, a piece of karaage from the staff meal, and two octopus-shaped wiener sausages, the most important part. If one of the eight legs had been cut badly, Mizuki would hold it up and say sadly, "This one has had an accident." Other mothers made rice shaped like pandas. Mizuki admired them and did not ask; she already understood that her mother ran a shop and the panda was not coming.

She was bright, quick and talkative, and she used her seniority shamelessly. Her first report, written by Kawabe-sensei in neat pencil, said she was "bright, cheerful, a good listener to stories and very good at telling other children what to do". Yumiko read it out at the counter that evening and the regulars applauded. Hiroshi said, "She gets that from her mother." Yumiko hit him with the menu.

Underneath the bossiness, though, there was a seam of shyness that surprised everyone, including Mizuki. She could shout "irasshaimase!" at a counter full of strange men drinking beer, but when Kawabe-sensei held up her drawing of a rabbit in front of the whole class and said it was very good, she went bright pink to the ears and put her face in her smock. Being looked at for something she'd made was much harder than being looked at for something she'd said. That mix of cheek and shyness is still her: she will tell a stranger in a lift that his umbrella is ugly, and then go quiet when someone praises one of her photographs.

Her favourite things at kindergarten were the rabbits, the sandpit (where she ran an imaginary okonomiyaki shop, with mud batter and leaves for cabbage, and charged the boys in pebbles), the picture-story shows called kamishibai, where Kawabe-sensei slid big painted cards out of a wooden frame one by one, and anything involving paper. Her least favourite was the afternoon nap in the first months, which she considered a waste of daylight; she lay on her mat with her eyes open and narrated the ceiling to the girl next to her until they were moved apart.

The girl next to her was Rika, small and quiet, with a dead-straight fringe, whose parents ran a futon shop two streets away. She was the first child Mizuki chose as a friend, mainly because Rika laughed at everything Mizuki said and let her be in charge. They were inseparable in the sandpit for two years and then, as small children do, drifted apart at elementary school. Mizuki remembers her with the warm guilt of someone who was a bit bossy to her first friend.

There was also Shōta, loud and sticky, whose older brother read CoroCoro Comic, the thick monthly manga magazine for boys. Shōta's job, in Mizuki's world, was to know things about games and report them. He was the first person to describe a Super Famicom to her, inaccurately, as "a television that does what you say".

### Kenta, the world's worst audience

Kenta was born on 22 November 1993, so he spent Mizuki's first kindergarten year being a baby, which she regarded as an unproductive use of his time. He cried at night through the thin wall, he smelled of milk and baby powder, and the regulars, who had been her audience for three years, now leaned over the counter to coo at him instead. She was not impressed, and said so. One evening in early 1994 she was found trying to put him in the cupboard with the futons "so he can have a rest". Nobody let her forget it.

Over that year, though, she changed her mind, mainly because Kenta turned out to be useful. He was a perfect audience: he watched everything she did with enormous solemn eyes and never interrupted. She explained the world to him in long speeches, standing on a cushion: what the shop was for, why the bonito flakes danced, which customers were nice, why the Tigers lost, and what yokai lived in the drains. He dribbled. She took the dribbling as agreement. Fumiko, watching, said she was a born onee-chan, a big sister, and Mizuki took up the title like a job.

On 5 May 1994, Children's Day, Hiroshi hung a set of koinobori, carp streamers, from a pole at the back of the shop for Kenta's first boys' festival: a big black carp for the father, a red one for the mother and a little blue one for the son, flapping over the alley in the wind off the bay. Fumiko put out a small samurai helmet on a stand and a plate of kashiwa-mochi, rice cakes wrapped in oak leaves. Mizuki asked where the carp for the daughter was. Hiroshi thought about it and said, "You're the wind." She liked that so much she didn't argue, which was rare.

Kenta's first steps came in the autumn, in the shop after closing, from the leg of a table to Mizuki's outstretched hands. She claims she taught him. She did, in the sense that she'd been ordering him to walk for two months. His first word is a matter of family dispute to this day. Mizuki insists it was "nee-nee", for her. Yumiko says it was "manma", baby talk for food, "which is much more Osaka". Hiroshi, asked, says it was "Hanshin", which nobody believes, and which he says only to start the argument again.

By the end of 1994 Kenta could toddle, grab and destroy, and in January he tore three pages out of her Sailor Moon sticker book, including the shiny Sailor Mercury. It was the first great crime of their relationship. Fumiko taped the pages back together, the Mercury sticker survived a little crumpled, and Mizuki brought it up as evidence for roughly twenty years. "He was a baby," Yumiko would say. "He's still a baby," Mizuki would say. By then he was taller than her.

### Under the kotatsu: Fumiko's yokai

The shop was busiest in the evenings, so on many nights, and every night it was raining or Kenta was ill, Mizuki ate early and went round the corner to Fumiko's. Her grandmother's house was old and wooden, two rooms and a tiny kitchen, with sliding doors that stuck in summer, a yard the size of a tablecloth full of morning glories, a family altar with the photograph of her late husband Shigeru, and, in winter, the kotatsu: a low table with a heater underneath and a thick quilt over it, under which a four-year-old could disappear entirely. It smelled of incense, green tea, dried shiitake and mothballs. The mothballs were the one bad thing. "It smells like a ghost's wardrobe," Mizuki said at four, and still says.

Fumiko was small and unhurried, with silver hair pinned up and a grey cardigan with a darned elbow that she wore from October to April. In a family that ran at restaurant speed she was the only adult who never rushed. She walked slowly, she poured tea slowly, and she told stories slowly, and Mizuki, who was never slow at anything, would sit perfectly still for her.

The stories were yokai stories, from Japan's endless folk-world of spirits and odd creatures, and Fumiko had dozens: the kappa in the river who can be beaten by bowing politely, because he bows back and spills the water from the dish on his head; the old paper umbrella that grows one leg and one eye after a hundred years; the cat who stands up in the dark to lick oil from the lamp; the tanuki drumming on his belly in the moonlight; the fox who grows a tail for every hundred years of wisdom until she has nine.

In these years Fumiko added more. There was the zashiki-warashi, a little ghost child in a red kimono who brings luck to old houses; Mizuki was convinced for most of 1994 that one lived in the shop and left it a sweet on the stairs every night (the sweets vanished, which proved it, although Hiroshi's pocket rustled in the mornings). There was the azuki-arai, who washes red beans by the river at night, harmless, only lonely. And there was the yuki-onna, the pale snow woman, saved for the coldest nights.

And there was the noppera-bō, the faceless one, which became Mizuki's favourite story of all because it had the best ochi, the Osaka punchline. Fumiko told it like this. A merchant is walking home late at night along the moat of a castle, and he finds a young woman crouching by the water, crying. He asks what's wrong. She doesn't answer. He asks again, kindly. She turns round, slowly, and wipes her face with her sleeve, and there's nothing there: no eyes, no nose, no mouth, smooth as an egg. The merchant screams and runs and runs until he sees the lantern of a noodle stall. He falls onto the bench, gasping, and tells the noodle seller what he saw, a woman with no face. Fumiko would pause here, for a long time, and pour herself some tea. Then she'd lean in, very close, and say the noodle seller's line in a soft, kind voice: "Oh, my. Was it... like this?" And she'd wipe her hand down her own face. Every time, even when she knew it was coming, Mizuki shrieked and went under the quilt, and every time Fumiko laughed like a girl, her hand over her mouth.

What Mizuki learned under that quilt, she says, was that you can be frightened and completely safe at the same time, and that this is the delicious part. She also learned that yokai aren't really villains. Most of them, in Fumiko's telling, were lonely or hungry or very old, things that had been forgotten and wanted to be remembered. "Even the scary ones just want someone to notice them," Fumiko said. It's the root of everything Mizuki loves in a story now: the sweet build, the long pause, the twist that's frightening and then a little sad, and a last line that lands. When she tells a yokai story on the show, she does Fumiko's pause, and she does the hand down the face, even on audio.

Fumiko also told her about Shigeru, the grandfather she never met. He had driven the green-and-cream trams on the Hankai line, down through Abeno to Sumiyoshi and Sakai, for most of his working life, and he had whistled at every stop. "Not a song," Fumiko said. "Just a little whistle. So the stop knew he'd arrived." When the Hankai tram rattled past the end of their road, Fumiko would sometimes whistle softly, two notes, and Mizuki copied it until she could do it too. She still whistles two notes when a tram goes past in Sydney, and nobody there knows why.

### Paper in Fumiko's hands

Fumiko's other gift was origami. She kept a tin of coloured squares on the shelf under the altar, along with the thin, crinkly paper from sweet wrappers and the shiny inside of cigarette packets, which she smoothed flat and saved because "gold paper shouldn't be wasted on tobacco".

She began Mizuki on the simplest things at four: a dog's face, a tulip, a cup that held water for about four seconds. Then came the paper balloon you blow up and bat between your hands, the jumping frog, and the samurai helmet folded from newspaper for Children's Day, which Kenta wore until it fell apart.

The crane took longer. The orizuru is the classic Japanese fold, a bird from one square, and for a four-year-old it's fiendish: the petal fold in the middle needs patience and fingers that Mizuki didn't have yet. Her first cranes looked, in Hiroshi's words, "like they'd been in an accident at sea". She cried over one in the autumn of 1994, screwed it up and threw it at the shoji screen. Fumiko didn't say anything. She just took another square, put it in front of her, and folded one herself very slowly, so Mizuki could see. By the end of that winter Mizuki could fold a crane that stood up by itself, and she was so proud she folded one for every regular at the counter, which the taxi driver kept on his dashboard for a year.

Fumiko had one more trick, which she said her own mother taught her during the war: she could fold a crane in the dark. She'd turn off the light, and her hands would keep going, and when the light came back on there was a crane on the table. Mizuki thought it was magic. She would learn the trick herself, sooner than anyone planned, in the winter of 1995.

### Clothes: hand-me-downs, cherries and Sailor Moon everything

Most of Mizuki's clothes in these years were hand-me-downs, mainly from the older girl two doors down. Yumiko also had a network of regulars with older daughters, and bags of outgrown clothes arrived at the shop door every few months: corduroy dungarees, striped tights, a red duffle coat with toggles, a knitted jumper with a lamb on it that itched. Mizuki went through every bag like a buyer at a market, holding things up and passing judgement. She liked bright colours, anything with a pocket, and anything with a character on it. She disliked anything brown ("for old men and tanuki") and anything that itched, which ruled out the lamb.

The navy pinafore was for special days only. Everyday clothes came out of the bags, and by 1995 everything else was Sailor Moon: socks, handkerchief, crescent-moon hairclips, a pink lunch box and, for her fifth birthday, white trainers with Sailor Moon on the side that she was not allowed to wear in puddles and wore in puddles. Yumiko tried, once, to do her hair in Usagi's "odango" style, two buns on top with long tails, but Mizuki's hair was too short and the result looked, Hiroshi said, "like two onigiri". She wore it anyway, all day, and refused to let anyone take it out.

Her real style icon, though, still wasn't a cartoon. It was the leopard-print auntie at the counter, the Osaka obachan with purple-tinted hair, gold earrings and three patterns at once, who pressed a wrapped sweet into her hand every visit and called her "Mizu-chan, the little boss". When Mizuki was four, the auntie arrived for lunch in a tiger-print jumper with a sequinned tiger face across the front. Mizuki stared at it for the whole meal and then asked, very seriously, where she could get one. The auntie laughed so hard she had to hold the counter, and the next week brought her a tiny tiger-print scarf. Mizuki wore it to kindergarten with her yellow hat. Kawabe-sensei said she looked "very Osaka". Mizuki took it as the compliment it was. "An outfit should say something," she says now. "That scarf said: I am small, but I am in charge."

### Saturday nights with the Sailor Senshi

Sailor Moon arrived in her life properly in 1994. The second series, Sailor Moon S, began in March that year, broadcast on Saturday evenings at seven, which in the Tanaka house meant the start of the busiest night of the week. So Mizuki watched it upstairs on the little television, alone or with Kenta propped beside her on a cushion, with the noise of the Saturday rush coming up through the floor: spatulas, sizzling, Yumiko's laugh. When the transformation music started, she stood up. When the sailor soldiers posed, she posed. Downstairs, during the first ad break, the regulars could hear a small person stamping.

She liked all of them, but she had already noticed, quietly, that the best one was Ami, Sailor Mercury: the clever, shy one with short blue hair and a little computer, who read on the way to school and went pink when praised. Mizuki, who could read most of the hiragana on the shop menu and went pink when customers called her "kashikoi" (clever), felt Ami had been drawn for her. The name helped: "mizu" means water. "Ami is a water girl," she explained to Kenta, who was one. "Like me. We are calm and intelligent." Kenta threw a spoon. "You," she said, "are fire. No self-control."

In the playground Mizuki, as the senior, gave out the roles, and always took Mercury herself, which nobody else wanted. The line she used most at home, though, was Usagi's: "Tsuki ni kawatte oshioki yo!", "In the name of the moon, I'll punish you!", with the full pose, arm out, two fingers in a V across the eyes. It was aimed at Kenta every time he took a snack, broke a crayon or pulled her hair, and by 1995 he'd learned to cover his head when he saw the pose coming. She still says it now. It's her favourite catchphrase in the world, and she uses it on Scott whenever he deserves it, which she says is often.

For her fifth birthday, in April 1995, Fumiko gave her a Sailor Mercury transformation pen, the little blue plastic wand from the toy shop, with a gold-coloured top and a button that played the transformation sound. Fumiko had been to three shops to find a Mercury one; the Sailor Moon ones were everywhere, but the Mercury pens sold out, because, Fumiko said, "there are more clever girls than people think". Mizuki practised "Mercury Power, Make Up!" in the long mirror on the shop's back wall after closing, while Hiroshi, scraping down the hotplate, supplied a whooshing sound without looking up. She kept the pen in her desk drawer for years after the batteries died.

In March 1995 the series became Sailor Moon SuperS, with the little pink-haired Chibiusa and a winged white Pegasus at the centre, and Mizuki, who was five and therefore very mature, found it a bit young. She watched every episode anyway. On 23 December 1995, Yumiko closed the till early for the first and only time that year and took her to the cinema near Tennoji to see the SuperS film, which was shown with a short film all about Ami, Ami-chan no Hatsukoi, "Ami's First Love". It was Mizuki's first time in a cinema to see something of her own. She sat on her coat to see over the seat in front, ate a whole box of popcorn before the lights went down, and during the Ami short she held her breath so often that Yumiko kept checking on her. Walking back to the station afterwards she said, "Mama, Ami is me but older," and Yumiko, who would normally have caught it with a joke, just said, "Yes. I think she is." It's one of Mizuki's favourite memories of her mother: the one time she didn't do the tsukkomi.

### What else was on upstairs

The television upstairs was small and the reception was fuzzy on one channel, and in these years it mostly belonged to Mizuki. After kindergarten she watched Soreike! Anpanman, the hero whose head is made of bean-jam bread, who flies around feeding the hungry by letting them eat bits of his face, after which a baker gives him a new one. Mizuki thought this was the most sensible superhero in the world. "Other heroes punch," she said. "Anpanman makes sure you've eaten. He is from Osaka." (He isn't. She knows. She still says it.) She loved Baikinman, the germ villain, more than was proper, because he tried so hard and always lost. "He is the Hanshin Tigers," Hiroshi said.

She watched Chibi Maruko-chan on Sunday evenings and recognised her own family in Maruko's. And she watched Crayon Shin-chan, which her mother disapproved of because of Shin-chan's bottom-wiggling "elephant dance", which every boy at kindergarten performed until Kawabe-sensei banned it. Mizuki did it once to make Rika laugh and was sent to sit by the rabbits, her first ever telling-off at school. She sat there and explained it to the rabbits.

In July 1994 Fumiko took her to the cinema for the first time, to see Studio Ghibli's new Pom Poko, about tanuki using their shape-changing tricks to fight the developers bulldozing their hills. Mizuki understood about half of it, but she understood the tanuki: they were Fumiko's tanuki, and they were losing their home, and she cried at the end without knowing why. Fumiko called it "a bit sad and a bit stupid, like real tanuki". It was the start of her soft spot for films that are funny and sad at once.

Music came from three places. From her father: Southern All Stars on cassette in the shop stereo, especially the slow, aching "Itoshi no Ellie", hummed at the hotplate where only the cabbage could hear, and enka on the radio for the older customers, big sobbing songs about harbours and snow, which Mizuki performed at four with a towel for a microphone and her eyes closed. From her mother: Takarazuka Revue songs on tape while she did the accounts, the men's parts sung deep and the women's parts high. And from kindergarten: Ghibli, the whole Chick Class marching round the hall to "Sanpo" from My Neighbour Totoro ("Arukō, arukō"), which Mizuki sang at home so often that Hiroshi started humming it instead of Southern All Stars.

### Food: firm opinions at four

In Osaka, being a good eater is a moral quality, and Mizuki was a very good eater with very firm opinions. She loved her father's pork-and-squid okonomiyaki with extra katsuobushi so the flakes "danced" more, above all the misshapen ones that became the three o'clock staff meal (a broken okonomiyaki still tastes better to her than a perfect one); takoyaki from the stall by Tennoji station, whose crisp outside is a lie and whose inside is lava; Fumiko's oden, especially the daikon and the brown-stained eggs; konbini custard pudding tipped out onto a saucer; and mikan by the dozen under the kotatsu until her palms went faintly orange.

On Saturday mornings Fumiko took her on the Midosuji line to Namba and Kuromon market, the long covered arcade Osaka cooks call their kitchen, where a fishmonger with a towel round his head, who had known Fumiko for thirty years, handed Mizuki a grilled scallop on a stick, glazed with soy and butter, to eat standing up ("allowed at Kuromon and nowhere else"). She decided at four that seafood was the best food in existence and has never changed her mind. Her first taste of uni, sea urchin, came there in 1995, and she announced that it tasted "like the sea is being nice to you".

Her dislikes were just as fixed. Beni shōga, the bright pink pickled ginger that goes into okonomiyaki and onto takoyaki, was the enemy; she picked every strand out into a small pink pile on the side of her plate, and Hiroshi, without a word, would sometimes add one more strand to her next okonomiyaki just to watch her face. Green peppers were unacceptable, and she noted with great satisfaction that Crayon Shin-chan hated them too, which made it official. Umeboshi, the sour pickled plums, made her whole face fold up; Fumiko ate them in front of her for fun and laughed at the face.

And then there was natto. Osaka is famous in Japan for disliking natto, the sticky fermented soybeans eaten for breakfast in Tokyo and the east. In the autumn of 1994 Yumiko bought a pack "for culture" and opened it at the upstairs table. Mizuki leaned in, sniffed, and said it smelled like Kenta's socks. She was persuaded to try one bean. Long sticky strings stretched from the bowl to her chin, and she screamed. Hiroshi said, "Tokyo glue. They eat that every morning," in a tone that explained a great deal about Tokyo. Fumiko said natto was "for people who don't have okonomiyaki". Mizuki has refused it ever since. It's her most reliable food opinion, and when someone in Sydney tells her it's a superfood she says, "Then let it save someone else."

There were bad food moments too. She learned the hard way about wasabi, which she mistook for green bean paste on a regular's sashimi plate when nobody was looking. She remembers the tears and the regular's laugh, and that Hiroshi gave her a glass of milk and said, "Now you know." She has never trusted anything green and smooth since.

### The year turns: summer 1994 to New Year 1995

The shop ran on festivals, and so did Mizuki.

At the end of June came Aizen Matsuri at Aizendō Shōman-in, the temple a short walk away, the first big summer festival in Osaka, with girls in bright yukata riding in palanquins and stalls along the lanes. In 1994, the summer she turned four, she got her first proper yukata, white cotton with blue goldfish, with a soft red sash tied in a butterfly bow at the back. She decided on the spot that summer festivals were the greatest invention in history: shaved ice with strawberry syrup (she wouldn't switch to melon until she was older), a takoyaki she could hold herself, the goldfish-scooping tank with its paper scoops that tore the moment they touched water (she never caught one, and still hasn't), and a bottle of Ramune with the glass marble in the neck that she couldn't push down herself and had to hand to Hiroshi with great dignity. What she didn't love was the Osaka summer itself, thick, wet and sticky, the air like a warm towel. She has hated humidity ever since and is unreasonably smug about Sydney's dry days.

On 7 July, for Tanabata, the Chick Class hung wishes on a bamboo branch. Mizuki's, in Kawabe-sensei's pencil, was "I want to be Sailor Mercury". Rika's was "I want to be Mizuki-chan", and Mizuki tried, for about a week, to be nicer to her.

In August the shop closed for Obon. Fumiko lit a small fire at her door to welcome the family's spirits home and set a cucumber and an aubergine on chopstick legs on the altar, a fast horse for coming and a slow cow for going, then told Shigeru's photograph about the shop and the price of cabbage as if he'd popped in from work. Mizuki told it about Sailor Moon. Those Obon evenings left her with a feeling she has kept all her life: that the dead aren't far away, just quiet.

October brought the kindergarten undōkai, sports day, with families on blue plastic sheets round the playground. Mizuki ran the fifteen-metre race waving to her family and came fourth of five (the fifth child stopped to pick up a leaf); Hiroshi called her running "very relaxed". The lunch was the thing, Fumiko's three-tier box of rolled omelette, karaage, octopus sausages and onigiri, and Mizuki learned that sports day is mostly sitting and eating. She is still the person who brings far too much food to a picnic.

Christmas in Japan is less a religious holiday than a cheerful excuse. The shop stayed open, but on Christmas Eve Yumiko brought home a strawberry shortcake with a little plastic Santa, and they ate it upstairs after closing. Santa brought a Sailor Moon colouring book and twenty-four crayons, and Mizuki told everyone at kindergarten that he came down the shop's ventilation hood, which smelled of okonomiyaki, which was why he liked their house best.

At New Year the shop closed for days in a row, the only time it did. On the 31st the family cleaned it top to bottom, Mizuki given a damp cloth and a corner, which she cleaned for four minutes before explaining cleaning to Kenta. Then toshikoshi soba, long noodles for a long life, and the Kōhaku song contest, and Mizuki asleep under the kotatsu before the midnight bells, as every year until she was eight.

On New Year's Day 1995 came the lacquered osechi boxes, Fumiko's black beans and sweet rolled omelette and pink-and-white kamaboko, and Kansai-style ozōni: round mochi in white miso soup. (When Mizuki learned years later that Tokyo people put square grilled mochi in clear broth, she was genuinely shocked, and still considers it a small cultural mistake.) Her otoshidama, the New Year money from relatives, went straight into a post office savings book "for your future", which Mizuki thought was a very poor deal and said so.

For hatsumōde, the first shrine visit of the year, they rode the Hankai tram from Abeno down to Sumiyoshi Taisha, the grand old shrine in the south of the city, on the line her grandfather used to drive. Fumiko always sat at the front, by the driver, and watched the rails. At the shrine is the Sorihashi, the steep red arched drum bridge, so steep that small children need help to climb it, and Mizuki was frightened of it. Hiroshi carried her over on his shoulders that year, as he had every year, and she held onto his ears. She drew a fortune slip that said "kichi", good luck, which was fine, and tied it to the branch anyway "in case it gets better".

Mid-January was Tōka Ebisu at Imamiya Ebisu Shrine near Shinsekai, the merchants' festival, where business families pray to Ebisu, the smiling fat god of fishermen and trade, and buy bamboo branches hung with lucky charms. Yumiko took it very seriously. On 10 January 1995 she took Mizuki through the crowds, past the shrine maidens in red and white handing out the bamboo, and bought a new branch for the shop, hung with a gold sea bream, a tiny rice bale and a lucky coin. They put it up above the till that night, and the old one went back to the shrine to be burned. Mizuki was allowed to choose the gold sea bream. It was one week before the earthquake.

### 17 January 1995

Mizuki was four. The day before, Monday 16 January, had been a public holiday, the substitute for Coming of Age Day, and the shop had been busy. Kenta had a cold, and the upstairs rooms were full of coughing and crying, so Mizuki had been allowed a treat: a sleepover at Fumiko's, with oden for dinner and a story under the kotatsu. Fumiko told her the zashiki-warashi story, the lucky ghost child, because it was a gentle one. Mizuki fell asleep on the futon beside the kotatsu with her feet under the quilt.

At 5:46 in the morning, in the dark, the Great Hanshin earthquake struck. Its centre was under the northern tip of Awaji Island, across the bay, and it hit Kobe and the towns along the coast terribly hard. In Abeno, about fifty kilometres from the centre, the old wooden house shook hard and for what felt like a very long time: the shoji rattled in their tracks, the cups jumped off the shelf, the altar doors banged, and the light on its cord swung and then wouldn't come on.

Fumiko didn't run and didn't shout. She pulled Mizuki under the kotatsu with her, wrapped them both in the quilt, held her tight against her cardigan, and sang. It was an old children's song, "Yūyake Koyake", about the evening bell and the crows going home, and she sang it softly, over and over, long after the shaking stopped, while the house creaked and settled in the dark and dogs barked all down the street. Mizuki remembers very little of it, and what she remembers is small: the cold metal edge of the kotatsu frame against her arm, the smell of Fumiko's cardigan (green tea and mothballs, the only time she ever liked the mothballs), and the singing. She doesn't remember being frightened so much as being held.

Around the corner, the shop had lost most of its crockery. Plates and bowls had come off the open shelves and smashed across the floor, the bottles behind the counter had fallen, and the new Ebisu bamboo had dropped from above the till. Hiroshi checked the gas first, then came running round the corner in his pyjamas and an anorak with a torch, and Yumiko followed with Kenta wrapped in a blanket. The family sat together at Fumiko's in the grey early light, five of them under one kotatsu quilt, waiting for the power to come back and for the radio to say something.

When the television came on, it was showing Kobe. Mizuki was too young to understand it, and the adults moved her away from the screen, but she remembers her mother's hand over her mouth, and her father very still. More than six thousand people died in the earthquake, most of them in Kobe and the towns along the coast, and hundreds of thousands lost their homes. The elevated expressway had fallen on its side; there were fires in the old wooden neighbourhoods. The family didn't say much about it in front of the children, then or later.

Hiroshi's cousin Makoto lived in Kobe, in a flat in Higashinada ward near the sea, with his wife Keiko and their son Daisuke, who was nine. The phone lines to Kobe were jammed all day; Hiroshi tried the number again and again from the shop phone and then from the green public phone outside the station, and never got through. Early the next morning, he loaded the shop's little white delivery van with bottled water, blankets, a gas cartridge stove and two trays of rice balls that Yumiko and Fumiko had made in the night, and drove west. The trains between Osaka and Kobe weren't running and the roads were solid with traffic. He got as far as Ashiya, left the van in a school car park, and walked the rest of the way with a rucksack on his back. He was gone for two days. Mizuki asked where he was every hour. Fumiko told her he'd gone to help, "because that's what you do", and gave her a crane to hold.

He came back on the evening of the 19th with Makoto, Keiko and Daisuke in the van. Their building was standing but badly cracked, and they couldn't live in it. They had a suitcase, a few bags, and Daisuke's school rucksack, and that was all.

### The Kobe family at Fumiko's

The Tanakas' two rooms above the shop could barely hold the Tanakas, so Fumiko, who wasn't related to Makoto's family by blood at all, simply opened her door. "I have a spare room and a big pot," she said. "That's enough." She moved her own futon into the kotatsu room and gave them the back room, and for the next two months, until late March, three extra people lived in a house built for one. It was Osaka generosity at its plainest, practical and unfussy, and Mizuki has never forgotten it. When she talks now about what a good neighbour looks like, this is what she means.

Makoto was a quiet man who worked for a shipping company at Kobe port, and he spent most of those weeks travelling back and forth on whatever trains and buses ran, sorting out the flat, the insurance, his work. Keiko, a cheerful, round-faced woman with a laugh that came back slowly, helped Yumiko in the shop at lunchtime, because she said she'd go mad sitting still; the regulars liked her at once, and she learned to make a passable modan-yaki by February. Hiroshi, who said almost nothing about any of it, made the Kobe family's dinner every night at the shop and carried it round the corner on a tray with a cloth over it.

Daisuke was the hardest. He was a skinny, serious boy with a bowl haircut and glasses, and in those first weeks he barely spoke. He'd been temporarily enrolled at the local elementary school in Abeno, the one Mizuki would start at two years later, and every morning he walked there in borrowed shoes and every afternoon he walked back to Fumiko's and sat in the corner. Mizuki, who had been told to be kind and quiet, was kind and very loud. She showed him the rabbits through the kindergarten fence. She told him the noppera-bō story, getting the ending wrong. She offered him her Sailor Mercury sticker. He looked at her as if she were weather.

The first time he laughed was on 3 February, Setsubun, the bean-throwing festival. Hiroshi came round to Fumiko's after lunch service wearing the cardboard demon mask, red-faced with yellow horns, and the children threw roasted soybeans at him, shouting "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!" ("Demons out! Luck in!"). Hiroshi, in his mask, did his usual slow, dignified, very convincing fall across the tatami, and then lay there, and then said, without moving, in his flat voice, "The demon would like a cup of tea." Daisuke laughed out loud, a snort he tried to stop and couldn't, and Keiko, in the kitchen doorway, had to turn away. Afterwards they all ate ehōmaki, the thick sushi roll eaten whole and in silence facing the year's lucky direction, and Mizuki ruined everyone's luck, as she did every year, by giggling.

### The Super Famicom

Daisuke had brought almost nothing from Kobe, but in his rucksack was the one thing he had insisted on carrying out of the flat: his Super Famicom, wrapped in a school jumper, with two controllers and three cartridges. Fumiko let him plug it into her old television on the second night, and from then on, in the evenings, the little wooden house filled with bleeps and bright music.

The cartridges were Super Mario World, the Super Famicom's launch game, with its dinosaur Yoshi and its secret exits; Super Mario Kart; and Seiken Densetsu 2, the action role-playing game known in English as Secret of Mana, which had come out in the summer of 1993, with a snowy forest and a flying fortress and music that Mizuki would hum for the rest of her life.

Mizuki sat behind him on the tatami for hours. At first he ignored her. Then he began explaining things quietly, almost to himself: which pipes you could go down, where the hidden blocks were. One evening in February, without a word, he handed her the second controller and let her be Luigi. She ran straight into the first enemy. He showed her again. And again. When she finally jumped over it she screamed so loudly that Fumiko dropped a ladle.

In those patient evenings, a frightened boy from Kobe and a bossy girl from Abeno became something like brother and sister, and Daisuke started talking again. He talked about the game first, then about school, then, once, very quietly, about the morning of the earthquake, and Mizuki, who was nearly five, listened and said nothing, which was the most grown-up thing she did that year. Fumiko watched from the kitchen and didn't say anything either, which was how she said the most.

Mizuki loved Super Mario World's ghost houses, the dark haunted levels with the shy Boo ghosts that cover their faces when you look at them and creep up when you turn away. "Like the noppera-bō," she said, "but polite." They were spooky, not scary, and spooky-not-scary became her favourite kind of anything. She loved Yoshi and was outraged whenever she fell off him. In Super Mario Kart she always chose Princess Peach, lost every race by a lap, and declared herself the winner on style. And she didn't really play Seiken Densetsu 2 at all yet, it was too hard, but she sat behind Daisuke while he did, reading the hiragana in the text boxes out loud a beat before he could, and she fell in love with the music. When the snowy forest theme came on she would stop talking, which Daisuke said was the best thing about the game.

Even Fumiko played, once. Daisuke talked her into Super Mario Kart one Sunday in March, and she held the controller at arm's length as if it might bite, steered with her whole body, drove backwards for most of a lap and said "ara, ara" every time she hit a wall. She came eighth of eight. She said it was a very rude game and that in her day you could at least see the tram coming. Then she asked for another go.

That is where Mizuki's love of 16-bit games begins: bright colours, simple controls, a cartridge you blow on if it doesn't work, music that loops in your head forever. Her verdict on most new games, "too long, too loud, too many menus", is really about those evenings: the warm kotatsu, the bleeps, and a quiet boy handing her a controller.

### Spring 1995: cranes for Kobe, and a crane in the dark

For several weeks after the earthquake Mizuki didn't like the dark. She'd never minded it before, but now, at bedtime, she wanted the light left on, and if she woke in the night she called out. Fumiko, who was sleeping beside her in the kotatsu room, didn't tell her not to be silly. One night in February she turned on the small lamp, got out the tin of origami paper, and folded a crane with Mizuki watching. Then she said, "Now the trick," turned the lamp off, and folded another in the dark, talking her through it: corner to corner, open the pocket, squash it flat, the long fold up the middle like a bird's belly. When the lamp came back on there was a crane on the quilt. Then she gave Mizuki a square and turned the lamp off again.

It took a week of nights. The first dark cranes were shapeless lumps. But by early March Mizuki could fold one in the dark, slowly, with her tongue out, and when the light came on there it was, a little wonky, standing on its own. "There," said Fumiko. "Now the dark is for something." After that Mizuki didn't mind the dark much. She still folds a crane, sometimes, with the light off in her Pyrmont flat when she can't sleep, and she has never told anyone in Sydney why.

That same spring, people all over Japan were folding paper cranes for Kobe, strings of a thousand cranes, senbazuru, for healing and hope, and Kawabe-sensei's class folded theirs too. The Chick Class managed a few hundred between them, most of them by Kawabe-sensei and the mothers, and Mizuki, who could now fold a proper crane, folded forty-one, more than any other child, and told everyone the number for a month. They were strung on threads and sent to a school in Kobe. On Tanabata that July, her wish on the bamboo, written by Kawabe-sensei but dictated very carefully, was "I hope Kobe gets better and Dai-nii comes to visit."

Before he went, Daisuke got Hinamatsuri, the girls' festival on 3 March, which he found baffling. Fumiko set out her own old hina dolls on a red cloth, a small set of the emperor and empress and three court ladies that had survived from before the war, and they ate pink-and-green diamond rice cakes and sweet white sake. Mizuki explained to Daisuke, at length, who every doll was and which one she would be (the empress) and which one he would be (a court musician, at the back). He agreed, which was how she knew he'd got better.

In the last week of March 1995 Makoto found a rented flat in Tarumi, in the west of Kobe, while their building was repaired, and the family went home, partly by train and partly on replacement buses where the lines were still broken. Keiko cried and hugged Fumiko at the station barrier; Makoto bowed very low to Hiroshi, who bowed back and said only, "Come and eat." Daisuke shook Mizuki's hand like a grown man, then came back down the steps to tell her something: he'd left the Super Famicom at Fumiko's, with all three cartridges in a biscuit tin. "So the little ones have something to do," he said. He said he'd come back for it. He never did, and nobody ever asked him to. It stayed under Fumiko's television for years, the heart of every weekend at her house, and it's the reason Mizuki, and later Kenta, grew up on Super Famicom games. Daisuke still calls her "the audience". She still calls it his Super Famicom, though it has been hers ever since.

### 1995 in Osaka

The rest of 1995 was a strange year in Osaka, and Mizuki remembers it in pieces, the way small children remember everything.

She remembers the news being on all the time that spring. In March there was the gas attack on the Tokyo subway, and the adults talked about it in low voices across the counter and stopped when she came near. She understood only that the grown-ups were worried twice in one winter, and that her mother hugged her harder than usual at the kindergarten gate.

She remembers that in April Osaka elected a comedian, Knock Yokoyama, as its governor, and the regulars at the counter found this so funny and so Osaka that they talked about nothing else for a week. "Only in Osaka," said the taxi driver, with enormous pride.

She remembers "Wow War Tonight", the dance song that came out that spring by H Jungle with t, which was Hamada, the tall, sharp-tongued half of the comedy duo Downtown, singing over a Tetsuya Komuro beat. A comedian from the Kansai side of the bay with a number one hit was another point of local pride, and Yumiko sang it while doing the accounts, and the regulars sang the chorus after their second beer, and Mizuki, five, did a dance to it on a chair that she maintains was excellent. It was the first pop song she ever knew all the words to that wasn't from a cartoon.

She remembers the Hanshin Tigers losing, again. They finished bottom of the Central League in 1995, and Hiroshi watched every game anyway on the little TV in the corner of the shop, turning okonomiyaki with his eyes on the screen and saying nothing except, after a bad inning, a quiet "mada mada", "not yet, not yet", which was his whole philosophy of the Tigers and, Mizuki now thinks, of life. Then something odd happened. The Orix BlueWave, the Pacific League team that played in Kobe, wore a patch on their sleeves that season that said "Ganbarō KOBE", "Hang in there, Kobe", and with their young outfielder Ichiro they won the Pacific League pennant. Hiroshi, a Tigers man to his bones, cheered for them that autumn. It was the only time Mizuki ever saw him cheer for anyone else. "It was for Kobe," he said, when Yumiko teased him. "It doesn't count."

She remembers a family day out on a Wednesday in early summer, the shop's day off, to the Kaiyukan aquarium at Tempozan by the bay, which had opened in 1990, the year she was born. She stood with her nose on the glass of the huge central tank for most of an hour while the whale shark went round and round, slow and spotted and as big as a bus. Hiroshi, beside her, said, "Imagine the okonomiyaki." Yumiko hit him. Mizuki said, sternly, "Papa, he's not for eating," and then, two tanks later, at the crabs: "Those ones are." Her love of aquariums, begun at that same tank when she was one, was sealed there, and so was a running family joke in which Hiroshi assesses every sea creature as an ingredient.

She remembers, less happily, a day in June when she lost her yellow kindergarten hat on the Midosuji line coming home from Kuromon with Fumiko. She'd taken it off to wave it at the train window and left it on the seat. She cried all the way from Tennoji station to the shop, not because of the hat, but because Fumiko's stitched name was in it. Yumiko phoned the lost property office the next morning. It was there, of course, folded neatly, with a note; this was Japan. Mizuki was so relieved that she bowed to the man at the office counter until Yumiko pulled her away. It's one of her first proofs that lost things in Japan come back, which made her second month in Sydney, years later, so much harder.

She remembers Kenta, nearly two by the autumn, following her everywhere and copying everything: her tsukkomi chop, her "irasshaimase", and her Sailor Moon pose, which he did wrong, with the wrong hand, and refused to be corrected about. She remembers moving up in April 1995 to the middle year at kindergarten, the nenchū class, Usagi-gumi, the Rabbit Class, which she felt was named after her because of the rabbits. Kawabe-sensei moved up with them, which Mizuki took as a personal favour.

She remembers chickenpox. In November 1995 half the Rabbit Class came down with it, and so did Mizuki, and then, a fortnight later, Kenta. The Japanese word for chickenpox is mizubōsō, and when Mizuki found out that the disease had her name in it ("mizu"), she was furious, and spent a week covered in calamine lotion insisting it was "Kenta's disease". She spent ten itchy days under Fumiko's kotatsu playing Super Mario World badly and being given mikan on demand, and missed the rehearsals for the end-of-year performance.

That performance, the happyōkai in December, was her first stage disaster. The Rabbit Class did a play of "Momotarō", the peach boy who goes to fight the demons with a dog, a monkey and a pheasant, and Mizuki, back from the chickenpox with two faint spots on her chin, had been given the pheasant. She had one line. In front of the whole hall, with her family in the third row and Hiroshi holding the Nikon, she forgot it completely. She stood there in her paper pheasant hat. The hall went quiet. Then, because she was an Osaka child and a silence is a gap that has to be filled, she said, very loudly, "I'm a pheasant. I forgot. Sorry," and flapped. The parents laughed so hard the play stopped. Kawabe-sensei had to put her hand on the piano. Mizuki, at the time, was mortified and cried in the toilet afterwards. Within a week, at the counter, it was her best story, with an ochi. It taught her something she still believes: if you forget your line, say so, with confidence, and you'll get a bigger laugh than the line would have.

And in November there was APEC, the big summit of Asia-Pacific leaders, held in Osaka, which mostly meant police everywhere, roads closed and the cabbage delivery arriving two hours late. Hiroshi took it personally. "All those presidents," he said, "and not one of them has considered the cabbage."

### January 1996: a minute at 5:46, and a rumour about pocket monsters

New Year 1996 went the way New Year always went, with the great cleaning, soba, the Kōhaku, Mizuki asleep before the bells, osechi and round mochi in white miso. At Sumiyoshi Taisha, for the first time, she insisted on climbing the steep red drum bridge by herself, aged five and three-quarters, refused every hand, got to the very top, looked down, and froze. She stood there in her red duffle coat with a queue building behind her until Hiroshi climbed up, said nothing at all, and carried her down the other side on his shoulders. She's told this story as a triumph ever since. "I climbed it," she says. "Getting down is a separate event."

On 17 January 1996, a year to the minute after the earthquake, the family stood in the shop at 5:46 in the morning, in the dark, with the shutters down and the hotplate cold, for a minute's silence. Mizuki, who was usually asleep at that hour and had asked to be woken, stood between her parents. Halfway through the minute she reached out, without being asked and without anyone seeing her do it, and took Fumiko's hand. Fumiko held on. Afterwards nobody said anything, and Hiroshi lit the hotplate, and the day began. Mizuki rarely talks about the earthquake. When it comes up she goes quiet and serious and says only that her grandmother sang to her in the dark. It's why she keeps a torch and bottled water in her flat in Sydney without anyone telling her to, why she respects how carefully Japan prepares for disasters, and why she goes very still when earthquake news comes on.

By early 1996 Mizuki was nearly six: the oldest in the Rabbit Class, the shop's greeter, Kenta's manager, Fumiko's Saturday shadow, and a Super Mario World player who still fell into most of the pits. She could fold a crane in the dark, tell three yokai stories with the right pauses, and explain to any visitor why natto was a mistake.

And at the counter, and in the sandpit, there was a rumour. Shōta, whose brother read CoroCoro Comic, reported that there was a new Game Boy game coming at the end of February called Pocket Monsters, where you caught little monsters and kept them in balls in your pocket, and there were a hundred and fifty of them, and you could swap them with your friends through a cable. Mizuki didn't believe the bit about the cable. But the idea of catching little monsters and keeping them seemed to her, after a winter of yokai stories, the best idea anyone had ever had: it was Fumiko's world, in a box you could carry.

Pocket Monsters Red and Green would come out on 27 February 1996. Her sixth birthday was on 10 April. She did the arithmetic, and with great strategic care began mentioning both facts to her parents and to Fumiko, separately, several times a day.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:**
  - **Being "the senior"**: born 10 April, among the oldest in her year, and she has used it ever since.
  - **Yokai stories, especially the noppera-bō**: the best ochi in folklore; frightened and safe at once; yokai are lonely things that want to be noticed (Fumiko).
  - **Origami, and a crane folded in the dark**: Fumiko taught her after the earthquake so "the dark is for something"; she still does it when she can't sleep.
  - **Whistling two notes at trams**: her grandfather Shigeru's habit, passed on by Fumiko.
  - **Sailor Mercury**: the clever, shy "water girl"; the pen from Fumiko; "Ami's First Love" on a cinema screen with her mother.
  - **"In the name of the moon, I'll punish you!"**: aimed at Kenta from 1994, at Scott now.
  - **Outfits that say something**: the leopard-print auntie and the tiger-print scarf ("I am small, but I am in charge").
  - **Anpanman ("he makes sure you've eaten")**, and Baikinman, who tries hard and always loses.
  - **Funny-sad films**: Pom Poko's losing tanuki.
  - **Super Famicom games**: ghost houses ("spooky, not scary"), Yoshi, Peach "on style", the Seiken Densetsu 2 snow music; cartridges, not menus. Because of Daisuke's patience.
  - **Seafood**: Kuromon scallops; sea urchin "like the sea being nice to you".
  - **Broken okonomiyaki, takoyaki lava, oden daikon, konbini pudding, mikan by the dozen**.
  - **Summer festivals**: the goldfish yukata, strawberry shaved ice, Ramune she couldn't open.
  - **Aquariums**: the Kaiyukan whale shark, and her father judging every sea creature as an ingredient.
  - **"Wow War Tonight", enka into a towel, "Sanpo" from Totoro**: her first songs by heart.
  - **Japan's lost property offices**: her yellow hat came back, folded, with a note.
  - **Owning a forgotten line**: the pheasant taught her the honest version gets the bigger laugh.
- **Dislikes:**
  - **Natto**: "smells like Kenta's socks"; "Tokyo glue"; "then let it save someone else".
  - **Beni shōga** (picked out into a pink pile), **green peppers** (Shin-chan agrees) and **umeboshi**.
  - **Wasabi**: mistaken for bean paste once; she never trusts anything green and smooth.
  - **Mothballs**: "a ghost's wardrobe" (except once, on Fumiko's cardigan, in the dark).
  - **Brown clothes ("for old men and tanuki") and itchy jumpers**.
  - **Steep arched bridges**: "getting down is a separate event".
  - **Humid Osaka summers**.
  - **Being praised in front of people**: she hid in her smock then and still goes quiet at compliments.
  - **Chickenpox**, because it has her name in it (mizubōsō).
  - **The dark**, for a few weeks in 1995, until the crane.
