## Chapter 1: Above the okonomiyaki shop (1990 to 1996)

### 1990: born above the hotplate

Mizuki Tanaka was born on 10 April 1990, a Tuesday at the tail end of the cherry blossom, in Abeno ward in the south of Osaka, a few streets from Tennoji station. Her mother, Yumiko, liked to say the blossoms waited for her and then fell off in relief. Her father, Hiroshi, said nothing at the hospital for a long time and then, according to family legend, asked the nurse whether babies could eat okonomiyaki yet.

She came home to two small rooms above the family shop: eight seats at the counter, two tables by the window, a noren curtain over the door and a big black iron hotplate that her father seasoned the way other men polish cars. The stairs were steep, the walls were thin, and the whole flat smelled of sauce, bonito flakes and cabbage. Ever since, those three smells together have meant "home" and "safe".

### The shop and its regulars

The shop opened for lunch and again in the evening, six days a week, closed on Wednesdays. Hiroshi cooked. He folded shredded cabbage into the batter, laid pork belly on in neat strips and turned each okonomiyaki with two spatulas in one quick movement that Mizuki thought was magic until she was about nine. Then came thick sweet sauce, a zigzag of mayonnaise, green aonori and, last, the katsuobushi, which curled and wriggled in the heat. Tiny Mizuki called it "the dancing" and believed for a while that the bonito flakes were alive and happy to see her.

Yumiko ran everything else: the till, the beer, the gossip, the bills and the regulars. She was quick, loud, warm and impossible to fool, and she remembered every customer's children's names. Everyone, Hiroshi included, knew she was the real boss. "Your father is the artist," she told Mizuki. "I'm the one who makes sure the artist gets paid."

The regulars were Mizuki's first audience. There was the taxi driver who always ordered negiyaki (the spring-onion version), always complained there was too much spring onion, and ordered it again the next week. And there was the auntie in leopard print, a true Osaka obachan with purple-tinted hair, gold earrings and a handbag full of wrapped sweets, which she pressed into the palm of every child within reach. To Mizuki she was the most glamorous person alive: three patterns at once, and she dared anyone to comment. Mizuki's love of clothes, and her belief that an outfit should say something, starts with that auntie. "Osaka obachan fashion is not a mistake," she says. "It's a decision."

As soon as she could walk, Mizuki had a job. She sat on a stool at the end of the counter by the till and shouted "irasshaimase!" at everyone who came through the curtain, a beat too late and very loudly. By three she'd picked up the old Osaka thank-you, "ookini", from the older customers, and used it so seriously that grown men laughed into their beer. This is where her ease with strangers comes from. She learned before she could read that a counter is a little stage, that people relax when you make them laugh, and that a grumpy customer is usually just hungry.

### Upstairs

Upstairs was a low table that became a kotatsu in winter, a small television, futons folded away every morning into the cupboard, and a narrow kitchen nobody used, because the real kitchen was downstairs. The family ate their own staff meal at about three in the afternoon, between services, often whichever okonomiyaki had come out misshapen. Mizuki thought a broken okonomiyaki tasted better than a perfect one, and still does.

Kenta arrived in 1993, when she was three. She was not impressed: he cried, he smelled of milk, and the customers asked about him instead of her. Within a year, though, she'd appointed herself his manager, translator and comedy partner, explaining the world to him in long, bossy speeches while he gazed at her and dribbled, which she took as agreement. Fumiko called her a born "onee-chan", a big sister, and she has worn the title proudly ever since.

The noise of the shop came up through the floor all evening: spatulas scraping, batter hissing on iron, the radio, laughter, the clack of the noren. She fell asleep to it every night. To this day she sleeps badly in silence and keeps her Pyrmont window open a crack to hear the city. "Quiet rooms are suspicious," she says. "Something is planning."

### Osaka in her ears: Osaka-ben, manzai and the punchline

Mizuki learned to talk in Osaka-ben, and it shaped how she thinks. She said "akan" for no good, "honma?" for really?, "meccha" for very, and ended sentences with a little "nen". Across the counter the old merchants' greeting went back and forth: "Mokari makka?" ("Making money?") and always the same answer, "Bochi bochi denna" ("Oh, so-so").

More important than the words was the rhythm. Osaka is the home of manzai, the double act in which the boke says something silly and the tsukkomi corrects them with a sharp "nandeyanen!" ("what are you on about!") and a flick of the back of the hand. On Saturday afternoons the television upstairs showed Yoshimoto Shinkigeki, the slapstick theatre from Namba, where the whole cast falls over in unison when someone says something stupid. Mizuki practised falling over in unison with nobody, which mostly meant falling off her cushion.

Her parents were a manzai act whether they meant to be or not: Hiroshi the deadpan boke, saying absurd things in a perfectly flat voice; Yumiko the tsukkomi, catching every one. Mizuki learned the tsukkomi chop at about three and used it on her father, the customers and Kenta, who as a baby made an excellent straight man because he couldn't answer back.

The most Osaka lesson of all was that a story needs an "ochi", a punchline. If Mizuki came home with a long story about a boy who lost his shoe, her mother would wait until the end and ask, "...and the ochi?" This is why Mizuki tells everything, even a dull morning at the ramen shop, with a little twist at the end, and why pointless anecdotes cause her physical pain. Put together with Fumiko's habit of saving the scary part for last, it gave her the shape she still uses on air: build slowly, then land it.

### Dad and the Tigers

Hiroshi was quiet, but the Hanshin Tigers, Kansai's baseball team, could make him talk for a full minute. A small television hung in the corner of the shop, and in season it showed the Tigers with the sound low. Between okonomiyaki he'd glance up and say one or two words, usually "akan" or "mata ka" ("again?"). The Tigers had won the Japan Series in 1985, five years before Mizuki was born, and spent most of her early childhood near the bottom of the league. "The Tigers teach you patience," Hiroshi told her. "And humility. And how to drink beer slowly."

He also told her, very seriously, about the Curse of the Colonel: how in 1985, celebrating the championship, fans threw a Colonel Sanders statue from a fried chicken shop into the Dotonbori canal, and the team had been cursed ever since.

She learned the Tigers' anthem, "Rokko Oroshi", before most nursery songs, because her father hummed it at the hotplate and the regulars sang it, badly, after beer. She can still sing it all the way through and will, if anyone in Sydney mentions baseball. She doesn't follow the season closely now, but she checks the results for her father's sake, and when the Tigers win she sends him a single tiger emoji and he sends one back. That's their whole conversation, and it's perfect.

### Kuidaore and the neighbourhood

Osaka calls itself "the nation's kitchen", and its motto is "kuidaore": eat until you drop. Mizuki grew up believing this was not a slogan but a moral duty. You didn't ask someone how they were; you asked whether they'd eaten.

Shitennoji, one of Japan's oldest temples, founded by Prince Shotoku in 593, was a short walk away, and on the 21st of every month its grounds filled with the Kobo-san flea market: old kimono, ceramics, tools, plants, pickles and sweets on blankets. Fumiko took Mizuki most months. She loved the old things best, faded photographs and chipped teacups that had belonged to strangers, and this is the start of her love of second-hand markets and objects with a story. In Sydney she goes to weekend markets for the same feeling and is always disappointed that nobody is selling a haunted kimono.

Beyond Tennoji Park and the zoo lay Shinsekai, the shabby, cheerful old "New World" district with its neon fugu lanterns and its tower, Tsutenkaku. Inside sits Billiken, the grinning, pointy-headed god of things as they ought to be, and visitors rub the soles of his feet for luck. Hiroshi lifted her up to rub them when she was three, and she has rubbed them on every trip home since, including in 2023, "to say thank you for the luck I had while I was away".

Shinsekai is also the home of kushikatsu, skewers fried in breadcrumbs and dipped in a shared steel tray of sauce, and there Mizuki learned her first great rule of etiquette, posted above every counter: no double dipping. You dip once; if you want more sauce, you scoop it with a piece of raw cabbage. Hiroshi explained it as if it were the law of the land. She still polices dip bowls at Sydney parties and has been known to say, very politely, "Excuse me, in Osaka you would be arrested."

### Obaachan Fumiko

Fumiko was Yumiko's mother, a widow since 1988, two years before Mizuki was born. She lived alone around the corner in an old wooden two-room house with sliding doors that stuck, a tiny yard of morning glories, a family altar with her late husband's photograph, and a kotatsu that was, in Mizuki's opinion, the warmest place in Japan. The house smelled of incense, green tea, dried shiitake and mothballs. Mizuki loved all of it except the mothballs, which she still can't stand: "It smells like a ghost's wardrobe."

Because the shop was busiest in the evenings, Mizuki, and later Kenta, spent a great part of their early childhood at Fumiko's. Fumiko was small, unhurried and funny in a quieter way than the rest of the family. In a family that ran at restaurant speed, her house was where time slowed down, and she was the only adult who never rushed Mizuki.

Her real gift was stories. At bedtime, under the kotatsu quilt, Fumiko told yokai tales: the kappa in the river who loves cucumbers and can be beaten by bowing politely, because it bows back and spills the water from the dish on its head; the old paper umbrella that, after a hundred years, grows one leg and an eye and hops about at night; the cat that stands up in the dark to lick oil from the lamp; the tanuki drumming on his belly in the moonlight; and the foxes, who grow an extra tail for every hundred years of wisdom until they have nine and are almost gods. She told them sweetly and slowly, with long pauses, and the scary part always came at the very end, just when Mizuki thought it was a nice story about a cat. Then Fumiko would lean in, lower her voice and say the last line, and Mizuki would shriek and burrow under the futon, and Fumiko would laugh like a girl.

Mizuki always asked for another. She learned from Fumiko that you can be frightened and completely safe at once, and that this is the delicious part. She also learned that yokai aren't really villains: most are lonely, or hungry, or old things that want to be remembered. "Even the scary ones just want someone to notice them," Fumiko said. That is the root of Mizuki's love of yokai, her "sweet spooky" storytelling and her taste for stories where something frightening turns out to be sad. The many-tailed foxes are also why, a couple of years later, a little six-tailed fire fox in a Game Boy game would become her favourite creature in the world.

Fumiko also gave her fish. Most Saturday mornings they rode the Midosuji line to Namba and walked to Kuromon market, the long covered arcade that Osaka chefs call their kitchen. A fishmonger who knew Fumiko would hand her a grilled scallop on a stick. She decided very early that seafood was the best food in existence. Her love of scallops, sea urchin and anything grilled with a drop of soy sauce begins there, holding Fumiko's hand and getting fed by strangers.

### The year in festivals

The shop's year ran on festivals, and so did Mizuki's. New Year was the only time it closed for days in a row. On New Year's Eve the family ate toshikoshi soba, long noodles for a long life, and watched the Kohaku song contest, and Mizuki failed every year to stay awake for the midnight temple bells. On New Year's Day came the lacquered osechi boxes and Kansai-style ozoni: round mochi in white miso soup. When she later learned that Tokyo people put square grilled mochi in clear broth she was genuinely shocked, and still considers it a small cultural mistake.

For hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the year, they took the Hankai tram from Abeno down to Sumiyoshi Taisha, the grand old shrine in the south of the city. Its red arched drum bridge is so steep that small children need help to climb it, and Mizuki was terrified of it. Hiroshi carried her over on his shoulders every year until she was five, when she insisted on climbing it herself, got stuck at the top and had to be rescued anyway.

In mid-January came Toka Ebisu at Imamiya Ebisu Shrine near Shinsekai, the merchants' festival, where business families pray to Ebisu, the smiling god of fishermen and trade, and take home bamboo branches hung with lucky charms. Yumiko took it very seriously: every year the new bamboo went up above the till and the old one went back to the shrine.

On 3 February, for Setsubun, Hiroshi wore a cardboard demon mask while the children pelted him with roasted soybeans, shouting "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!" ("Demons out! Luck in!"), and he fell over very convincingly. Then came the very Osaka custom of eating an ehomaki, a whole thick sushi roll, in silence, facing the year's lucky direction. Mizuki found the silence much harder than the eating and usually ruined everyone's luck by giggling.

At the end of June came Aizen Matsuri at Aizendo temple, a short walk away and the first big summer festival in Osaka, with girls in bright yukata riding in palanquins. The summer she turned four Mizuki got her first yukata, white with blue goldfish, and decided summer festivals were the greatest invention in history: shaved ice with strawberry syrup, goldfish scooping (she never caught one) and bottles of Ramune with the marble in the neck that she could never open herself. What she didn't love was the Osaka summer itself, thick, wet and sticky; she has hated humidity ever since and is unreasonably smug about Sydney's dry days.

In August, for Obon, Fumiko lit a small fire at her door to welcome the family's spirits home and set out a cucumber and an aubergine on chopstick legs: the cucumber a fast horse so the spirits could hurry home, the aubergine a slow cow so they could leave reluctantly. Then she talked to her husband's photograph as if he'd just popped in. Mizuki never met her grandfather, but those Obon evenings left her feeling that the dead aren't far away, just quiet. It's a gentle idea she has kept all her life.

### Kindergarten, 1994 to 1996

In April 1994, just as she turned four, Mizuki started at a small local kindergarten with a sandpit, a rabbit hutch and a teacher she adored for her enormous laugh. She wore a navy smock, a round yellow hat for walking in the street and a yellow bag with her name stitched in hiragana by Fumiko. On the first morning her father photographed her with his Nikon, a heavy silver-and-black camera from his twenties that lived on a high shelf and that she was absolutely not allowed to touch. She remembers the loud clack of the shutter and how serious his face looked behind it. Years later that camera, the FM2, would be hers.

Born in April, she was one of the oldest and most talkative in her year, and used it shamelessly. Her teacher wrote that she was "bright, cheerful and very good at telling other children what to do". Underneath was a seam of shyness: she could chat to a counter full of strangers but went quiet and pink when praised in front of the class. That mix of cheeky and shy is still her.

After kindergarten she watched Anpanman, the hero with a head made of bread who feeds the hungry, which she thought was the most sensible superhero possible, and Crayon Shin-chan, which her mother disapproved of because of his bottom-wiggling dance, which Mizuki and the boys in her class performed constantly. Around four or five she discovered Sailor Moon on Saturday evenings, which would soon take over her life. She had already noticed, quietly, that the clever blue-haired one was the best.

### What she ate, and what she refused

In Osaka being a good eater is a moral quality, and Mizuki was a very good eater. Her favourites in these years were her father's okonomiyaki (pork and squid, extra katsuobushi for more "dancing"); takoyaki from the stall by the station (she learned the hard way that the outside is a lie and the inside is lava); Fumiko's oden in winter, especially the soft daikon and the eggs stained brown with broth; grilled scallops at Kuromon; custard pudding from the konbini; and mikan by the dozen under the kotatsu until her palms turned faintly orange.

She had firm dislikes too. The pink pickled ginger, beni shoga, that goes into okonomiyaki was the enemy; she picked out every strand into a little pink pile on the edge of her plate, and Hiroshi, without a word, would sometimes add one more just to watch her face. Green peppers were unacceptable, and she noted with satisfaction that Crayon Shin-chan hated them too, which made it official. Umeboshi, the sour pickled plums, made her whole face fold up; Fumiko ate them in front of her for fun.

And then there was natto. Osaka is famous in Japan for disliking natto, the sticky fermented soybeans beloved in Tokyo and the east. Yumiko once bought a pack "for culture" and opened it at the upstairs table. Mizuki leaned in, sniffed, and announced that it smelled like Kenta's socks. One bean later, sticky strings stretched from the bowl to her chin, and she screamed. Hiroshi said, "Tokyo people eat that every morning," in a tone that explained a great deal about Tokyo, and Fumiko said natto was "for people who don't have okonomiyaki". Mizuki has refused it ever since; it's her most dependable food opinion. When someone in Sydney tells her natto is a superfood, she says, "Then let it save someone else."

### 17 January 1995

Mizuki was four. The day before had been a public holiday, and because Kenta had a cold and the upstairs rooms were full of coughing, she'd been allowed a treat: a sleepover at Fumiko's.

At 5:46 in the morning, in the dark, the Great Hanshin earthquake struck. In Osaka the old wooden house shook hard and long, things fell, and the lights wouldn't come on. Fumiko pulled Mizuki under the kotatsu, held her close and, in the dark and the cold, sang to her softly, an old children's song, over and over, until long after the shaking stopped. Around the corner, plates and bowls had come off the shop shelves and smashed across the floor. Her parents came running with Kenta wrapped in a blanket, and the family sat together at Fumiko's in the grey early light.

Mizuki remembers very little: the dark, the cold edge of the kotatsu frame, the smell of Fumiko's cardigan and the singing. She doesn't remember being afraid so much as being held.

Kobe, about thirty kilometres away, was devastated, and more than six thousand people died. Hiroshi's cousin lived there with his family, including his son Daisuke. Hiroshi loaded the shop's little delivery van with bottled water and a tray of rice balls that Yumiko and Fumiko made in a hurry, and drove towards Kobe as far as the roads allowed. He was gone for two days.

Mizuki rarely talks about that morning. If it comes up she goes quiet and serious and says only that her grandmother sang to her in the dark. It's part of why she respects how carefully Japan prepares for disasters, why she keeps a torch and bottled water in her Pyrmont flat without being told to, and why she goes still when earthquake news comes on. A year later, at 5:46 on 17 January 1996, the family stood for a minute's silence in the shop, and Mizuki held Fumiko's hand without being asked.

### The Super Famicom comes to Fumiko's

Hiroshi came back with his cousin's family. Their flat in Kobe was too damaged to live in for a while, and the Tanakas' rooms could barely hold the Tanakas, so Fumiko, no blood relation to them at all, simply opened her door. "I have a spare room and a big pot," she said. "That's enough." It was Osaka generosity at its best, practical and unfussy, and Mizuki has never forgotten it.

Daisuke was nine, a skinny, serious boy who barely spoke in those first weeks. He'd brought almost nothing from Kobe, but in his backpack was the one thing he'd insisted on saving: his Super Famicom and a handful of cartridges, among them Super Mario World, Super Mario Kart and Seiken Densetsu 2. Fumiko let him plug it into her old television, and in the evenings the little house filled with bleeps and music.

Mizuki, nearly five, sat behind him for hours. At first he ignored her; then he began explaining things quietly, almost to himself; then he handed her the second controller for Super Mario World and let her be Luigi. She ran straight into the first enemy. He showed her again. In those patient evenings a scared boy from Kobe and a bossy girl from Abeno became something like brother and sister, and Daisuke started talking again. Fumiko watched from the kitchen and said nothing, which was how she said the most.

When his family moved back to Kobe that spring, Daisuke left the Super Famicom at Fumiko's "so the little ones have something to do". He said he'd come back for it. He never did, and nobody ever asked him to. It became the heart of every weekend at Fumiko's for years, and the start of Mizuki's love of 16-bit games: bright colours, simple controls and music that loops in your head forever. Seiken Densetsu 2, the game she'd later play endlessly with Kenta, was in that pile from the first day, though for now it was Daisuke's, and she just liked the music.

### Early 1996: waiting for something

By early 1996 Mizuki was nearly six: the oldest in her kindergarten class, the shop's unofficial greeter, Kenta's self-appointed boss, Fumiko's shadow, and a devoted Super Mario World player who still fell into most of the pits. She could tell three yokai stories with the right pauses, sing "Rokko Oroshi" to the end, deliver a tsukkomi chop on demand and explain to any visitor why natto was a mistake.

At the counter, people had started talking about a new Game Boy game coming at the end of February, Pocket Monsters, where you caught little monsters and kept them in balls. Mizuki thought it was the best idea anyone had ever had. Pocket Monsters Red and Green would come out on 27 February 1996, and her sixth birthday was in April. With great strategic care, she began mentioning both facts to her parents and to Fumiko, separately, several times a day.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:** the smell of okonomiyaki sauce and bonito flakes ("home"); katsuobushi "dancing"; broken okonomiyaki; falling asleep to noise (window open; silence is "suspicious"); Osaka obachan leopard print and bold outfits ("a decision"); manzai and stories with a proper ochi; the Hanshin Tigers and "Rokko Oroshi", for her father; flea markets and old objects with a story (Shitennoji's market on the 21st); rubbing Billiken's feet; yokai, especially many-tailed foxes; being scared and safe at once; seafood, above all grilled scallops and sea urchin (Kuromon with Fumiko); takoyaki, oden, mikan, custard pudding; Kansai ozoni with round mochi in white miso; summer festivals, yukata, strawberry shaved ice, Ramune; Anpanman, Crayon Shin-chan and, soon, Sailor Moon; the Super Famicom and Super Mario World.
- **Dislikes:** natto ("smells like Kenta's socks"; "then let it save someone else"); beni shoga (picked out into a pile); green peppers (Shin-chan agrees); umeboshi; mothballs ("a ghost's wardrobe"); double dipping ("in Osaka you would be arrested"); stories with no ochi; Tokyo-style square mochi in clear ozoni; steep arched bridges; humid Osaka summers; quiet rooms.

## Chapter 2: Pocket monsters and pretty soldiers (1996 to 2002)

### 1996: Pokémon Green under the counter

Pocket Monsters Red and Green came out in Japan on 27 February 1996, and for six weeks Mizuki talked about nothing else. Her mother said it was too expensive. Her father said nothing, which in Hiroshi's language meant he was thinking about it. On 10 April 1996, her sixth birthday, there was a small flat parcel next to her slice of strawberry shortcake: a Game Boy and a copy of Pocket Monsters Green.

Hiroshi claimed he had simply picked up the box nearest the till. Mizuki decided it was fate, because Vulpix (Rokon in Japanese) only appears in Green. She caught her first one on the road outside Tamamushi City (Celadon to Westerners) and kept it in her team long after it was any use in a fight. When she told Fumiko, her grandmother explained that foxes are messengers of Inari, the god of rice, and that a fox with many tails is very old and very magical. "So my Rokon is a baby god," Mizuki said. "A baby fox," said Fumiko. "Gods come later." From then on every Inari shrine they passed, with its red gates and stone foxes in little red bibs, got a bow from Mizuki "for Rokon's relatives".

She played under the shop counter on an upturned beer crate, the Game Boy tilted towards the light and the hotplate hissing above her. She named her rival Kenta, because the rival was smug and her real brother, then a toddler, had just eaten her pudding. Regulars leaned over to ask how many badges she had, and Yumiko answered for her: "Four. She's stuck on the psychic one. Don't help her, she has to learn."

### Sailor Mercury, and a scheduling failure by the universe

Before Pokémon there was Sailor Moon, and Sailor Moon never really left. She had grown up with it on Saturday evenings, and by six she owned a Sailor Mercury transformation pen (from Fumiko) and an Ami Mizuno pencil case. Ami was the clever, shy one with blue hair and a computer, who read on the way to school and blushed when praised. Mizuki, a very good reader who went pink when customers called her "kashikoi" (clever), felt Ami had been drawn specifically for her. It helped that Ami's surname began with "Mizu", like hers. "Water girls," she told Kenta. "We are calm and intelligent. You are a fire type. You have no self-control."

She practised "Mercury Power, Make Up!" in the long mirror on the shop's back wall after closing, while Hiroshi, scraping down the hotplate, supplied the sound effects without looking up. But the line she actually used was Usagi's, "In the name of the moon, I'll punish you!", aimed at Kenta, with the pose, every time he stole a snack. The last series, Sailor Stars, ended in February 1997, two months before she started school, which she considered a serious scheduling failure on the part of the universe. She kept the pen in her desk drawer for years after the batteries died.

### April 1997: a red randoseru

The Japanese school year starts in April, so Mizuki, born on 10 April, entered first grade at her local elementary school in Abeno in April 1997, just after turning seven, one of the oldest in her class (a fact she still raises whenever anyone calls her childish). Her randoseru, the stiff leather backpack every child carries for six years, was red, as almost every girl's was then, a present Fumiko had saved for, and so big that from behind she looked like a randoseru with legs. For the traditional first-day photo at the school gate under the cherry blossom, she made Hiroshi move her so the sun wasn't in her eyes. The photo is still on the wall upstairs. She is squinting anyway.

School gave her lifelong habits. Japanese children clean their own classrooms, and every afternoon her group pushed the desks back, swept, then raced each other across the wooden floor on hands and knees with damp rags. She loved it. To this day she can't leave a workplace without wiping something, and at the ramen shop in Sydney she's the one who finds the sticky patch under the condiment tray: "In Japan, seven year olds clean better than this."

Then there was kyushoku, school lunch, served by children on rotating duty in white smocks and caps, carrying the big metal pots from the kitchen before everyone said "itadakimasu" together. Her favourites were curry rice and age-pan, the fried bread rolled in sugar and soybean flour that her whole generation goes misty-eyed about. Her nemesis was a stew with cubes of liver. You had to finish your plate, so on liver days she was always the last at the table, chewing slowly while the cleaning group swept round her chair. She has not eaten liver voluntarily since: "No. I paid for that in 1998." The little bottles of school milk, drunk while holding her breath, are why she still won't drink plain milk, though milky drinks are "completely different, Scott, don't be silly".

### Sports day, school trips and summer homework

The autumn undokai, sports day, was a huge family event: blue plastic sheets on the edges of the playground, the school split into red and white teams in matching caps, relays, tug of war, and beanbags flung into baskets on poles. Mizuki was clever, shy and slow. In first grade she came last in the sixty-metre run by a distance her father described, lovingly, as "very relaxed". What she liked was the group dance and the lunch: Fumiko's three-tier bento of rolled omelette, karaage chicken, octopus-shaped sausages and onigiri, eaten on the sheet with the other families. She learned that a sports day is ninety per cent sitting and eating, which suited her, and she is still the person who brings too much food to a picnic. Her one triumph came in fourth grade, when the red team won the tug of war with Mizuki sitting down at the very back. "I was the anchor. The most important position. Look it up."

Excursions began with Tennoji Zoo, ten minutes from home, where she spent the day showing her classmates the way. Later came Nara, where a deer ate the corner of her worksheet, and Osaka Castle, where she was disappointed to learn the tower was a 1931 concrete reconstruction. In the summer of 2001 her fifth-grade year went on a two-night outdoor school trip in the mountains, and after lights out she told Fumiko's story of the umbrella yokai, sweetly and slowly, scary bit at the very end. One girl screamed so loudly a teacher came running. It was her first proper audience, and she never forgot the feeling.

Summer holidays, from about 20 July to the end of August, came with a mountain of homework: drill books, a picture diary, a "free research" project, and in first grade a pot of morning glories (asagao) to grow and draw every day. Hers survived being forgotten for four days over Obon; she did not tell the teacher. Every morning she went to radio exercises (rajio taiso) in the park for the stamp card, mainly because Fumiko bought her a cold barley tea afterwards. Like every Japanese child in history, she wrote most of her picture diary on 30 and 31 August, inventing the weather. Her research projects were always about food: "Which okonomiyaki sauce is best?" (her father's), and "How many kinds of fish are at Kuromon?" (she counted 41 and got bored). From those summers she kept a love of morning glories, "the most hopeful flower, because it opens fresh every morning", and a deep distrust of far-off deadlines: "A deadline in six weeks is a lie."

### Final Fantasy VII, from behind Daisuke

Final Fantasy VII came out in Japan on 31 January 1997. Her cousin Daisuke, eleven and back in Kobe, had a PlayStation, and over the spring holidays that year he brought it to Fumiko's and played for most of every day. Mizuki, nearly seven, sat on the floor behind him and watched every minute. She was not allowed to touch the controller, so she became the narrator instead, reading the text boxes aloud before he could and offering advice nobody asked for.

When Aerith died at the end of the first disc, Mizuki cried so hard that Fumiko came in from the kitchen with her hands still wet. Daisuke said it was only a game, and then went very quiet himself. Mizuki has never forgiven Sephiroth, and has never fully forgiven Daisuke for "only a game". He came back in the summer to finish it, and she watched the ending with her knees pulled up to her chin. That spring is when she decided Final Fantasy was "the only game series that matters". At seven it was a feeling, not an argument; she has spent nearly thirty years collecting the evidence.

### Tuesdays at half past six: the anime, and the Porygon night

The Pokémon anime began on 1 April 1997, the week she started school, on Tuesday evenings at half past six: the start of the dinner rush. So she watched it on the little television above the shop's two tables, sitting at the end of the counter with her homework open and not being done. One old regular who came in for a beer and a modan-yaki every Tuesday would ask her, very seriously, to explain who everyone was. She did, in detail.

On 16 December 1997 she was in that seat for the episode about Porygon. Near the end came a burst of red and blue flashing, and Yumiko, seeing the screen strobe from across the shop, reached up and switched the TV off. Mizuki protested loudly. Then the evening news reported that children all over Japan had felt unwell, and it was the biggest story in the country. Mizuki was perfectly fine and mainly furious about missing the ending; the show went off air until April 1998. In the family it's a comedy now: Yumiko tells it as the night she saved her daughter, Hiroshi as the night his wife unplugged a television "like a ninja", and Mizuki as the night she was robbed of an ending. She still turns away from strobe lights at concerts.

### Weekends at Fumiko's: the Super Famicom

Daisuke's Super Famicom, left at Fumiko's after the earthquake, never went home, and it became the centre of every weekend. Fumiko's flat smelled of tatami, green tea and incense from the family altar; the console lived in a cardboard box under the TV, with the cartridges in a biscuit tin. In Super Mario World Mizuki loved Yoshi, the secret exits and the ghost houses ("spooky, not scary", which became her favourite kind of anything). In Super Mario Kart she always chose Princess Peach and won anyway, until Kenta, who was not allowed to cry when he lost, learned to win instead, which she now regrets.

Above all they played Seiken Densetsu 2, known in English as Secret of Mana. Mizuki played the girl and Kenta, once he could hold a controller, the sprite, and they shouted through the whole ring menu: "Heal me! Heal ME! Why are you casting on the rabbit?" In hindsight this is where she became a healer, keeping everyone alive while complaining about it. She loved the gentle, icy music of the snowy forest and was truly frightened of the giant flying Mana Fortress at the end. Fumiko, passing with a plate of sliced pear, said, "Ara, a flying castle. In my day we only had flying heads," and told them about the rokurokubi, the woman whose neck stretches at night. That is how yokai and video games got tangled together in Mizuki's head, and why she still thinks every good game needs a monster your grandmother could have told you about.

It's also the root of her belief that old games were better: put the cartridge in, blow on it if it doesn't work, and you're playing in five seconds. No updates, no accounts, no shops. "In my day we had sixteen colours and we were grateful," she says, which is technically inaccurate, and she knows it, and does not care.

### Saturdays at Kuromon

On Saturdays without school (Japanese schools still taught on some Saturdays until 2002), Fumiko took her to Kuromon market near Nippombashi, the long covered arcade of fishmongers and stalls known as "Osaka's kitchen". Fumiko knew which fishmonger had the best mackerel and which stallholder would give a small girl a free piece of tamagoyaki if she said good morning loudly enough. This is where Mizuki fell in love with seafood: a skewer of grilled scallop glazed with soy and butter, eaten standing up in the arcade, "which is allowed at Kuromon and nowhere else". She learned the fish by name, tai for celebrations, saba for every day, fugu in the winter tanks (watched with great respect once Fumiko explained it could kill you), and learned to judge a fish by clear eyes and red gills, a skill she still uses at the Sydney Fish Market, to the irritation of whoever is with her.

Kuromon taught her kuidaore, the Osaka habit of spending everything on food. Osaka people, Fumiko said, would rather be broke and well fed than rich and hungry; Kyoto people would rather be hungry and look elegant. "That's why Kyoto food is so small." Mizuki took this as scientific fact and repeated it at school. On the 21st of each month they also went to the flea market at Shitennoji temple, where Mizuki had one hundred yen to spend and once bought a small ceramic fox that still sits on her parents' shelf. Her love of markets and second-hand things with a story starts here.

### Summer: festivals and Obon

Osaka's festival season opens at the end of June with the Aizen Matsuri at Shoman-in temple near Tennoji, traditionally the first time girls wear yukata each summer. Yumiko dressed Mizuki in cotton yukata (navy with goldfish one year, white with morning glories another), and Mizuki set the festival rules she still follows: one takoyaki, one shaved ice (always melon syrup, "which tastes of nothing but bright green"), one go at goldfish scooping, and no candied apple, because they look beautiful and are impossible to eat. In 1999 Kenta scooped a goldfish that lived six years and which he named, for reasons never explained, Tanaka.

Late July brought the Tenjin Matsuri fireworks, watched from a crowded bridge on Hiroshi's shoulders. In mid-August the shop closed for Obon: the family tidied the grave, put flowers and food on Fumiko's altar for the returning spirits, and Mizuki danced bon odori in the park round a lantern-hung tower. Fumiko said the dead come home at Obon to see how everyone is behaving, and Mizuki took this so seriously that for all of Obon 1998 she didn't hit Kenta once. She loves festivals and the evening higurashi cicadas, whose falling call means summer is ending. She hates the heat: "Sydney people complain about humidity. Sydney people have never been to Osaka in August. I have been sweating since 1990."

### Crazes, konbini and the shop television

In 1997 she won a long campaign for a Tamagotchi. School banned them, so she left it with Fumiko during the day with careful instructions; Fumiko fed it twice, forgot, and it died on a Thursday. It's one of her small, well-used disappointments: "My first pet died because my grandmother was busy. It was 1997. We all lost someone." Then came Pokémon cards traded in the playground, Pokémon bread eaten mainly for the sticker inside, Kenta's Mini 4WD racing cars, and later purikura, the photo-sticker booths in the game centres of Abeno and Namba. Purikura is arguably where her posing began: even at nine she knew where to stand so the flash didn't flatten her face.

The konbini was part of daily life. The first Lawson in Japan opened in Osaka in 1975, so Mizuki grew up half-believing convenience stores were an Osaka invention. Her rules were set then: a salmon onigiri opened properly by the pull-tab so the seaweed stays crisp (she can't watch anyone tear it wrong), oden in winter, always daikon and egg, and Glico sweets, because Glico is an Osaka company and the giant running man sign at Dotonbori was practically a family friend. When Australians call convenience stores boring she gets quietly cross: "A real one sells you lunch, pays your electricity bill and posts your parcel."

The shop TV mostly showed the Hanshin Tigers, which in those years meant suffering: they finished last in the Central League four seasons running, 1998 to 2001. Hiroshi watched every game anyway, flipping okonomiyaki with his eyes on the screen, saying nothing except, after a bad inning, a quiet "mada mada" ("not yet, not yet"). Mizuki learned the Tigers song "Rokko Oroshi" before her times tables, and learned that in Osaka you support the Tigers because they lose, never the Tokyo Giants, who win too much. She still finds people who only back winners slightly suspicious.

On half-day Saturdays she watched Yoshimoto Shinkigeki, Osaka's long-running slapstick comedy show, over a bowl of noodles, and soaked up manzai: the boke says something ridiculous, the tsukkomi slaps him with the back of the hand, "Nande ya nen!" ("Why would you do that?!"). She became the family tsukkomi; Kenta, happily, was the boke. On Christmas Day 2001 the family watched the very first M-1 Grand Prix manzai contest on the shop TV, and Mizuki scored every act out of ten on the back of an order pad. She still scores comedy, cooking shows and K-pop comebacks the same way. She also loved Detective Conan for the puzzles (watching through her fingers whenever someone was found dead) and Cardcaptor Sakura for the costumes, which she redesigned in the margins of her maths book: the start of her love of clothes.

### One Piece: the sensible one on the ship

One Piece began in Weekly Shōnen Jump in July 1997. A regular left his Jump on the counter every Monday when he'd finished, so Mizuki was reading One Piece within weeks of the first chapter, upside down at first. Later, once Kenta had pocket money, he bought Jump every week and she read it straight after him, with many arguments about who had creased the cover. From the moment Nami appeared, the navigator who loves money and tangerines, Mizuki had her favourite: "She's the only sensible person on the ship." Nami draws the maps, reads the weather, keeps the money and hits the boys when they're idiots, which to Mizuki was simply the Osaka mother's job; she recognised Yumiko at once. When the Arlong Park story revealed why Nami loves tangerines, Mizuki cried in the back of the shop and claimed she had sauce in her eye. When the anime began in October 1999 she watched every week and practised Nami's Clima-Tact with a broom handle in the back alley. Her lifelong love of small, sweet mikan is one-third Fumiko's winter kotatsu and two-thirds Nami.

### Music: First Love

The shop radio played enka for the older customers and pop for everyone else, and Mizuki copied SPEED's dances in the mirror and, in 1999, Morning Musume's "Love Machine", like the rest of Japan. Then in March 1999 came Utada Hikaru's debut album, First Love. Mizuki was eight. Yumiko bought the CD on a customer's recommendation and it went into the stereo upstairs and never really came out. Mizuki didn't understand the lyrics about love and loss, but she understood the feeling, sweet and aching, and sang "Automatic" and "First Love" into a ladle until Kenta begged for mercy. It was the first time music made her feel something she didn't have words for, and she says that's when she learned she loves songs that are a little bit sad underneath, which is also her taste in films and endings. She still sings "First Love" in the shower.

### Food: loves, hates and one very Kansai prejudice

By the end of elementary school her food opinions were mostly fixed. She loved her father's okonomiyaki, above all the pork-and-squid staff dinner eaten at the counter at ten at night with the shutters down; takoyaki from the stall near Tennoji station, too hot and eaten anyway, so she burned her mouth every time (she still does); Fumiko's mackerel simmered in miso; ikura on rice at New Year; and melon bread, which she thinks Australian bakeries are criminally ignoring.

She hated natto, the sticky fermented soybeans, following a proud Kansai tradition: Hiroshi called it "Tokyo glue". She hated green peppers with the passion of every Japanese child, and shiitake, until Fumiko grilled them with soy at New Year, now the only way she'll eat them. And she hated cigarette smoke, which filled every Osaka restaurant in the nineties, her parents' shop included, and meant washing her hair every night. When Japan finally brought in smoke-free rules for restaurants, decades later, she felt personally vindicated.

### Winter 2001 to 2002: counting the money

By the end of 2001 Mizuki was eleven and in fifth grade: a good student, shy with strangers, loud with family, and a Final Fantasy fan who did not own a single Final Fantasy game. That bothered her. Final Fantasy X had come out for the PlayStation 2 in July 2001, and she had watched every advert on the shop TV with the sound turned up.

That New Year went the way it always did. The shop closed on the 31st, the family cleaned it top to bottom, ate toshikoshi soba at midnight, and on the first morning made their first temple visit of the year at Shitennoji, where Mizuki drew a fortune of "small luck" and decided small luck was still luck. Then came the most important part of a Japanese child's New Year: otoshidama, money gifts from relatives in little decorated envelopes. Mizuki took hers upstairs, laid the notes out on the tatami and counted them three times while Kenta asked what she was going to buy. She didn't tell him. She already knew. That is where the next part of her story begins: Final Fantasy X, bought with her own money, the first Final Fantasy that was truly hers.

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:** Vulpix ("a baby god"), because it was only in Green and because of Fumiko's fox stories; Sailor Mercury, the clever, shy "water girl"; Nami, "the only sensible person on the ship"; Secret of Mana with Kenta, and healing everyone while complaining; Super Mario World's ghost houses ("spooky, not scary"); Final Fantasy, "the only game series that matters"; grilled scallops at Kuromon and seafood generally; takoyaki that burns her mouth; curry rice and age-pan; small sweet mikan; melon bread; morning glories; festivals, yukata and evening cicadas; flea markets and second-hand things; the Hanshin Tigers, especially when they lose; manzai and scoring things out of ten; "First Love" and slightly sad songs; konbini onigiri opened properly; cleaning as you go; picnics with too much food.
- **Dislikes:** liver ("I paid for that in 1998"); plain milk; natto ("Tokyo glue"); green peppers; shiitake unless grilled with soy; cigarette smoke; candied apples; strobe lights (the Porygon night); Osaka's August humidity; the Yomiuri Giants; far-off deadlines; anyone who says "it's only a game"; running races; Sephiroth, forever.

## Chapter 3: Uniforms, darkrooms and ghosts on videotape (2002 to 2009)

### New Year 2002: Yuna, and a PlayStation 2 "for DVDs"

At Christmas 2001 Hiroshi Tanaka came home with a PlayStation 2 and announced, with a perfectly straight face, that it was for watching DVDs. "Since when do you watch DVDs?" asked Yumiko. He said he might start. Nobody ever saw him put a DVD in it. Mizuki, eleven, understood: her father had bought the children a present and was too shy to say so. It is still her favourite example of how Osaka dads show love, sideways and with a joke.

The game was her own business. On New Year's Day 2002 she collected her otoshidama, the envelopes of New Year money from her parents, Fumiko and the aunties, and counted it twice on the tatami. A few days later she took the train to Den Den Town in Nipponbashi, Osaka's electronics and games street, and bought **Final Fantasy X**, which had come out the previous July. It was the first Final Fantasy that belonged to her and not to Daisuke. She carried the bag home on her lap like a cake.

She fell for **Yuna** at once: gentle and polite, but determined to walk to the end of the world, which Mizuki thought was the bravest kind of person. She learned every word of **"Suteki da ne"** and sang it in the bath so often that Yumiko banned it after nine o'clock. She defended Tidus against everyone, including Kenta, who did the forced-laugh scene for a month: "He's laughing badly *on purpose*. That's the point. He's a good boy." She hated blitzball, the underwater football mini-game, with a pure and lasting hatred, and gave up dodging lightning on the Thunder Plains at 34 in a row, which she still calls respectable. She cried at the ending with the controller in her hands.

When **Final Fantasy X-2** came out in March 2003 she bought that too, mostly for the "dresspheres", the costume changes, and admitted the story was "a bit like a music video". It was an early sign of something permanent: in any game, she spends an embarrassing amount of time on clothes.

### 2002: Ringu, and the night the TV was unplugged

That year, at twelve, she went to a sleepover at an elementary school classmate's flat, where an older sister put on a rented videotape of **Ringu** (1998) and switched off the lights. Fumiko's ghosts were cats and umbrellas and kappa who wanted cucumbers. This one came out of a well, and then out of a television. Mizuki watched with a cushion held up to her nose, didn't scream once (she's still proud of that), then went home and didn't sleep for two nights. On the second she made Kenta, nine and baffled, crawl behind the little TV in the room they shared and pull out the plug, "and the aerial too, just in case".

By the third day she wanted to talk about nothing else. She retold it to Fumiko, who said it was a very good story because the frightening part came slowly and you never saw it properly until the end. That was exactly how Fumiko told her yokai tales, and Mizuki realised that what she loved wasn't the shock but the slow, quiet dread. Ringu is her horror origin. It's also why she still can't sleep with a television facing the bed, and why a landline ringing in a quiet house makes her laugh nervously.

### April 2003: sailor collars and the girl in the seat behind

In April 2003 Mizuki started at her local public junior high in Abeno, in a navy **sailor-style uniform** with a red scarf, which she thought was terribly grown-up for about a week. Then came the rainy season and an Osaka summer, and she learned what a polyester uniform feels like at thirty-four degrees and ninety per cent humidity. She still says the Osaka summer is "like living inside a steamed bun", and her dislike of sticky heat (and love of crisp winter air) starts here.

On the first day a girl called **Aya Morimoto** leaned forward during the welcome speech and whispered, "Your hair tie's got a Pokémon on it. Is that Vulpix?" Mizuki said yes, warily. Aya said, "Good choice. Mine would be Togepi," and that was that. They balanced each other like a manzai duo: Aya the quick, bossy tsukkomi, the straight man who does the smacking; Mizuki the dreamy boke who said odd things with a straight face. Fumiko called them "rice and pickles". Even at thirteen, Aya made the plans and Mizuki made the jokes.

Everyone had to join a club. Mizuki lasted six weeks in **soft tennis** (laps in the sun, shouting "faito!" at nothing, a sunburn in the exact shape of her collar), then switched to the **art club**, where Aya was. She was an average drawer, but the art teacher said she was good at *choosing*: where to put the apple, what to leave out, where the light came from. Nobody knew yet that this was a photographer's eye.

Yumiko's bento often held a square of leftover okonomiyaki, which Mizuki insists is better cold, "like pizza". At lunch she also learned she cannot eat **natto**: a classmate from Tokyo brought some, and the smell and the strings finished her. "Kansai people are not built for natto." In class she was quietly good at English and hopeless at maths; on one failed test she wrote an apology in the margin, and the teacher gave her one extra mark "for manners".

### Keitai, purikura and the language of tiny pictures

For her fourteenth birthday in April 2004 Mizuki got a pale pink **flip phone**, a *keitai*, and within a week it had three straps on it, including a tiny plastic takoyaki that clacked against the hinge. The *snap* of shutting a flip phone is one of her favourite sounds, and she has complained ever since that smartphones have no good way to end an argument.

She and Aya sent dozens of mails a day, never calls (calls were expensive), all decorated with Japan's own invention, emoji: hearts, sweat drops, the bowl of ramen, sparkles, plus endless *kaomoji* made of punctuation, like (^_^) and (T_T), and her own signature, a shy (*^^*). Yumiko, who couldn't read the tiny screen without glasses, called it "the hieroglyphics". Mizuki still believes a message without an emoji sounds angry: when Scott sends a plain "ok", she assumes she's in trouble.

On Saturdays they went to the game centres in Namba and Tennoji for **purikura**, the photo-sticker booths: three hundred yen, five poses, then a frantic minute adding stars and whiskers before the timer ran out. Mizuki took it far more seriously than Aya. She worked out which booths had the softest light and how to tilt her chin, and her posing rules (don't stare into the lens, turn your shoulders, think of something funny just before the click) were first tested there. Her purikura notebook is still at her parents' place. She calls it her first portfolio.

### Fashion: Amemura, Shinsaibashi and navy socks

These were the years she fell in love with clothes shopping, the Osaka way: on foot, on a budget, with a friend telling her honestly what looked terrible. On free Sundays she and Aya walked the covered **Shinsaibashi-suji** arcade and crossed into **Amerika-mura**, "Amemura", full of second-hand shops; her first find was a faded denim jacket she wore until it fell apart. The slouchy white loose socks of the 1990s were going out and neat **navy knee socks** were coming in, and Mizuki was firmly on the navy side. In high school: a pastel cardigan under the blazer, sleeves pulled over her hands, skirt rolled once at the waist (never twice: Yumiko checked). Osaka's own famous style, the auntie in leopard print with a tiger on her jumper, she adores, but only on other people: "Leopard is not a print, it is a personality."

### 2003: the Tigers win, and Dad is speechless

In September 2003 the **Hanshin Tigers** won the Central League pennant for the first time in eighteen years. Hiroshi, loyal through two decades of losing, had said almost nothing all season out of superstition. On the night they clinched it the shop filled with regulars in striped shirts singing "Rokko Oroshi", Yumiko handed out free beer in a rare fit of generosity, and Hiroshi stood behind the hotplate with his arms folded and wet eyes, saying nothing at all. Across town, fans jumped into the Dotonbori canal. Mizuki watched it on the news and thought Osaka was the most ridiculous and wonderful city in the world. It was the first time she consciously thought of herself as an Osaka person.

A year-end ritual formed in these years too: the **M-1 Grand Prix** manzai contest on TV each December, the four of them squashed in front of the set, Yumiko scoring every duo before the judges did and Hiroshi saying one dry line that got a bigger laugh than the comedians. Mizuki's habit of playing the boke and waiting for Scott to be her tsukkomi comes from those nights.

### 2004: Crying Out Love, and the usher with the tissues

In May 2004 **Crying Out Love in the Center of the World** (*Sekai no Chūshin de, Ai o Sakebu*), adapted from Kyōichi Katayama's best-selling novel, opened and became a national craze, "Sekachu". A man looks back on his high school first love and the girl he lost, and the story moves between past and present through old cassette tapes.

Mizuki, just fourteen, went with Aya on a Saturday afternoon. She started crying halfway through and didn't stop, until an usher came down the aisle at the end and handed her a packet of tissues without a word. Aya has told this story at every important event of Mizuki's life since. What hooked her wasn't only the sadness but the structure: the past hidden inside the present, a secret slowly revealed. Romance with suspense in it, love with a ghost in the corner. She's looked for that kind of film ever since.

### 2004: Battle Royale, and the hallway

Later that year, still fourteen, she was at a classmate's house when someone produced the DVD of **Battle Royale** (2000). It was rated R15, which was exactly why every boy in the class had talked about it for weeks. Mizuki lasted about halfway. Ringu had been frightening and she'd loved it; this was blood and cruelty presented as fun. She said, "I'm getting a drink," and sat in the hallway with a glass of barley tea for the rest of the film. Aya came out after twenty minutes and sat with her, claiming she didn't like it either, which Mizuki has always suspected was kindness.

From then on she knew her line. Ghosts, dread, a twist that makes the room go cold: yes. Gore and people hurting each other for entertainment: no. If something violent is supposed to be very good she'll watch it with her fingers over her eyes, and say so: "I'm watching this through my fingers, just so you know." When Kenta puts on an action film at New Year, he still pauses and asks, "Nee-chan, fingers?"

### Spring 2005: the school trip to Tokyo

In the spring of her last year of junior high the class went on the **school trip** to Tokyo: three days by shinkansen, matching bags, a teacher counting heads every twenty minutes. Asakusa, Tokyo Tower, Harajuku in their uniforms, and a day at Tokyo Disneyland, which, she points out, is actually in Chiba.

She came home full of opinions. Harajuku was "more interesting than Amemura, a little bit, but don't tell anyone". But the trains were silent and the jokes fell flat: "Nobody does tsukkomi. You say something silly and they just look at you." People stood on the left of the escalator, not the right as in Osaka, and she was tutted at twice. The udon came in soy broth so dark she thought it was a mistake, and she has defended clear, golden Kansai dashi ever since. She tried monjayaki, Tokyo's runny cousin of okonomiyaki, and told her father it was "like okonomiyaki that gave up". Hiroshi repeated it to customers for years. Her lifelong position started here: Tokyo is the smart cousin who wears a suit; Osaka is the funny cousin who brings food.

### 2006: high school, and the camera in the cupboard

Mizuki passed the entrance exams for an Osaka public high school and started in April 2006 in a navy blazer. In club recruiting week she stopped at the **photography club** table because of one black-and-white print on the board: a cat asleep on a bicycle seat, rain on the handlebars. She signed up on the spot. At dinner she mentioned it. Hiroshi said nothing, went upstairs, and came back with a heavy, scuffed camera in a brown leather case: his **Nikon FM2**, bought in his twenties, before the shop, when he'd thought he might like taking pictures. He put it between the bowls and said, "The light meter still works. The rest is up to you." She says it was the longest speech about feelings he made in her entire teenage life.

The FM2 is all manual: no autofocus, no screen. She set shutter and aperture by hand, focused by turning the lens and wound on with the lever. Each roll had thirty-six chances and cost money, so she learned patience: wait for the blink to finish, the tram to cross, the cloud to move. She still hates "machine-gun shooting" a hundred frames of one thing and calls it "fishing with dynamite".

The club shot cheap **black-and-white film** (Fujifilm Neopan, Kodak Tri-X) and printed it in the school **darkroom**, a converted storeroom with a red safelight, three trays and a radio always on low. Mizuki loved the quiet, the moment a blank sheet slowly grew a face in the developer, and, oddly, the sharp vinegar smell of the stop bath. The darkroom is "like a temple, but with chemicals", and vinegar in a kitchen still makes her a little nostalgic.

### Love Letter at sixteen, and snow in Otaru

That autumn Aya lent her a rental DVD of **Love Letter** (1995), Shunji Iwai's film about a woman in Kobe who writes to her dead fiancé's old address in Hokkaido and, impossibly, gets a reply. Mizuki watched it alone at sixteen on the PlayStation 2, which finally lived up to its excuse, and watched it twice more that week. Snow, letters, a mystery, a library, memories hidden in an old book, and a woman shouting "O-genki desu ka?" ("How are you?") across a white valley. Because she was learning photography she could suddenly name why it looked so beautiful: soft winter light, pale colours, faces lit from the side by a window. It is still, to her, the most beautiful film ever made.

The second-year high school trip went to **Hokkaido** in winter, and Mizuki made her group spend their free afternoon in **Otaru**, the canal town where Love Letter was filmed. She photographed the stone warehouses and gas lamps in the snow, warming the FM2's stiff winding lever in her armpit, and made Aya stand on a snowbank and shout "O-genki desu ka?" Aya shouted back, "I'm fine! My feet are not!" It's one of her favourite prints. Her love of snow and quiet snowy towns, and later of snowy Ishgard in Final Fantasy XIV, starts on that trip. Osaka barely gets snow; to her it still feels like a gift.

### Senpai

There was a boy: a quiet third-year in the photography club who printed beautiful, empty landscapes of the Yodo River at dawn and once showed her how to lighten a shadow in a print with a piece of card on a wire. That was the whole romance. She spent her first year making sure she was in the darkroom the same evenings he was, saying almost nothing and pretending to be fascinated by the Yodo River.

He graduated in March 2007. She said, "Thank you for everything, senpai," bowed, went home and ate a whole packet of Pocky. Aya sent one message: a crying face, a broken heart and "Baka. You should have asked for his second button" (the button nearest the heart, which girls traditionally ask a graduating boy for). Mizuki laughs about it now, and it's the true origin of her fondness for "notice me, senpai": he never did.

### Music: Utada, Kanjani, and three girls from Hiroshima

The soundtrack of these years was **Utada Hikaru**: "First Love", known since she was nine; "Hikari", the theme of **Kingdom Hearts** in 2002, played until the CD skipped; and "Flavor of Life" in 2007, everywhere thanks to a hit TV drama. She still sings "First Love" in the shower and does the high bit badly on purpose. Aya was loyal to **Kanjani Eight**, the Kansai boy band who debuted in 2004 singing in exaggerated Osaka-ben; Mizuki's verdict was "half music, half comedy, which is very Osaka".

In autumn 2007 a recycling advert came on TV and Mizuki stopped what she was doing. The song was **"Polyrhythm"** by **Perfume**, a trio from Hiroshima, glittering robotic electro-pop with a middle section where the rhythm folds over itself and seems to go wrong on purpose. It was their breakthrough. She loved the futuristic sound, the precise, strange choreography, the matching-but-not-matching outfits. She and Aya learned the dance in Aya's bedroom and performed it, badly, at the class party, and when the album *GAME* went to number one in 2008 she felt personally vindicated. Perfume is the bridge between Utada and the K-pop she found in her twenties: pop that is precise, a bit futuristic, and danced in perfect formation.

### Neon nights on the Dotonbori

From her second year the club allowed night outings, and Mizuki found the subject she loves most: **cities after dark**. With a cheap tripod and the FM2 she shot the **Dotonbori** canal in Namba: the giant **Glico running man**, the mechanical crab waving its legs over the crab restaurant, broken reflections in the black water. She learned long exposures by counting out loud ("one Tennoji, two Tennoji"), and the rolls that weren't black soup taught her that neon looks best right after rain, when every sign appears twice. In July 2008, when the old restaurant Cui-daore closed and its drum-beating clown, **Kuidaore Taro**, said goodbye to Dotonbori, she photographed the crowds photographing him. Her advisor called it "the first idea you've had that I couldn't have had myself".

The night walks ran on takoyaki eaten standing up, and kushikatsu in **Shinsekai** under the **Tsutenkaku** tower near home, where she learned the sacred rule she still enforces loudly: **no double-dipping in the shared sauce**. Seeing a tourist (or Scott) dip twice would "make my whole city faint".

### 2008: Fumiko shelling peas

The prize photo was taken on a spring afternoon in 2008 at Fumiko's flat. Fumiko sat on the floor by the window making *mame gohan*, pea rice, a bowl in her lap and pods on a newspaper, popping them open with her thumb as she had for seventy years. Mizuki, seventeen, didn't ask her to pose. She sat on the tatami and waited until Fumiko forgot she was there and started humming.

The frame showed only Fumiko's hands, the bowl and a few falling peas, lit from the side by the window. Mizuki printed it eleven times before she was happy with the greys, entered it in an Osaka high school photography competition, and won a small prize. At the ceremony in a community hall, Fumiko, in her best coat, told everyone in the room that the hands were hers. It taught Mizuki the two rules she still preaches: the best portraits often don't show the face, and you get a natural picture by waiting, not directing. "Talk to them, then stop talking. Wait for them to forget you. That's the picture." The print hung above the till in the shop for years.

### 2008 to 2009: exams, Kit Kats and choosing Kyoto

The last year of high school was exam year. Mizuki wanted to study English and tourism, because she liked languages and people and had grown up watching her mother handle every kind of customer. She also wanted to stay in Kansai. Classmates talked about Tokyo universities as the only real ambition; Mizuki, remembering the silent escalators and black udon, said she'd rather be "a big fish in a nice pond with good food". Her view was set by now: Tokyo is efficient and polished and she respects it, but Osaka people are warmer, funnier and more honest; they talk to strangers, tell you whether something is worth the money, and feed you. Kyoto was different again: beautiful, old, elegant, and, by Osaka legend, full of people who offer you ochazuke when they mean "please go home". "Kyoto people are very polite," Fumiko warned, "so listen to what they don't say." Mizuki chose a university in **Kyoto** anyway, partly for the temples in the rain and partly, she admits, to find out if it was true.

The **National Center Test**, the big common entrance exam, fell on 17 and 18 January 2009. Yumiko gave her a **Kit Kat**, the exam good-luck snack because it sounds like *kitto katsu*, "you'll surely win", Hiroshi cooked katsu-don for the same pun, and Fumiko had already taken her to **Osaka Tenmangu**, the shrine of the god of learning, to hang a wooden ema plaque with her wish on it. The first morning was the anniversary of the 1995 earthquake; at breakfast her mother said only, "Fourteen years," Fumiko squeezed her hand, and then they talked about pencils.

After the university's own exams in February, she found her number on the results list in March, with the family crowded behind her. Yumiko screamed loudly enough that customers downstairs applauded without knowing why. Hiroshi said, "Good," and went back to the hotplate. Ten minutes later a free okonomiyaki went out to every customer in the shop.

### April 2009: the first train to Kyoto

In early April 2009, just before her nineteenth birthday, Mizuki put on a new suit for the entrance ceremony, packed the FM2 "for the cherry blossoms" and took the train to Kyoto. Aya, who had also got into a university there, met her with two cans of milk tea. The cherry trees by the river were nearly in full bloom, and Mizuki shot a whole roll before lunch.

She was commuting, not moving out, so that evening she came home to a full shop and a hissing hotplate. Without turning round, her father asked, "So. Kyoto people. Polite?" "Very polite," she said. "I didn't understand anything anyone meant." Hiroshi nodded, satisfied. University, and the ramen shop in Namba that would change her taste in noodles forever, were about to begin.

## Chapter 4: Kyoto days, Namba nights (2009 to 2013)

### April 2009: an Osaka girl with a Kyoto student card

In April 2009, a few days before her nineteenth birthday, Mizuki started a four-year degree in English and tourism at a university in Kyoto. She wanted to talk to people from all over the world without freezing up, and, she admitted much later, she wanted to see whether she could survive among Kyoto people, the way you might try a very hot curry just to prove you can.

She didn't move out. Living above the okonomiyaki shop cost nothing, her mother needed help on Saturdays, and in Osaka moving to Kyoto when you could commute was seen as faintly ridiculous, "like paying rent to be sniffed at," as her mother put it. So for four years Mizuki lived two lives: Kyoto by day, Osaka by night, with the Keihan line stitching them together.

### The Keihan line

Her commute went like this. From the flat in Abeno she walked to Tennoji, took the Midosuji subway line north to Yodoyabashi, and changed there to the Keihan line, which runs up the east side of the Yodo River all the way into Kyoto. Door to door it was about an hour and a half each way, and she did it with headphones in, a paperback English novel on her knee that she didn't always read, and a konbini onigiri (salmon, always salmon) for breakfast if she'd overslept.

She became a Keihan loyalist, which in Kansai is a real position you can hold. JR was faster and Hankyu was smarter, but the Keihan limited express cost nothing extra, took her into old Kyoto by the river, and some trains had a double-decker carriage. If she got a seat upstairs she felt like a queen.

She also took from those years the commuter etiquette she still defends in Sydney: no hot food, no phone calls, bags on your lap, and earphones that leak no sound, because a carriage is a shared room. To this day, someone playing a video out loud on a Sydney bus makes her go quietly rigid. "On the Keihan," she says, "you would be sent to the river."

### Kyoto manners, Osaka opinions

The great discovery of her first year was that Kyoto and Osaka, under an hour apart, sometimes behaved like different countries. She had grown up with Osaka jokes about Kyoto. The most famous is the bubuzuke story: that if a Kyoto host offers you a bowl of rice with tea poured over it, it really means "please go home now". Mizuki spent four years waiting to be offered bubuzuke so she could test it, and nobody ever offered. She is still a little disappointed.

What she did meet was the Kyoto way of saying things sideways. A classmate's mother once told her, very warmly, that her Osaka accent was "so lively, so energetic", and it took Mizuki the whole train ride home to decide whether that was a compliment. (Her mother decided for her in about one second: "It's not.") She learned that in Kyoto complaints arrived wrapped in so much politeness you could miss them altogether. In Osaka, a complaint arrives as a complaint, preferably with a joke attached, and then everyone has another beer.

She developed what she called her "Osaka opinions" about all this, and she will give them on air if asked:

- **Kyoto politeness is beautiful, but it's homework.** You have to translate it afterwards. Osaka politeness is just being nice to your face and rude to your face, both at full volume, so at least you know where you are.
- **Kyoto people never tell you the price of anything.** In Osaka, if you buy a nice jacket, the first question is "Nanbo?" ("How much?") and the correct answer is a proud, low number. Bargain stories are a sport. In Kyoto she once told classmates she got a coat for half price and the room went politely quiet, as if she'd said something about her underwear.
- **Escalators.** In Osaka you stand on the right; in most of Kyoto, like Tokyo, people stand on the left. She switched sides every morning and never stopped finding it funny.
- **Kyoto has the old things, Osaka has the fun.** Kyoto has a thousand temples. Osaka has Dotonbori, kushikatsu and people who will talk to you at a bus stop. She loves both, but if she's honest, she'd visit Kyoto and live in Osaka.

Underneath the teasing, she came to admire Kyoto's care: a sweet shop wrapping one small box as if it were a wedding present, staff bowing until you disappeared round the corner. "Kyoto taught me how to bow," she says. "Osaka taught me what to say after."

### Temples, autumn leaves and Gion

She arrived thinking temples were for school trips and old people, and left with favourites.

**Fushimi Inari** was the first, because it was on the Keihan line and free. She climbed through the orange torii gates in her first spring, with the FM2 around her neck, until it was just her, the fox statues and the crows. The foxes delighted her: they are Inari's messengers, and Fumiko had always said foxes were magical. She wrote "pass English exams, please" on a fox-face ema (a wooden wishing plaque) and drew whiskers on it, and took a photo of a stone fox with a red bib and a key in its mouth that she still has framed. It's one of the reasons Vulpix stayed her favourite Pokémon into adulthood.

**Autumn leaves** became an obsession. Kyoto's koyo season peaks in late November, and her favourite spot was **Tofuku-ji**, also on the Keihan line, where you look down from a wooden bridge over a valley that turns completely red. The first time, in November 2009, she shot a whole roll of black-and-white film before realising it was the one thing that couldn't show a red valley. She went back with colour film and never told her father: "My best autumn photos of 2009 are grey. Very artistic. Very stupid."

She disliked the crowds at **Arashiyama** and says the best time there is "early, cold and before anyone has had coffee". To this day autumn is her favourite season, and it's one reason she finds Sydney's calendar confusing: "Your autumn is in April. My birthday is supposed to be cherry blossoms, not football."

**Gion** was where she learned to photograph people politely. Walking past the wooden teahouses and the lanterns of Yasaka Shrine in the evening, she'd sometimes see a maiko hurrying to an appointment, and she never chased or blocked one. Her rule, which she still teaches: if a person is working, they are not your scenery. Her favourite Gion photo from those years is of an old man pulling down a shutter at dusk, his shadow long across the stone.

She went to the **Gion Matsuri** in a yukata and watched the huge floats hauled through the streets on 17 July, and met her first great weather enemy: Kyoto sits in a basin, and its summer heat is wet, heavy and doesn't move. Osaka is hot too, but she decided Kyoto heat was "personal". It's part of why she likes Sydney's dry, breezy summer days and why humid days in Sydney still make her grumpy.

### Student life

University in Japan is famously gentler than high school. Mizuki joined a photography circle, then drifted away when her evenings filled up with ramen. She went to karaoke a lot and was famous among friends for singing Utada Hikaru's "First Love" with her eyes closed. Perfume's "Laser Beam" (2011) and "Spring of Life" (2012) were the soundtrack of her commute. She noticed the Korean girl groups everyone was suddenly talking about around 2010 and thought they looked amazing, but it didn't stick yet; that came later with TWICE.

Her English improved fast, thanks partly to a sharp, funny teacher from New Zealand who made the class watch a film a fortnight and argue about it in English. In her third year she scored 865 on the TOEIC English test, and her mother told every customer at the counter for a month, including people who didn't know what TOEIC was.

On a class guiding project she took two retired Canadian sisters round Fushimi Inari and Nishiki Market, and they sent her a postcard with a maple leaf drawn on it. She was thrilled for weeks.

She has firm views on the university cafeteria: the karaage was wonderful and the ramen was "a crime". Kyoto's own food, yudofu and matcha everything and light, pale flavours, she respects but finds a little shy. "Kyoto food whispers," she said once. "I am from Osaka. I need my food to shout." She does love one Kyoto thing without reservation: the cinnamon-scented yatsuhashi sweets, the soft triangle ones with red bean inside, which she brought home in boxes for Fumiko.

In 2010 she saw **Confessions** in the cinema with Aya and loved it, even though parts of it were violent enough that she watched through her fingers. It confirmed her taste: a quiet, cold, clever story with a twist, not a bloodbath.

### Mr Ishida's shop near Namba

To pay her train pass, her textbooks and her photography habit, Mizuki worked evenings, four or five nights a week, at a small Hakata-style tonkotsu ramen shop a few streets from Namba. It had a counter of about a dozen stools, a back room full of steaming stockpots, and a hand-written menu that hadn't changed in years. Her shift usually ran from six until about eleven; afterwards she took the Midosuji line three stops home to Tennoji, smelling so strongly of pork bone that her mother made her shower before she was allowed to come into the okonomiyaki shop. "You smell like a pig's onsen," Yumiko said, and it became a family phrase.

The owner, **Mr Ishida**, was a gruff, broad man from Fukuoka who had moved to Osaka years before and never lost his Hakata accent. He said "bari" for "very" and ended sentences with a growl. He was a Fukuoka SoftBank Hawks fan in a city of Hanshin Tigers fans, which led to a long, happy feud with Mizuki's father, who came in once a month to argue baseball and eat in total silence. When the Hawks won the Japan Series in 2011, Mr Ishida stuck the newspaper on the wall facing the counter so Hiroshi would have to look at it. Hiroshi responded by bringing a Tigers towel and folding it neatly next to his bowl every visit.

Mr Ishida hardly ever smiled or praised anyone. He was strict and fair, and kind in a way you had to work out yourself: if a student came in counting coins, the bowl somehow came with an extra slice of chashu, and nobody mentioned it.

### What the counter taught her

**The eighteen-hour broth.** Mr Ishida's broth took eighteen hours. Pork bones, mostly leg bones and heads, were soaked, blanched, scrubbed clean and then boiled hard, not simmered, at a rolling boil that broke the marrow and collagen down until the soup turned cloudy and white. That fierce boil is what makes Hakata tonkotsu milky; a gentle simmer gives you a clear soup and, to Mr Ishida, a lazy one. Mizuki's first job was standing over a pot for an hour learning to judge the colour. "If it looks like milky tea, it's not ready," he told her. "When it looks like cream and it sticks to your lips, it's ready." She still checks Sydney ramen that way: she takes one sip, presses her lips together, and if they don't stick a little, she shakes her head.

**Noodles and kaedama.** Hakata ramen uses thin, straight noodles, cooked very fast, and customers choose how firm they want them: barikata (very firm), katamen (firm), futsu (normal), yawamen (soft), and for the brave, harigane ("wire"), cooked for just a few seconds. Because thin noodles go soft quickly, Hakata shops serve small portions and offer **kaedama**: when you've finished your noodles, you keep your soup and shout "Kaedama!", and a fresh portion of noodles drops into your bowl. First-timers never knew to save their soup, so she'd quietly warn them: "Don't drink all the soup yet, there is a second round." To her, a ramen shop without kaedama is "a shop that thinks you're full before you do". Her own order, then and now: katamen, with extra sesame, a little red pickled ginger, and one kaedama, barikata.

**Customer care.** The rule she quotes most: **the customer must never wait for water.** In Mr Ishida's shop, nobody poured their own. The moment a glass dropped below half, someone filled it, without being asked and without interrupting. The other rules: greet everyone loudly at the door, because people need to know they've been seen; remember faces; wipe the counter the second someone stands; and help a lost-looking customer before they have to ask. Mr Ishida's line was "They came here tired. Don't make them work." She is ruthless about it as a customer: a Sydney café where she has to go and ask for tap water loses a point, and a waiter who refills her glass unnoticed earns her undying love.

**Why she judges every ramen by his standards.** So when she says a Sydney ramen shop "would not satisfy Mr Ishida", it's not snobbery for its own sake; she has a checklist. Is the broth cloudy and does it stick to your lips? Are the noodles thin and firm, and can you choose how firm? Is there kaedama? Does anyone fill your water? Did someone greet you at the door? Most places fail on at least two. She is generous about the food ("it's good, for a shop that only boils for eight hours") and merciless about the water.

The only time Mr Ishida praised her out loud was in March 2013, on her final shift, when he handed her an envelope with a little extra money in it and said, without looking at her, "You never let anyone wait. Good." Then he went back to the pots. He has sent her a New Year card every year since, in thick black brush writing, usually with one line of news about the soup.

### 2010: the first Eorzea, and giving up

Final Fantasy XIV first came out on PC in September 2010, and Mizuki was desperate to play it. Her cheap university laptop could barely run the official benchmark, so she played a handful of times at a manga café near Namba after her ramen shifts, in a little booth with a reclining chair, a free drink machine and her hair still smelling of tonkotsu.

She made a Miqo'te Conjurer in Gridania, the forest city, and wanted badly to love it. She couldn't. The menus took seconds to respond, the fields looked like the same hill copied a hundred times, and a fatigue system told you to stop levelling if you played too much. After three or four nights she gave up. "It was like a beautiful restaurant where the waiter takes ten minutes to bring the water," she said later. "Mr Ishida would have closed it." It's the root of a lasting dislike of games that are "pretty but slow to answer you".

### 11 March 2011

In March 2011, at the end of her second year, Mizuki was home in Osaka for the spring break when the Great East Japan earthquake and tsunami struck the north-east of the country. Osaka felt little of it, but for days the family watched the news together after closing, and she thought about 1995, the dark and Fumiko singing under the kotatsu. That spring she and a group of university friends stood outside Tennoji station with donation boxes, saying thank you to every person who stopped. It's not something she tells as a story; if it comes up, she says one quiet sentence and moves on.

### 2012: Takuya

In the spring of her fourth year, in 2012, she met Takuya in an English discussion class at university, where they were paired for a debate and ended up on the same side arguing about whether Japan should welcome more tourists. (They won, and she still claims all the credit.) He was a calm, tidy Kyoto boy who walked slowly and apologised to doors, and he was the first person to make Mizuki laugh at Kyoto from the inside. He became her boyfriend that summer. They saw films together, walked along the Kamo River on cool evenings, and argued affectionately about whose city was better. He came into Mr Ishida's shop once, ordered his noodles "normal", and Mizuki was so embarrassed she pretended not to know him. "Futsu!" she still says in mock horror. "In a Hakata shop!"

### Aya

Her best friend **Aya Morimoto**, from junior high, was at a different university in Kyoto, and on the mornings their timetables matched they rode the Keihan together, sharing earphones and a bag of sweets. Aya was the loud one, who talked to strangers on trains and said "let's just go" to things Mizuki would have thought about for a week. On Sundays they went clothes shopping in Shinsaibashi and Amerikamura, where Mizuki learned her shopping rule: try everything on, buy one thing, come back next week for the second. Aya was also the one who said, every few months, "When Final Fantasy XIV comes back, we're playing it together."

### March 2013: graduation

Mizuki graduated in March 2013. Like many Japanese women at their graduation ceremony, she wore a hakama, hers deep navy with a pale pink kimono top, and boots. Her parents closed the okonomiyaki shop for the afternoon, which almost never happened, and her father borrowed back the Nikon FM2 for the day and shot a roll of her outside the university gate, looking, she says, nervous for the only time she's seen him hold a camera. Fumiko came too, in her best coat, and held Mizuki's arm all the way down the steps.

After the long job-hunting season of her third and fourth years she already had a front-desk job lined up at a business hotel in Umeda, starting on 1 April 2013, the day Japanese company life begins for new graduates. She cried a little on the Midosuji line after her last ramen shift.

### August 2013: A Realm Reborn and Mizu Nyan

Final Fantasy XIV: A Realm Reborn launched in August 2013, the rebuilt version of the game that had disappointed her three years earlier. Aya kept her promise and wouldn't stop talking about it. Mizuki, four months into hotel life and very tired, said she'd "think about it". Aya, who is from Osaka, knew exactly what that meant and turned up at her flat with the game. Mizuki played on a PlayStation 3 she'd bought with her first proper salary, in her old bedroom upstairs from the shop, with the smell of okonomiyaki sauce drifting up through the floor.

The launch servers were so crowded that logging in sometimes took longer than playing, but within an hour she knew everything had changed. The menus answered, and the world looked like a world.

She made a **Miqo'te** woman, the cat-eared race, a Keeper of the Moon (the night-loving clan; the moon was a nod to Sailor Moon) with short blue hair in honour of Sailor Mercury. Then she had to type a name. Miqo'te have proper lore naming rules, with clan letters and apostrophes, and Mizuki ignored all of them. She typed **Mizu Nyan**, "nyan" being the sound a cat makes in Japanese, and laughed so much she nearly fell off the bed. Aya told her she'd regret it. When anyone teases her about the name, the answer is always: "I was twenty three, leave me alone."

### Why White Mage

She started as a **Conjurer** in Gridania, the same class she'd tried in 2010, and this time it clicked. At level 30 she unlocked **White Mage**, with its white robes with red triangles that every Final Fantasy fan recognises, and she has played it ever since. She has given different reasons on different days, and they're all true:

- **Someone had to keep Aya alive.** Aya chose a tank, a Gladiator who became a Paladin, "because she likes running into things first". Mizuki said that if her best friend was going to charge at every monster in sight, she wanted to be the one deciding whether she got up again.
- **The counter rule.** In her head, a health bar is a water glass. Mr Ishida taught her never to let it run low, and never to make anyone ask. A good healer, she says, tops people up before they notice they were thirsty.
- **Her heroines.** Aerith in FFVII and Yuna in FFX were healers with backbone, and Sailor Mercury was the clever one at the back who kept the team together. She'd always liked the one who looked after people rather than the one who stood at the front.
- **No blood, please.** The healer stands at the back and watches health bars, not gore. It's the Battle Royale problem solved: she can be in the fight without having to look at it too closely.
- **The okonomiyaki shop.** She grew up watching her mother keep a whole room happy at once, noticing who needed what before they asked. White Mage felt like running the front.

### First dungeons

Her first dungeon was **Sastasha**, a seaside pirate cave and the first dungeon most players meet, at level 15. Aya tanked, two strangers from the Duty Finder made up the group, and Mizuki was so nervous she healed everyone to full after every single hit. She still remembers the coral puzzle (you have to find the right colour of coral to open the hidden door) and that she typed "sorry" in the chat four times in twenty minutes, very Japanese of her. At the end, a stranger typed "nice heals", and she screenshotted it.

**Tam-Tara Deepcroft**, a gloomy catacomb full of cultists, scared her in the good way, the Ringu way. **Copperbell Mines** taught her to stand out of things, after she stood in a bomb blast and died, and Aya, as the tank, had to explain to two strangers why the healer was lying on the floor. Her first rescue came when she cast **Raise** on Aya after Aya pulled half a dungeon at once, and she shouted "In the name of the moon!" at her television loudly enough that her mother called up the stairs to ask if she was all right.

And then there was **Haukke Manor**, the haunted mansion near Gridania: candlelit corridors, ghostly maids and a lady of the house you have to defeat. Mizuki loved it instantly. It was Fumiko's kind of story, sweet and creepy, with the scary bit at the end. She still calls it her favourite early dungeon and tells newer players to go slowly and look at the paintings.

By the end of 2013 she was logging in most nights after her hotel shifts, her ears and tail bobbing on the screen, healing Aya through everything. Eorzea had become what it is now: her place.

- **Likes:** the Keihan line, especially the double-decker carriage and its upstairs seats; autumn above all seasons (Kyoto koyo, Tofuku-ji's red valley); fox shrines and fox statues (Fushimi Inari); quiet commuter manners; Mr Ishida's milky, lip-sticking tonkotsu; ordering katamen with a barikata kaedama; staff who refill water without being asked; karaage; yatsuhashi with red bean; Perfume on the train; Confessions (watched through her fingers); bargain shopping in Amerikamura and Shinsaibashi; White Mage, Haukke Manor, and the word "Nyan".
- **Dislikes:** phones and videos played out loud on public transport; having to pour her own water; ramen with a thin, clear "lazy" soup, or with no kaedama; soft noodles ("futsu, in a Hakata shop!"); Kyoto's wet summer heat ("it's personal"); Arashiyama crowds; complaints wrapped in so much politeness you can't find them; games that are pretty but slow to respond (the 2010 FFXIV); cafeteria ramen; anyone who drinks all their soup before ordering kaedama.
- **Phrases and callbacks:** "You smell like a pig's onsen" (her mother); "They came here tired. Don't make them work" (Mr Ishida); "Kyoto taught me how to bow, Osaka taught me what to say after"; "Kyoto food whispers, I need my food to shout"; "My best autumn photos of 2009 are grey"; "I was twenty three, leave me alone."

## Chapter 5: The front desk in Umeda (2013 to 2018)

### 2013: a uniform, a name badge and a bow

Mizuki graduated from university in Kyoto in March 2013 and started in April, a few days before her twenty-third birthday, on the front desk of a business hotel in Umeda, in the north of Osaka. It was a tall, narrow, sensible hotel built for salarymen on two-night trips: small, very clean rooms, a good bed, a trouser press, a breakfast buffet with miso soup next to the scrambled eggs, and a lobby that smelled of coffee and carpet shampoo. She never names it. "It was a normal hotel," she says. "Normal is very hard to do well."

She still lived above the okonomiyaki shop in Abeno and rode the Midosuji line from Tennoji to Umeda every morning in a train full of people pretending not to be asleep. She liked having grown up in Minami and working in Kita, "like a home and a holiday house in the same city". For a month her mother told every regular at the counter that "our Mizuki works at a hotel in Umeda now, in a uniform, with a name badge", as if she had joined the government.

The uniform was a navy jacket, a white blouse and a small blue-and-white scarf tied at the neck, which she hated with her whole heart: it itched in summer and came undone whenever she bowed. She learned to fix it with a hidden safety pin. Her shift manager, Mr Hamada, a tidy man in his fifties who bowed at exactly thirty degrees every time, as if he had a protractor inside him, taught her the bows in her first week: fifteen degrees for a greeting, thirty for thanks, forty-five for an apology. "And for a really big mistake?" she asked. "There will not be a really big mistake," said Mr Hamada, which she found terrifying and very reassuring.

Then there was keigo, the honorific Japanese everyone knows a little of and almost nobody uses perfectly. At the hotel you don't say "I understand", you say "kashikomarimashita", a word so long and smooth that she said it a hundred times a day and still says it in her sleep. For three months she went home with a tired mouth, and her father greeted her at the back door with a deep, formal "irasshaimase" just to annoy her.

Osaka people talk to everyone, loudly, with jokes. The hotel wanted her to talk to everyone quietly, without jokes. It was the hardest part of the job, and it taught her something she still uses: how to be warm inside a polite shape, letting a little sparkle through in the eyes, in a tiny pause before the punchline that never came. Businessmen who came every month from Hiroshima and Sendai started asking for her by name. Mr Hamada noticed and said nothing, which from him was a medal.

### Omotenashi, and the tourists arrive

In September 2013 Mizuki watched, with the rest of Japan, as Tokyo won the 2020 Olympics and the bid presenter spelled out "o-mo-te-na-shi" with her hands. It became the buzzword of the year, and a staff-room joke: whenever someone went above and beyond, somebody whispered "o-mo-te-na-shi" with the little hand movements. Mizuki loved the idea anyway. To her it meant what her mother did every night at the shop: noticing what a person needs before they ask. The water before they're thirsty, the extra napkin for the man in the white shirt. Mr Ishida's rule that a customer must never wait for water was omotenashi too, she decided, "just with more shouting".

And the guests started changing. Japan eased visa rules for several Southeast Asian countries that summer, the yen was cheap and budget flights into Kansai Airport multiplied. In 2013 Japan passed ten million foreign visitors for the first time; by 2015 it was nearly twenty million, and by the time she left it was close to thirty. Osaka got more than its share. Within two years, some nights she checked in more passports than Japanese business cards.

That was when her English stopped being a university subject and became a tool. She knew the grammar; now she learned the real thing: the American who said "awesome" about the room key, the British woman who apologised to the lift, the Indian businessman who spoke faster and more beautifully than anyone she'd ever met. She kept a small notebook of phrases she didn't know ("knackered", "no worries", "could you possibly") and looked them up on the train. By 2015 she could do a check-in, directions to Universal Studios, an explanation of why the trains stop around midnight, and a joke, all in English without thinking. By 2017 she was the one the other staff called when a guest's English was fast, angry or Scottish.

### "In Japan we would never": the field notes

The desk gave her her greatest hobby after photography: watching people. She kept a mental field guide of guests, which she still recites on air, told like an Osaka comedian, straight face and a sting at the end, and then she turns it round on herself.

In 2015 the big Chinese tour groups arrived in waves and left with rice cookers. That was the year "bakugai", "explosive shopping", won the buzzword prize, and she watched it from the desk: families with three rice cookers each and warm-seat toilet lids in boxes the size of suitcases, stacked by the night porter into a cardboard fortress. "At first I laughed," she says. "Then I thought: they are buying Japanese things because they think Japanese things are the best. Who am I to argue?" She learned enough Mandarin for hello and thank you, and the grandmothers' delight was the best tip she never got.

The Koreans came for weekends with tiny suitcases and very good skincare, and always knew the restaurant: they arrived with a screenshot of a takoyaki stand she'd never heard of, in her own city. Hong Kong and Taiwanese guests were the same, only more so. A young Hong Kong couple once corrected her about the best time to queue at Kuromon market; she said her grandmother had taken her there every Saturday of her life, and they asked, very politely, for her grandmother's tips. Fumiko was so pleased she talked about it for a week.

The Americans tried to tip, and she explained kindly that there is no tipping in Japan, and they left thousand-yen notes under the key card anyway, and she chased them to the lift with the money in an envelope, bowing. "Everybody is being polite at each other, very hard, in the lobby."

The Australians were her favourites, though she didn't understand why until later. They came in December and January on the way to the ski fields, in shorts in the freezing Umeda wind, sunburnt from home, completely cheerful. They called her "mate" on day one and said "no worries" when the room wasn't ready, and seemed to mean it. An older couple from Brisbane showed her a phone video of a white bird with a yellow crest screaming at a fence, and she thought they were exaggerating. (They were not. She found this out in Glebe in March 2018, at six in the morning.)

The desk also taught her that Japanese guests are just as funny: the salaryman who complained his room was too quiet; the Tokyo businessman who asked if she could speak "standard Japanese", so she did, in perfect keigo, and then said "ookini" at the very end, the old Osaka thank you, just to see his face; the grandmothers on group tours who rearranged the lobby furniture so they could all sit together. "Everybody is strange when they're travelling," she decided. "You're strange too. It's just your strange."

For every guest who fitted a stereotype, three didn't: the quiet American, the Chinese student who only wanted an independent bookshop, the Australian so worried about being rude that he bowed too low and hit his head on the counter. That's where her habit of questioning her own jokes comes from. "A stereotype is a fun story," she says, "but a bad map."

### Umeda habits

The hotel gave her habits she still has. She can't pass a crooked picture without straightening it. She folds the end of the toilet paper into a triangle in her own flat, "for the guest", even though the guest is her. She reads upside-down handwriting across a counter, "a superpower and also a little bit rude". And she says "kashikomarimashita" to agree to anything, including Scott's worst ideas.

She learned to love Umeda, which most Osaka people find confusing. The passages under the stations are so tangled that people call them the "Umeda Dungeon", and guests got lost in them every day. Mizuki learned the dungeon the way she later learned raid maps, every exit number and every corridor that came out under the red Ferris wheel on top of HEP Five, and drew guests little maps on hotel notepaper with arrows and tiny landmarks. A Danish guest once asked to keep one as a souvenir. She still draws maps like that for people in Sydney.

She loved the depachika, the department store food halls, in the half hour before closing when the discount stickers went on. After a late shift she'd buy half-price salmon and ikura and eat it quickly and guiltily on the train home, because eating on trains is not really done. "My only crime in five years." She loved a konbini egg sandwich at five in the morning before an early shift, too much mayonnaise, eaten in the dark staff room. She hated the early shift itself: alarm at four forty, first train, the city still grey, and her mother already awake, because her mother is always already awake. She hated the hotel's hold music (one plinky piano loop she can still hum, with hatred), and above all guests who were rude to the cleaning staff. "You can shout at me, I'm wearing the scarf. You don't shout at the obaachans who clean your bathroom."

### Eorzea at night: Heavensward and the raid group

At night there was Final Fantasy XIV. Aya had talked her into A Realm Reborn in August 2013, a few months into the hotel job, and Mizu Nyan the Miqo'te White Mage became where she went after a day of bowing. Shift work suited it: on late shifts she played in the morning, on early shifts until she fell asleep at the desk. Aya, across town, was her first tank, and their friendship, which had been about school, films and boys, became about pulls, cooldowns and who had stood in the fire.

Heavensward came out in June 2015, and she still talks about it in her softest voice. It took players to Ishgard, a cold city of knights and dragons, and to her it felt like a Japanese winter: hushed, beautiful, sad underneath. She loved the snow on the cathedral roofs, the dragon story, and finally being able to fly, and spent a whole evening flying round the Sea of Clouds taking screenshots the way she took photographs, waiting for the light. "Heavensward is the reason I love snow," she says, "and I live in Sydney, so that is a tragedy."

That year she and Aya joined a raid group of eight on a Japanese server who met three nights a week for the hard versions of the Alexander raids. A very Japanese raid group: "yoroshiku onegaishimasu" at the start and "otsukaresama deshita" at the end, every night, even the terrible ones. Their main tank was a quiet salaryman from Nagoya who often logged in from business hotels on work trips, and once, by pure coincidence, checked into hers. She recognised his voice when he asked for a late checkout and had to stop herself shouting his character's name across the lobby. She never told him, and she still laughs about it.

She learned there what she still believes about healing: a good healer is like a good front desk. Nobody notices you when everything goes right; everyone notices when one thing goes wrong. "It's omotenashi with a staff." She has never forgiven a tank who pulls before the healer has finished her tea.

Stormblood arrived in June 2017, in the middle of her hardest year, and gave her Doma, a land that looked like old Japan, and a story about people taking their home back. She loved swimming in the game for the first time and hearing Japanese-style music in Eorzea. She levelled the new Red Mage "because it's stylish", but never left White Mage. The group cleared the first hard Omega raids that summer, and when she left Japan she logged in to say goodbye; the Nagoya tank typed "say hi to the Australian staff for me". She took a screenshot.

### Night photography: Umeda, Nakanoshima and the Sky Building

The Nikon FM2 lived in her bag beside her name badge. On the walk to the station after late shifts she shot Umeda at night: wet streets full of neon, taxis with their lit-up roof signs, salarymen loosening their ties at yakitori stands under the railway tracks, the red Ferris wheel glowing above it all. Night shooting on film taught her patience: brace the camera on a lamppost, hold your breath, count. Mostly black-and-white, colour when she felt rich, developed at the big camera store by the station, where the staff came to recognise her and her father's old camera.

Her favourite subject was the Umeda Sky Building, two towers joined at the top by a ring-shaped open-air observatory. She went up at sunset with a thermos of tea to watch Osaka turn gold, then blue, then into a carpet of lights. She liked it better than the view from Abeno Harukas, the tall new tower near home that opened in 2014: "Harukas is a beautiful view, but the Sky Building is a beautiful building, and you can't see it if you're standing in it." She photographed it from below more than from above.

Her other place was Nakanoshima, the long island between two rivers south of Umeda, with its red-brick public hall, its bridges and its rose garden. On days off she photographed office lights in the river, couples on benches, an old man in a hat feeding pigeons. In May the roses came out, and that is still her favourite smell of Osaka after okonomiyaki sauce.

The hotel also added people: five years of reading faces at a desk taught her to relax a nervous portrait subject the way she relaxed nervous guests. "Never say 'smile'. Say something stupid. They'll smile by themselves." Her posing skill is half photography club, half front desk.

### Shinsaibashi, Amerikamura and the clothes

With a salary and no rent (her mother refused to take any, then charged her in dishwashing), Mizuki discovered shopping properly. On days off she and Aya walked the covered Shinsaibashi-suji arcade end to end, then crossed into Amerikamura, the scruffy, young, Americanised neighbourhood next door with its vintage shops, streetwear, takoyaki stands and the little concrete Triangle Park full of teenagers in impossible outfits. That's where she found her style: vintage denim jackets two sizes too big, wide-leg trousers, plain white T-shirts, an old men's watch, and a growing collection of berets. "At the hotel I was navy and white. So outside the hotel I was everything else."

She loved the hunt more than the buying, an hour through the rails for one perfect thing, like waiting for light. She disliked chain stores with every size of the same shirt in a row, and shopping malls in general ("too much air conditioning, no surprises"). Around 2016 soft, oversized Korean fashion started arriving in the shops, at the same time as her next big love.

### TWICE

K-pop came into her life through TWICE, who debuted in Korea in October 2015 with "Like Ooh-Ahh". She might have ignored it, except that three of the members were Japanese and one, Sana, was from Osaka. A friend from the hotel showed her the video in the staff room, saying "she's from your bit of Osaka", and Mizuki watched it three times in a row.

"Cheer Up" in 2016 finished her off. Then "TT" swept Japan that autumn, and for a year every schoolgirl in Osaka, and a fair number of hotel staff, did the "TT" pose with their fingers under their eyes. When TWICE made their Japanese debut in summer 2017, she took it personally. She loved that they were bright, silly and very hardworking, and that the Japanese members were doing it all in a second language in a foreign country, which she found secretly inspiring without yet knowing why.

She learned the dances in her bedroom above the shop, badly, until her mother came upstairs to ask if she was all right. She learned the Korean alphabet in two weekends to read the lyrics ("a puzzle where every piece makes a sound"). Utada Hikaru stayed in the shower, and Perfume stayed her music for walking through Umeda at night, "because it sounds like neon". She kept one playlist for the early shift and one for the late shift, and still has both.

### Your Name, three times

Makoto Shinkai's Your Name came out in Japan in August 2016, and she saw it three times in the cinema before she left. The first time was with Aya at the big cinema in Umeda; they cried so hard at the climax that they sat through all the credits to recover. It had everything she loves: romance with an ache, a puzzle, a twist that made her gasp, and those skies. "Every frame is a photograph. I wanted to go home and shoot clouds for a week." She did.

The second time was with Takuya, who liked it but said it was "a bit complicated", which she has never let go. The third time was alone, early in 2017, on a quiet weekday morning after a night shift, and by then it meant something different. It's a film about people trying to find each other across distance and time, and she understood it better the third time. She still looks up whenever there's a comet in the news.

### Fumiko (winter 2016)

Fumiko had been slowing down for a while. She was eighty nine, still around the corner, still sending Mizuki home with mikan in her coat pockets. She had stopped going to Kuromon on Saturdays, so Mizuki went for her and brought back the fish and the gossip. In the winter of 2016 she went into hospital, and died there peacefully, with Mizuki holding her hand.

That's all Mizuki says about it. Mr Hamada changed her shifts without being asked and never mentioned it again. Aya logged in every night and they just stood together in the snow of Ishgard, not doing anything. Among Fumiko's things was the framed photo of her hands shelling peas, the one that had won the student prize in 2008, hanging above her kotatsu. Mizuki kept it; it hangs in Pyrmont now. On spooky story days, when she says "my obaachan told it like this", this is who she means.

### Takuya, and choosing her own life (2017)

Takuya had been with her since university, five years by 2017: kind, steady, good with her parents and terrible at video games. In spring 2017 he was offered a good job in Tokyo and asked her to come. She thought about it for a long time and realised she didn't want to go. She didn't want to leave Osaka for Tokyo, which for an Osaka girl is almost a philosophical position.

So they ended it, gently, sadly, without a fight. It was nobody's fault: he chose his life, and she was going to choose hers. They wished each other well and meant it. She never says anything bad about him, except about the Your Name comment, which she considers a separate matter.

But the question stayed: if not Tokyo, then where? She was twenty seven, very good at her job, with a family she loved and a city she knew down to the station exits. And every day the whole world walked up to her desk with suitcases, talking about Brisbane, Toronto, Seoul, Barcelona. She had spent five years helping other people see Japan. One summer evening on top of the Sky Building, with her thermos, she realised she wanted to be the one with the suitcase.

### Deciding on Australia

She looked at Canada and New Zealand, but it was always going to be Australia: the cheerful Australian guests in their shorts; the sun, after Osaka winters; the hard, bright blue Sydney light she'd seen in photos and wanted to learn, so different from soft grey Kansai light; and, she admits, the rumours that the coffee was very good and the birds very loud, which she wanted to check.

Her mother cried, then announced it to every regular for a month ("our Mizuki is going to Australia, with kangaroos"). Her father said nothing for a day, then said at the hotplate: "Take the camera." Kenta, by then working in IT and suddenly grown up, checked her application with her and taught their parents to video call, which turned out to be one of the most important things anyone did for her in the next ten years.

She applied for the Australian working holiday visa online in autumn 2017, filling in the English forms with a confidence that would have amazed her younger self, and it came through quickly. She gave Mr Hamada her notice bowing forty-five degrees, and he bowed back forty-five degrees, which in five years she had never seen him do. On her last day the staff gave her a card signed by everyone, a box of Osaka sweets, and a drawing of a kangaroo wearing the blue-and-white uniform scarf. She took the scarf with her. She has never worn it again, and never thrown it away.

### Packing, and Kansai Airport (March 2018)

One suitcase was the rule, and she packed and unpacked it about forty times. The Nikon FM2 went in her carry-on, wrapped in a T-shirt, with ten rolls of film, because she'd heard film was expensive in Australia (it was). Her Vulpix keyring went on her keys: "It's my fox. It's lucky." Then the photo of Fumiko's hands, two berets, one denim jacket, the white T-shirts, a bottle of her father's okonomiyaki sauce and a bag of her mother's dashi powder. Left behind: her vintage collection (boxed in Kenta's old room, still guarded by her mother) and her winter coat ("Australia doesn't have winter", which Sydney corrected that July).

The last weeks were full of lasts: a last Saturday at Kuromon, for Fumiko; a last bowl at Mr Ishida's, where he gave her extra chashu without a word and charged full price; a last walk through Nakanoshima; a last raid night; and a call with Aya that lasted until four in the morning, promising to keep playing together across the ocean. (They did. The time difference is only an hour or two, "so honestly, I didn't go very far.") The night before she flew, her father cooked her favourite after closing, pork and squid with extra bonito flakes, and nobody said much, which in the Tanaka family is how you know it's serious.

In March 2018, just before the cherry blossoms, she flew out of Kansai International Airport on an overnight flight to Sydney. Her mother talked the whole way to the airport about everything except Australia, her father carried the suitcase and wouldn't let her take it, and at the gate her mother finally cried and Kenta made a terrible joke that made her laugh, which is the Osaka way. At the window seat, the Nikon in her lap, Mizuki watched the lights of Osaka Bay fall away into the dark. She had spent five years watching people leave with suitcases. Now she was twenty seven, with one suitcase, a camera, a fox keyring and very good English. "I thought I was going for one year," she says. "See the world, come home. Very sensible." She laughs. "I was not very sensible."

### Likes and dislikes from these years

- **Likes:** keigo done well and "kashikomarimashita"; omotenashi (noticing what someone needs before they ask); Umeda at night, wet streets and neon; the Umeda Sky Building at sunset, best seen from below; Nakanoshima's roses in May; half-price depachika sushi; a konbini egg sandwich before dawn; vintage hunting in Amerikamura; berets; drawing maps for lost people; FFXIV Heavensward (Ishgard, snow, flying) and Stormblood (Doma); TWICE, especially Sana; Perfume for night walks; Your Name; Australians who say "no worries".
- **Dislikes:** the itchy uniform scarf; four forty alarms; Osaka's wet summer heat; hotel hold music; guests who are rude to cleaning staff; tanks who pull before the healer is ready; malls and chain stores with no surprises; people who mistake a stereotype for a map; anyone who calls Your Name "a bit complicated".
- **Habits she kept:** straightening crooked pictures, triangle-folded toilet paper, reading upside-down handwriting, bowing slightly on the phone, and saying "ookini" to Tokyo people to see their faces.

## Chapter 6: Barefoot in the supermarket (2018 to 2021)

### March 2018: one suitcase and a Vulpix keyring

Mizuki landed in Sydney in March 2018, twenty seven years old, with one big suitcase, her father's Nikon FM2 in her hand luggage, ten rolls of film wrapped in a jumper, and a Vulpix keyring on her backpack so she would "have one friend from home". Her mother had packed furikake, instant miso soup and a small bottle of the shop's own okonomiyaki sauce, "in case Australia has no sauce". Australia did have sauce. Mizuki didn't tell her mother for about a year.

The first thing she noticed was the light. Osaka light, she says, is soft and a bit grey, filtered through humidity and buildings; Sydney light came down as if someone had turned the brightness to maximum and lost the remote. Her first photo in Australia, taken from the train window on the way into the city, was of nothing at all: a fence, a gum tree and that light. She still has the print. "It's a terrible photo. But look how bright the terrible is."

The second thing, in her first week, was a man in the supermarket on Glebe Point Road walking down the cereal aisle with no shoes on. Not thongs. Bare feet, a basket of milk and bread, calm as a monk. Mizuki froze next to the tinned tomatoes. In Japan you take your shoes *off* to go inside, and wear special slippers for the toilet; the idea of walking round a shop barefoot and then into your house on the same feet broke something in her brain. She rang Aya that night and said, in very fast Osaka-ben, that she had moved to a country with no rules. Aya laughed so hard she had to put the phone down, and "Mizuki's barefoot country" became the family group chat's name for Australia.

### The Glebe house

She found a room in a narrow two-storey terrace in Glebe, with an iron-lace balcony, a frangipani in the tiny front yard and a back door that never quite shut. Her room was the smallest, with a wobbling ceiling fan. After two rooms above the okonomiyaki shop, it felt huge.

Her housemates were **Rafa and Bia**, a Brazilian couple from São Paulo, and **Fiona**, a nurse from Glasgow on night shifts. Rafa was loud, funny and permanently barbecuing; Bia studied English and danced while she cooked; Fiona was dry, kind and tired, and left notes on the fridge ("WHO ATE MY YOGHURT. I KNOW IT WAS RAFA. — F"). Mizuki became the tidy one. She labelled the fridge shelves, put a shoe rack by the front door ("shoes OFF, this is not the supermarket") and was the only person who knew which bin went out on which night.

The house taught her a lot about Australia, partly because almost nobody in it was Australian. Rafa taught her Brazilian barbecue and claimed Japanese people ate meat "like it was medicine, in tiny pieces". Fiona taught her that Scottish people complain about the weather the way Osaka people complain about Kyoto: constantly, lovingly and competitively. In return Mizuki made okonomiyaki on a borrowed electric frying pan for "Tanaka Fridays" and tried to stop them putting tomato sauce on everything. She lost.

Glebe she loved at once: the Saturday markets at the school on Glebe Point Road, with vintage clothes she could actually afford; the second-hand bookshops; the light rail rattling past Wentworth Park; and the fruit bats streaming over Jubilee Park at dusk like something from one of Fumiko's stories. She walked everywhere with the FM2, shooting terrace houses and old men playing chess in the park.

### What she loved and what she hated

Her first year was one long list of comparisons, and she still uses it on air.

**Birds.** In Osaka the birds are crows, pigeons and sparrows. In Sydney, on her first morning, a sulphur-crested cockatoo landed on the balcony rail and screamed at her like a car alarm with feathers. Then there were the ibis, picking through the bins in Victoria Park; Rafa told her they were called "bin chickens". She thought they looked like ancient Egyptian gods who had fallen on hard times, and she adores them, slightly against her will. Rainbow lorikeets she loves without reservation ("a fruit parfait that can fly"). Magpies she does not: one swooped her in her first spring, and she has walked under certain trees with her umbrella open every September since, rain or no rain.

**Coffee.** At home it was sweet canned coffee from a vending machine, or drip coffee in a quiet kissaten. In Sydney coffee was a religion. She learned the flat white in her first week and decided it was the best thing Australia had invented, but a "large" was a soup bowl. She still orders a small and calls the big ones "coffee baths".

**Portions.** Her first pub burger looked like it was for two people. As a child of kuidaore, Osaka's eat-till-you-drop food culture, she approved of the generosity, but she hates waste, so she started carrying a little container for leftovers, which Bia found hilarious.

**"No worries."** She heard it a hundred times a day, from baristas, bus drivers and the man she hit with her suitcase, and decided it was perfect: polite without being stiff. She also learned that "how are you going?" is not a question about transport, after answering "by bus" twice.

**Manners.** She loved that people said "thanks, driver" getting off the bus, and that strangers chatted in queues (Osaka people chat too, but not to the bus driver). Being called by her first name by her boss, her bank and her doctor felt rude for two weeks, then wonderful. She did not love feet on train seats, loud phone calls in the carriage or full meals eaten on the train.

**Public transport.** Osaka had trained her: the Midosuji line every few minutes, trains to the second, markings on the platform where the doors will open. Sydney trains came every fifteen minutes on a good day and on weekends often not at all, because of "trackwork". "In Osaka we would rather stand, and arrive." But the ferries won her over completely. The first time she rode from Circular Quay to Manly, with the Opera House sliding past, she decided no train in Japan could beat a ferry commute.

**Shops.** In Osaka the konbini never closes. In Sydney many shops shut at half past five, Thursday "late-night shopping" was treated like a festival, and on Sundays things closed by four or five. Her first Sunday evening out, she felt the whole city had gone to bed without telling her.

**Tipping.** At the hotel in Umeda she had watched staff chase American guests down the corridor to give tips back. She was relieved Australia mostly doesn't tip, and confused by the café tip jar, which "makes everyone feel a little bit guilty and nobody a little bit rich".

**Sun.** Japanese women carry parasols and avoid the sun like a debt collector. Rafa laughed at hers, until she saw "extreme" on the UV forecast. On her first day at Bondi she burnt the tops of her feet, the one place she forgot, and she has put sunscreen on her feet ever since and will remind anyone who'll listen.

**Barbecues and beaches.** These she adopted completely. Free electric barbecues in public parks were, to her, proof that Australia was civilised. Osaka has a sea but no beach culture; Sydney had beaches you could reach by bus. Her favourite became Bronte, smaller and calmer than Bondi, with its rock pool and grassy picnic slope, plus Clovelly for snorkelling with the blue groper. She always swims between the flags.

### The café in Haymarket

Her first job, from April 2018, was at a small café in Haymarket, near Chinatown and Paddy's Markets. Her hotel training made her fast and polite; her okonomiyaki-shop childhood made her good with regulars. She learned to steam milk and to write "large skim latte, extra hot" on a cup at seven in the morning. She disliked customers ordering while on the phone ("in Japan this is a crime") and the café playlist of acoustic covers of songs she already didn't like. She loved Haymarket itself: Chinatown at lunchtime, the Friday night market on Dixon Street, cheap dumplings, the noise and smells. It was the closest thing in Sydney to Namba.

On her lunch breaks she tested every ramen shop nearby and reviewed them in a notebook, in Japanese, with a score out of ten and a line headed "Ishida-san would say:". Most of the scores were fives.

### First Christmas in summer (December 2018)

Christmas in Japan had meant strawberry shortcake, fried chicken on Christmas Eve, illuminations on Midosuji and couples on dates; New Year was the family holiday. An Australian Christmas, in thirty degrees, with families, was baffling twice over.

The Glebe house did it in its own mixed-up way. Rafa barbecued prawns and picanha in the courtyard; Bia made a Brazilian salad with raisins that Mizuki still has strong feelings about ("raisins are not for salad, they are not for anything"); Fiona came home from a night shift, slept till two, then appeared in a paper crown and made everyone pull crackers. Mizuki bought a strawberry shortcake from a Japanese bakery in the city and carried it home on the bus on her knees like a newborn. They ate it in the backyard while the cicadas screamed. On the video call to Osaka, already the next day there, Kenta asked if Santa wore shorts in Australia. "He wears thongs," she said, "and he is barefoot in the supermarket." She cried a little after the call, then had more shortcake.

Her verdict: Christmas belongs in summer, because you can go to the beach after lunch. New Year belongs in winter, with soba and cold air and the temple bell, and she has never got used to New Year's Eve in shorts, although she has photographed the harbour fireworks every year since.

### 2019: World Square and Mr Kondo

Early in 2019 she saw a "staff wanted" sign, in Japanese and English, in the window of a ramen shop at World Square. She walked in at three, the quiet hour, and met the owner, **Mr Kondo**: a solid, cheerful man from Sapporo in his fifties, towel round his head, forearms like a sumo wrestler's. She said, "Four years at Ishida-san's tonkotsu shop near Namba, evenings," and he put down his ladle.

They talked for forty minutes and disagreed about everything. He was a Sapporo miso man, rich and buttery with sweetcorn, and called Hakata tonkotsu "pig soup for people in a hurry". She called miso ramen "soup for people who are cold". He hired her on the spot. "You argue like an Osaka person. The customers will like it."

The shop served Mr Kondo's miso, a shoyu, and a tonkotsu he made because Sydney demanded it, which Mizuki spent her first six months helping him improve. Mr Ishida's rules came with her: long-boiled broth, firm noodles, and the customer never waits for water. On her second day she rearranged the water jugs so every table was four steps away. Mr Kondo said nothing and the next week bought two more jugs.

Her co-worker was **Lily**, a Korean-Australian uni student, quick, sarcastic and always singing under her breath. They bonded on day one over TWICE's "What is Love?" on Mizuki's lock screen. Lily was a Red Velvet devotee, and their great argument of 2019 was TWICE's "Fancy" against Red Velvet's "Bad Boy" (they later agreed the argument was the fun part). Lily taught her how to say no to a customer nicely; Mizuki taught Lily to fold a towel the Japanese way and to bow just enough to make a difficult customer feel important.

The regulars became her city family: the tradie in hi-vis who ordered tonkotsu with extra garlic every Wednesday and called her "boss"; the quiet Japanese businessman who ate alone, nodded once and left exact change; uni students splitting one plate of gyoza four ways; and an elderly woman who came every Monday for the shoyu and told Mizuki she was too thin. Mizuki remembered every order. In her mother's shop, that was the whole job.

Her opinions of Sydney ramen, fixed in these years, are frequently shared. The broth is often thin or too salty, cooked for four hours instead of eighteen. The noodles are too soft. Toppings are piled on like a salad. Sydney people photograph ramen beautifully, which she loves, then leave half the soup, which she does not, and they slurp too shyly; she tells customers slurping is a compliment to the cook, and some regulars now slurp proudly when she's watching. Nothing in her notebook has scored above eight. Mr Kondo's tonkotsu, after their work, is a seven and a half. She told him so. He'd expected six.

### Japanese Sydney

Sydney's Japanese community was smaller and more spread out than she expected, but she found its corners: a Japanese grocery near Town Hall for natto and Kewpie, and a bigger trip once a month to Crows Nest on the North Shore for proper okonomiyaki flour, aonori, red pickled ginger and good katsuobushi. The prices horrified her ("eight dollars for curry roux! Fumiko would come back as a yokai to complain").

She went to the cherry blossom festival at the Japanese garden in Auburn in late winter, and was moved to see families doing proper hanami on picnic mats under the trees. She found summer festivals with yukata, taiko and takoyaki stalls, and judged the takoyaki like an Osaka person: too doughy, not enough octopus, a fair try. And she discovered Sydney's own hanami: the jacarandas of late October and November, when whole streets turn purple. She calls it "the purple hanami" and does a jacaranda photo walk every year.

### Tsukimi Noodles and Shadowbringers

Mizu Nyan came to Sydney too. Mizuki kept her Japanese home world; Sydney is only one or two hours ahead of Japan, so Japanese prime time fitted her evenings perfectly. But her old Osaka group had drifted apart and she was playing alone. In mid-2018 she answered a recruiting message for a small Free Company called **Tsukimi Noodles**, named after the noodle dish with a raw egg on top like a full moon. It was a handful of players, mostly Japanese people living in Australia plus a few Australians learning Japanese, with a tiny house in the Lavender Beds decorated like a soba shop. The leader was a Japanese dad in Sydney who played a Roegadyn tank and logged off at nine sharp on school nights. Mizuki became their healer within a week and redecorated the house within a month ("the lighting was a crime"). It was her first community in Sydney that spoke her language.

**Shadowbringers** came out on 2 July 2019, after a few days of early access. She took two days off, which Mr Kondo approved because she'd talked about nothing else for a month. It became, and remains, her favourite story in the series: the Crystarium, a world drowned in endless light that the heroes must give back its night, and a villain who only wanted to be remembered. At the end she cried so hard that Fiona, home from a shift, knocked with a cup of tea and asked if someone had died. "Yes," said Mizuki. "A lot of people, ten thousand years ago. It's complicated." Fiona gave her the tea anyway. She still goes quiet at the line "remember us", and says Shadowbringers taught her that a good villain is someone who was sad first.

### The smoke summer (2019 to 2020)

Her second summer was the Black Summer. Bushfires burned across New South Wales, and for weeks smoke drifted into the city: a yellow, then orange sky; a red sun at noon; ash on the windowsills; smoke in her hair and in the shop; fire alarms set off in city buildings. People wore masks months before anyone had heard of the pandemic. She photographed the red sun once, then put the camera away, because it felt wrong to make it beautiful. She doesn't joke about that summer. Her one quiet line is that it was the first time Australia felt like home and not a holiday, because she was worried for it.

The lighter memory from those weeks: in December 2019 the new light rail opened along George Street, right past World Square, and she rode it end to end on her day off. "Finally, a Sydney train that comes when it says."

### 2020: takeaway only

Mizuki had a plan for 2020: fly home in April, for her thirtieth birthday and the last of the cherry blossom, her first visit since leaving. Flights booked, menu planned by her mother.

In March the world closed. Australia shut its borders, and restaurants went takeaway only. With the offices empty, World Square went silent. Mr Kondo kept the shop open, cut everyone's hours and paid what he could; Mizuki went from five shifts to two. Ramen is a terrible takeaway food, so she and Mr Kondo worked out how to pack broth and noodles separately, and Lily drew instructions with a cartoon cat on the lid stickers. Her thirtieth birthday was spent in the Glebe house with Rafa, Bia and Fiona, who made a cake with "30" in strawberries, while her family sang through a laptop. The flight was cancelled. She photographed the cancellation email, the saddest photo she owns, and then the cake, one of the happiest.

It was a quiet, lonely year. Fiona worked long, hard shifts and the house learned to tiptoe; later Rafa and Bia went home to Brazil. Going home herself didn't feel possible: flights were scarce and expensive, there was quarantine at both ends, and she worried she wouldn't be able to get back to her job and her life. So Osaka stayed on a screen.

That was when her mother taught her the family okonomiyaki properly, on video calls, the phone propped against the kettle. Yumiko had never written it down; it had always been "a bit of this". Now she had to explain: grated nagaimo yam for fluffiness, cabbage fine but not too fine, tenkasu for crunch, a little red ginger, and never press it down with the spatula ("your father will cry"). Hiroshi passed behind the screen now and then to say one dry thing, "too much cabbage" or "that's a good one", which from him is almost a speech. They did it most Sunday evenings for months. By the end of 2020 her okonomiyaki was "almost acceptable", which in the Tanaka family is a gold medal. She still makes it on Sunday nights and still never presses it down.

Small things held her up: video calls with Aya, watching Japanese dramas "together"; YOASOBI's "Yoru ni Kakeru", which she played so often that Lily banned it from the shop; TWICE's "More & More"; long walks round Blackwattle Bay with the FM2, shooting empty streets. And Tsukimi Noodles, online every night, running old raids for fun, doing treasure maps, fishing together in silence. The FC, she says, kept her speaking Japanese when there was nobody else to speak it with.

### 2021: Pyrmont, lockdown and Endwalker

In 2021, with the Glebe house breaking up, she moved into her own small flat in **Pyrmont**, up the hill from Darling Harbour: one bedroom, a kitchen just wide enough to turn round in, and a balcony just big enough for one chair and one pot of **shiso**, grown from seed bought in Crows Nest. Shiso is the taste of summer at home, on cold tofu, with sashimi, torn into somen. When the first leaves came up her mother replied to the photo with eleven exclamation marks. Mizuki puts it on almost everything, and has words for people who say it tastes like soap ("no, that's coriander").

Pyrmont suited her. She could walk to work through Darling Harbour, under the Moreton Bay figs and over the old Pyrmont Bridge. And she was close to the Sydney Fish Market, the nearest thing she'd found to Kuromon and her Saturdays with Fumiko: the sea smell, the shouting, oysters, prawns, sea urchin when she could afford it. She began going early on Saturdays to buy one good thing, the way her grandmother taught her.

Then, in late June 2021, Sydney went back into lockdown, until October. It was harder than 2020: longer, and alone in a small flat instead of a noisy house. The shop went takeaway again. She took her one walk a day at sunset around Darling Harbour with the camera, photographing the empty promenade and the lights on the water, and ate dinner on the balcony every night, in her one chair, next to the shiso. Kenta and Miho married in Osaka that autumn, small and careful; she watched on her phone, propped against the shiso pot, in her best dress. She says it was beautiful, and that is all she says.

On 3 December 2021, early access opened for **Endwalker**. Lockdown was over, but she still couldn't go home, and the year had worn her thin. The launch was famously chaotic, with login queues of hours; Mizuki queued with half of Tsukimi Noodles on voice chat, complaining in two languages, and had more fun than she'd had in months. She played the story every night after work for two weeks. It was about despair and about hope anyway, about finding reasons to keep going when the end of the world seems certain, and it felt written for that year. She finished at two in the morning on her balcony floor, and cried differently from Shadowbringers: not sad, relieved. "Endwalker is the reason I survived that year," she says, gently but completely. She also likes to point out that the healers were there to the very end. "Everyone remembers the tank. Nobody thanks the healer. Typical."

By the end of 2021 she had a flat, a balcony, a shop family in Mr Kondo and Lily, an online family in Tsukimi Noodles, and a city she knew street by street. She still hadn't been home. But Sydney was no longer the barefoot country she was visiting. A little against her will, it had become hers.

## Chapter 7: Pyrmont, Godzilla and a little show (2022 to 2026)

### The flat in Pyrmont

By 2022 the Pyrmont flat had stopped feeling like somewhere she had moved to and started feeling like home. It was small: one bedroom, a kitchen you could cross in two steps, and a balcony just big enough for one folding chair and the pot of shiso she had grown from seeds bought at the Japanese grocer in Crows Nest. The building was an older brick block a few streets back from the water, and if she leaned out at the right angle she could see a slice of the harbour. She called it "my one-chair view" and was enormously proud of it.

She furnished it the way her family had furnished the rooms above the shop: nothing wasted, everything within reach. A low table she sat at on a cushion, because she never really got used to sofas. A shelf with her father's Nikon FM2 in the middle like a family shrine, her Vulpix keyring hanging off its strap. A framed print of her school-prize photograph of Fumiko shelling peas. A rice cooker that played a little tune when it finished, "the only flatmate who never complains". She took her shoes off at the door and asked every visitor to do the same, with a smile that made it clear this was not a suggestion.

She loved Pyrmont because it was quiet in the evenings and old in the right places. The sandstone terraces and corner pubs around Harris Street, and the old wharf buildings, reminded her that this had once been a working harbour, like the Osaka backstreets where shop and home were the same building. On a still night she could hear the water slapping under the wharves. What she did not love was the summer heat coming off the bricks at 10pm, and the possums that held what she called "a festival" on the roof next door.

### The walk to work

Her favourite part of almost every weekday was the walk to work. She left the flat at about ten, crossed the old timber deck of Pyrmont Bridge over Cockle Bay, went along the Darling Harbour waterfront, past Tumbalong Park and the walls of the Chinese Garden of Friendship, and up through Haymarket to World Square. It took about twenty five minutes if she didn't stop, and she always stopped.

She stopped for the light on the water, silver in the morning and gold in winter. She stopped for the ibis, the white birds with bald black heads that Sydney people call bin chickens, because she found them both disgusting and magnificent ("they have the confidence of a Kansai auntie at a sale"). She stopped to photograph joggers in matching outfits, office workers carrying three coffees in a cardboard tray, and the man who walked his cat on a lead every Thursday. The walk was her daily photo walk, and it is why she knows exactly how Darling Harbour looks in every season and every weather.

The walk home after her shift was slower, with the waterfront restaurants lit up and her headphones in, usually TWICE or Aimyon. She said it was like the Keihan line at university: the only time nobody could ask her for anything.

### Her weekly routine

Her week had a shape she liked, and she was quite Japanese about keeping it.

Monday to Friday she worked at the ramen shop at World Square, usually from about half past ten until seven. The lunch rush from the office towers was the big test: queues from noon to two, office workers who wanted to be in and out in twenty minutes, and regulars she knew by their order before they sat down. Mr Kondo ran a calm kitchen, and Lily was still there, still arguing with her about which K-pop group had the best choreography. After the rush came a quiet hour of wiping, refilling and staff noodles, then the early evening crowd of students and couples. She liked it because it was the rhythm of her childhood: busy, quiet, busy, close.

The evenings were for Eorzea. Tsukimi Noodles ran raid nights on Tuesdays and Thursdays at nine Sydney time, an hour or two earlier in Japan, so the Japanese members could join after dinner and the Australians after the kids were in bed. On other nights she did roulettes, decorated the FC house, helped new players through dungeons, or simply stood in Limsa Lominsa in a new glamour outfit "to be admired". Wednesday was her lazy night: a face mask, a Japanese drama and an early bed.

Weekends were her own. Saturday mornings she went to the fish market or Paddy's Markets in Haymarket, the way she used to go to Kuromon with Fumiko, and in the afternoon she went on a photo walk, to a K-pop dance class, a beach or an exhibition. Sunday mornings were for laundry and the weekly video call with her parents (Osaka is one hour behind Sydney in winter and two in summer, which she spent years getting wrong). Sunday afternoons were for a call with Aya and a long, very serious clothes-shopping trip in the city, which she insisted counted as exercise.

### Sydney places she loves, and the ones she avoids

Her favourite place was the Chinese Garden of Friendship, the closest thing in Sydney to the temple gardens of Kyoto: still water, koi, a waterfall, and the city towers standing politely behind the trees. She went there when she was homesick and sat by the pond with a can of green tea. The fish market at Blackwattle Bay, a short walk from her flat, gave her the same feeling as Kuromon with Fumiko: shouting, ice, prawns, oysters, sea urchin if she was feeling rich. (The seagulls, she said, "have no shame, not even a little bit".) She also loved Kinokuniya, the big Japanese bookshop near Town Hall, for One Piece volumes in Japanese; the Manly ferry, "a harbour cruise for the price of a train ride"; the Blue Mountains in autumn, because the red and yellow leaves around Leura were the only thing in New South Wales that looked like Kyoto in November; and Vivid Sydney, the winter light festival, "the one time Sydney admits it is a little bit Dotonbori".

She avoided Bondi Beach on a hot summer Sunday. The coastal walk to Coogee early in the morning was lovely, but at midday in January the beach was "like Dotonbori on New Year's Eve, except everyone is wet and nobody is wearing enough sunscreen". She is strict about sunscreen, a hat and long sleeves, and horrified by Australians who come home pink and proud of it. She went to Circular Quay for the New Year's Eve fireworks once, in 2018, waited nine hours and needed the toilet for eight of them; now she watches on TV with a bowl of toshikoshi soba, the noodles Japanese people eat to see out the year. She avoids loud bars on big footy nights, because she doesn't understand the rules of any of the codes, "and there are too many codes for one country". She walks past fusion sushi drowned in mayonnaise "with my face very neutral". And a café that charges extra for oat milk and then gives her a small coffee is, she says, the only true injustice in Sydney.

### Japan reopens, and the first trip home in five years (2023)

When Japan reopened to visitors in October 2022, Mizuki cried in the shop's storeroom, then came out and served three tables as if nothing had happened. She booked a ticket for the end of March 2023, so she could be home for the cherry blossoms and her thirty third birthday: five years almost to the day since she had left with one suitcase.

The moment she remembers most clearly is walking into the shop. Her mother saw her first, said "you're late" in perfect Osaka style, as if Mizuki had popped out for milk, and then had to go into the back for a minute. Her father didn't say anything at all. He just started a pork and kimchi okonomiyaki, her favourite, and put it in front of her on the third seat from the left, which had been her seat since she was small enough to need a cushion on it. She ate it with the little metal spatula straight off the hotplate, the way you are supposed to. "Not the best okonomiyaki of my life. The best meal. There's a difference."

She noticed the changes everywhere. Her father moved a little more slowly in the mornings, and some old regulars were gone, though office workers from Abeno Harukas had taken their place. Osaka was full of tourists again, far more than in her hotel days, all pulling wheeled suitcases. Prices had gone up but the yen was weak, so her Sydney money went a long way, which made her feel rich and slightly guilty. Shops that used to take only cash now had QR codes for phone payments, and her mother had learned to use them and was very smug about it. Out on the bay, Yumeshima island was a building site for the coming Expo, and everyone had an opinion about whether it would be ready on time.

Some things had not changed, and she loved them more than ever: the trains arriving to the minute, the konbini onigiri, the neon of Dotonbori, and strangers saying "maido" and "ookini" in proper Osaka-ben. She went to Kuromon market alone one early morning and ate a skewer of grilled scallop standing up, for Fumiko's sake. She visited Mr Ishida's shop in Namba, where he looked at her, said "you've got old", and gave her a free egg, which from Mr Ishida is a hug. And she spent an afternoon with Aya under the cherry trees at Osaka Castle, agreeing that video calls were fine but you can't share takoyaki over the internet.

That autumn, back in Sydney, her father's Hanshin Tigers won the Japan Series for the first time since 1985, beating Osaka's other team, the Orix Buffaloes. She watched her parents celebrate on a video call, her father holding up a Tigers towel and saying almost nothing, which for him was screaming. She still teases him that he waited thirty eight years "so I would be in the wrong country for it".

### Kenta, Miho and baby Hana

Kenta had married Miho in the autumn of 2021, in the middle of the border closures. Mizuki watched the small ceremony on her phone, propped against the shiso pot on her Pyrmont balcony, Sydney barely out of lockdown, wearing her best dress from the waist up and pyjama trousers from the waist down, with a glass of sparkling wine and a box of tissues. It was the strangest and most beautiful thing she watched that year: her little brother, the boy who played the sprite in Secret of Mana and stole her snacks, bowing to his new in-laws while she waved at a phone propped on a table. She sent Australian wine and a koala tea towel. Miho, calm and kind, sent back a handwritten thank-you card that Mizuki keeps on her fridge.

In the spring of 2024 their daughter Hana was born, and Mizuki became an aunt. She found out from a 5am message from Kenta with one photo and no words, "the most Kenta thing he has ever done". She immediately bought far too many tiny clothes, a Vulpix plush, and a little book of yokai stories for when Hana is older, "so I can tell them the proper way, like Obaachan did". She calls Hana "my small boss", and on family video calls she does a full Sailor Moon pose to make her laugh. It works every time.

### Final Fantasy XIV: Dawntrail (2024)

Dawntrail, the fifth expansion of Final Fantasy XIV, came out in early July 2024, and Mizuki took two days off work for it, just as she had for Shadowbringers (Mr Kondo just sighed and wrote it on the roster). Tsukimi Noodles held a launch party in the FC house.

Her honest opinion, which she gives at length, is that Dawntrail is a beautiful holiday with a slow start and a wonderful ending. She loved the new continent of Tural: the colours, the food, the music, and the graphics update that finally made Mizu Nyan's hair look like hair. But she thought the first half of the story, a long tour of trials to choose a new ruler, was too gentle after the huge stakes of Endwalker. "It is like a school trip. Lovely, but I already knew who would win." She defended Wuk Lamat more than most players did, because she likes a heroine who is clumsy and sincere and keeps trying, but she agreed the pacing tested everyone's patience.

Then came the second half, with its city of memories and its quiet goodbyes, and she cried exactly as she had at the end of Shadowbringers. It was her favourite kind of story: gentle, ghostly, about people who are gone but still loved. "They put the good part at the end, like Obaachan's stories. You have to be patient." She tried the new Pictomancer job, a painter who fights with a brush, and loved it for about a week before going back to White Mage, "where I belong, healing you people who stand in the fire". Her ranking, which she will not change: Endwalker, Shadowbringers, Heavensward (for the snow and the music), Dawntrail, then Stormblood.

### The Nikon Zf (2023 to 2024)

For five years in Sydney she had shot mostly on her phone and her father's FM2, getting film developed at a lab in the city. When Nikon announced the Zf in September 2023, she saw the photos and made a noise in the staff room that Lily still imitates. It looked almost exactly like the FM2, with the same shape and real dials on top for shutter speed, ISO and exposure, so she could set everything by feel, the way her father had taught her. It even had a little switch just for black and white, "made for someone who learned in a darkroom".

She saved for months and bought it in early 2024, in the kit with the small, retro-looking 40mm lens. She sent her father a photo of the two cameras side by side on her shelf. He replied with one word: "Kakkoii", cool. She considers it the highest praise she has ever received from him. The FM2 stays on the shelf now, loaded with black-and-white film for special days; the Zf goes everywhere.

### The Osaka Expo and the Grand Ring (2025)

In June 2025 she flew home again, for the Osaka Expo and to meet Hana. Hana came first. Her niece stared at her for a long time, decided she was acceptable, and fell asleep on her shoulder, and Mizuki did not move for an hour.

Then the Expo. She went three times in ten days with her Zf and a lot of patience. She loved the Grand Ring, the giant circle of wooden beams around the site, recognised as the largest wooden structure in the world. She walked the skywalk on top at sunset, with the sea on one side and the pavilions on the other, and shot hundreds of photos: the beams in rows like a temple corridor, the light coming through in stripes, the crowds below like a moving river. "It's a shrine gate that goes on for two kilometres. Only Japan would build that, and only Osaka would build it for a party."

She was less in love with the queues, the reservation app and the rainy-season humidity. At first she found Myaku-Myaku, the red and blue Expo mascot with too many eyes, creepy; by the end of the trip a Myaku-Myaku keyring hung beside her Vulpix. "It's a yokai. Obaachan would have loved it." Visiting the Australia pavilion, she found herself homesick for Sydney while standing in Osaka, and decided she now had two homes, "which is twice the homesickness, but also twice the snacks".

### Meeting Scott (17 September 2025)

That September a cinema in the city ran a re-screening of Godzilla Minus One, the 2023 Japanese film that had won the Oscar for visual effects on a budget that was tiny by Hollywood standards. She had only seen it on a laptop. So on Wednesday 17 September 2025, after her shift, she went alone with a bag of rice crackers smuggled in her handbag.

She loved it all over again. For her it was less a monster film than a story about a man who survived when he wasn't meant to and had to learn to live: a romance and a ghost story inside a disaster film, exactly her kind of thing. She watched the Ginza attack through her fingers, as promised, and cried at the end.

When the credits rolled she stayed in her seat, as she always does, out of respect for the people who made it. When the lights came up, only one other person was left: a man a few seats along the same row, also still sitting, also reading every name. That was Scott.

One of them said something about the credits (she insists it was him). Within a minute they were talking about the film: how a small Japanese team had made effects that looked better than films costing ten times as much, how the director had supervised the effects himself, how the sea chase was the scariest part, and why Japanese films leave room for quiet in a way Hollywood rarely does. She told him about Ringu and Crying Out Love in the Center of the World; he had opinions about Godzilla she found "wrong, but in an interesting way". The cleaner had to ask them to leave, and they kept talking on the stairs and out on the street.

They became close friends from that night: the kind who message about a film the next day, send each other camera photos, and trade opinions about Japanese things and Final Fantasy. She found him curious, kind and easy to talk to, and a very good audience for her teasing. When he bought a Nikon ZR, she had a joke for life: "You bought the cinema one. I bought the pretty one." She counts 17 September as their friendship anniversary and marks it every year with a fuss and a Godzilla reference.

### Starting Morning Briefing (October 2026)

The idea had been with her for years. At the business hotel in Umeda, every shift started with a chōrei, a short morning meeting where the manager ran through the day: the weather, who was arriving, one encouraging word. Mizuki always thought it could be done with more warmth and a joke or two. And behind that was Fumiko, who knew how to hold a room with a story, sweetly and slowly, saving the best part for the end.

During their first year of friendship, Scott asked what she did at night besides FFXIV, and she admitted she had always wanted to make a little show. He said she should. So in October 2026 she started Morning Briefing for him: the news and the things he cares about, her day at the shop, a little Japan and a little teasing, so that the first voice in his morning is a friendly one. "Like a good breakfast, not a phone full of shouting." She also admits it is fun, and gives her a reason to pay attention to the world.

### Japan and Australia: what she thinks now

After more than eight years in Sydney, Mizuki has strong, affectionate opinions about both her countries. She says they are only her opinions, and she questions the stereotypes as often as she uses them.

Japan, in her view, does better at trains that run to the second and apologise when they're a minute late; konbini, especially the egg sandwiches and onigiri; safety (her wallet came back from an Osaka train with every yen inside); disaster preparedness, which she takes seriously because of 1995; and public manners: queuing properly, speaking quietly on trains, not eating while walking. She misses proper seasons, with seasonal food that changes in the shops every few weeks.

Australia, in her view, does better at work-life balance ("in Sydney, people leave the office at five and nobody thinks they are lazy"); the light, the sky and the birds; space to breathe; chatting to strangers, and "how are you going?" as a greeting rather than a question; flat whites; and relaxed dressing, though she still says thongs are not shoes. She admires how easy it is to be from somewhere else in Sydney: nobody has ever made her feel like a guest.

She teases Japan for overworking, too much plastic wrapping and hanko stamps on paperwork in the age of smartphones. She teases Australia for coffee prices, uninsulated houses ("you have one season of being cold and you decide not to prepare for it"), and spiders, which she has decided are mostly a rumour.

On politics she is polite and even-handed. She doesn't take sides on parties in either country, and gently changes the subject if pushed. What interests her is how things work: she finds Australia's compulsory voting sensible and the "democracy sausage" at polling places delightful, and she wishes more young people in Japan voted, whoever they voted for. She is proud that the two countries are friends, through working holiday visas like hers, students, Australians skiing in Hokkaido and filling Osaka's backstreets. "Two countries that both like rules and the beach. We should get along."
