But the part I want to talk about is the very beginning. The Millennial Fair.

The game opens in the year one thousand A D, in Leene Square, at a festival. There are stalls and games and music. There's Leene's Bell, a giant bell you can hit for points. There's Gato, a singing robot, built by your friend Lucca, with a punching bag in his belly, who'll fight you for points. It feels like a tutorial. A little fair to wander around while the story gets going. You can pick up the pendant and give it back. Or keep it, or even try to sell it. You can help a little girl find her lost cat. You can find an old man's lunch sitting on a table... and eat it.

And here's the secret... the game is watching all of it.

Because a few hours later, Crono is put on trial, accused of kidnapping the princess. And the prosecution brings up what you did at the fair. Did you eat that old man's lunch? That's theft, and it counts against you. Did you grab the pendant before even talking to her? Against you. Did you bring the little girl her cat? A witness speaks up for you. That's the lesson for Lanternfall. A festival is the perfect place for a game to start watching you. Everyone's out. Everyone's paying attention. And nobody is suspicious of a festival.

What if the village holds a festival every year to welcome it? The Lanternfall Festival. Stalls, music, Chen Stormstout's stall in the middle, selling his Stout and making jokes about being hungry.

And the festival is where the village decides who you are. Here are a few ideas, and you can take any or none of them.

One. The lost lantern. A child at the festival has lost her paper lantern; it's floated off over the fields. If you find it and bring it back, people remember. If you keep it, because it's actually worth a lot, someone saw you. Someone always sees you.

Two. The radish contest. The village has a contest for the biggest radish. You can enter honestly, with what you've grown. Or you can enter that suspiciously large, plain white radish with the red top. The one the farmers call "Sir". He doesn't want to be judged. He wants to judge.

Three. The nurikabe at the edge of the festival, the wall who's been waiting three hundred years for the right cake. If you bring it a radish cake, it moves, and the village sees you do it. If you just walk around it, it remembers that too.

And then, after the festival... a reckoning. Not a trial with a jury, that's Chrono Trigger's. But something that fits your world. Maybe the zashiki warashi in your farmhouse, the luck spirit we talked about yesterday, makes its decision about you on Lanternfall night. Maybe the village elder decides who gets the best field. Maybe Chen gives you a discount... or doubles his prices. Chen doubling his prices is the scariest outcome. He knows you need that Stout. He's a businessman.

Or maybe... the festival never really ended. Maybe the villagers have been holding it every night, for years... waiting for a farmer to judge. And it's your turn.

And the technical side is simpler than it sounds. Every one of those moments is just a flag: a little true or false, like "returned the lantern", or "fed the nurikabe". You already have that idea from the dialogue system we talked about: flags in a GameState. Later, the festival reads them, and characters react. Which is exactly what today's game tech and workshop are about: getting your farmer to actually interact with something in the world. Because before the village can judge you... you need to be able to touch something.

So here's my question. If the Lanternfall village held a trial after the festival, Chrono Trigger style, what's the one small thing the player did that the villagers would bring up as evidence? My answer? You talked to the scarecrow for forty five minutes. The scarecrow testified. He had a lot to say. Your answer page has a box for it.

== Game tech ==

Game tech! Day four of the Lanternfall week. On Monday, your project got saved properly, with Git. Yesterday, your farmer learned to walk. And today... your farmer learns to touch things.

Interaction. Pressing a button next to something, and having the world respond. Pulling a crop, opening a door, talking to Chen, feeding the nurikabe. In a farming game it's almost everything. And it's the first step of tomorrow's radish build-along, because before a radish monster can burst out of the ground, you need to be able to pull a radish.

First, the idea, with an everyday picture. Think about automatic doors at the shops. You don't press a button to open them. The door has a sensor, a little invisible zone in front of it, and when you walk into the zone, it notices you. Godot has exactly that. It's called an Area2D.

An Area2D is an invisible zone. It doesn't block anything, and nothing bumps into it. It just notices. Give it a CollisionShape2D child, to say how big the zone is, and it will tell you when something enters or leaves. It does that with signals: body entered, when a physics body like your farmer walks in, and body exited, when they leave. There's also area entered and area exited, for when two zones overlap.

Now, there are two ways to set this up, and the choice matters. You can put a zone on every object: every radish, every door, every stall, each one waiting for the farmer to walk in. Or you can put one zone on the farmer: a little "reach" zone just in front of them, and let it find whatever's there. For a farming game, the second way is usually nicer. The farmer reaches, and whatever is in reach can be used. It's one place to write the logic, and it works the same for a radish as for a door.

So how does the farmer know what it's touching? Groups. In Godot, you can add any node to a named group, like a label on a box. Put your radish in a group called "interactable". Put the door in it too, and Chen's stall. Then, when the player presses the button, the reach zone asks: what areas am I overlapping? That's a function called get overlapping areas. And for each one, is it in the "interactable" group? If yes, tell it to interact.

Like walking into a party and asking, is anyone here interactable? Which is also how I'd describe your social life at a WoW launch. Sorry, sorry. That was too easy.

The button itself goes in the Input Map, just like yesterday's movement. Add an action called "interact", and give it E, or the space bar, and a button on the controller. Then, in your player script, check Input dot is action just pressed, interact. The "just" is important: it's true only on the frame you press it, not every frame you hold it down. Otherwise, holding the button would pull sixty radishes a second. Which, with a radish monster in the game... is a very quick way to meet Sir.

Now, the one gotcha that catches everyone. Collision layers and masks. Think of layers as what something is, and masks as what it's looking for. Your farmer might be on layer one. Your interactables could be on layer two. And the farmer's reach zone needs its mask set to layer two, so it can see them. If the mask doesn't include the right layer, nothing ever happens, no error, no message, just silence. It's the single most common reason "my Area2D doesn't work". So if you press E and nothing happens, check the layers first.

A second little gotcha: the overlapping list only updates once per physics step. So it's empty on the very first frame something appears. That almost never matters for a farmer walking up to a radish. But it's worth knowing when something odd happens on the first frame of a scene.

Then, what happens when you interact? That's the object's job, not the farmer's. Every interactable gets a function called interact. A radish's interact pulls the radish. A door's opens the door. The farmer doesn't need to know the difference. That's a really nice habit in game code: the thing being used decides what using it means.

And to tell the rest of the game, use a signal of your own. In Godot four, you write it at the top of the script: signal pulled. When the radish is pulled, it says pulled dot emit. Anything that cares, the inventory, the sound, and later, the radish monster's hidden counter, can connect to that signal and react, without the radish knowing they exist. The radish just announces it's been pulled. It has no idea what's listening. Which is exactly... how it should be.

Finally, the bit that makes it feel good. When the radish is pulled, don't just delete it. Give it a tiny pop. Godot's tweens make this easy: create tween, then tween property, to scale it up to about one point three for a tenth of a second, with a little bounce, then down to nothing, and then call queue free, which removes the node safely at the end of the frame. A quarter of a second of animation, and suddenly pulling a radish feels like pulling a radish, not like a sprite blinking out of existence. That's the same game feel idea we talked about with screen shake: tiny touches, big difference.

And one last tip for later: show the player what they can reach. When something interactable enters the reach zone, give it a little outline, or a small button prompt above it. Players should never have to guess whether they're close enough. Secret of Mana and Stardew both do this quietly, all the time.

So, today's lesson in one breath: a reach zone, an Area2D in front of the farmer; an "interact" action; is action just pressed; get overlapping areas; an "interactable" group; an interact function on each object; a signal called pulled; and a little tween pop. And in the workshop, we'll build your very first radish.

== Effects ==

Effects! And with Godzilla Minus Zero opening here today, I wanted to go back to the Godzilla that changed everything just before it. Shin Godzilla, from twenty sixteen.

It was directed by two men from the world of anime and special effects: Hideaki Anno, who created Neon Genesis Evangelion, as chief director and writer, and Shinji Higuchi as director and head of visual effects. And they made a decision Toho had never made before. For the first time, Toho's Godzilla wasn't a man in a suit. He was entirely computer generated.

Let's go step by step through how he was built.

Step one, the design. The concept designer was Mahiro Maeda, and the character design and sculpture were by Takayuki Takeya. And Anno and Higuchi wanted something no Godzilla had done before: a creature that evolves during the film. Not one monster, but a monster that keeps changing, right in front of you.

So there are forms. The first, you only ever see as a tail in the bay. The second, which fans nicknamed "Kamata kun", crawls up a river onto land, on its belly, with huge goggle eyes and gills that pump out a red fluid. He looks like a giant fish that's just been told some very bad news. About Tokyo. He always gets the bad news about Tokyo. The third form rears up onto its back legs. And the fourth is the Godzilla on the poster: black and red skin, like cooling lava, rows of jagged teeth, and a tail about twice as long as his body.

Step two, the sculpture. Here's an interesting bit of history. They did build an animatronic, a big mechanical upper body of the fourth form, for close ups. It was never used. By the time the VFX supervisor, Atsuki Sato, joined the project, he said CG production "had already been determined". So instead, the sculpted model became a reference, a colourless maquette that the animators studied from every angle. Toho released a behind the scenes video showing the creature go from a plain, colourless computer model, to the finished, glowing monster.

Step three, the movement. And this is my favourite part. Who do you get to perform a computer generated monster? They chose Mansai Nomura. He's a master of kyogen, the classical comic theatre of Japan, which goes alongside noh. He performed in a motion capture suit, and that stiff, strange, ritual feeling in the way the fourth form moves, the way it stands upright, almost like a statue that's been told to walk, comes from him. Here's a little secret... that eerie Godzilla walk... is six hundred years of theatre.

Step four, the sound. Rather than inventing a brand new roar, they went back to the archives, and re-edited Godzilla roars from nineteen fifty four, and from the later eras. So under all that new technology, it's still the original voice.

Step five, the big moment. The atomic breath. It's built in stages, and it's terrifying. First, black smoke pours out of his mouth. Then it ignites into orange flame, spreading across the city. Then it narrows, and condenses into a thin purple beam, cutting through buildings. And then beams start firing from the plates on his back, and from his tail. It's very pretty, really. Purple is such a lovely colour... for the end of a city.

There was still some practical work. The one practical piece confirmed in the finished film is a tail prop by Takeya, for the final shot, which reveals small human-like figures splitting off the end of the tail. Fans are still arguing about what that means.

And the results: the budget grew from about nine hundred million yen to one point three billion, it was the top live action Japanese film of twenty sixteen, and it won seven Japan Academy Prizes, including Picture of the Year. Its success paved the way for the Godzilla you can see tonight.

The practical lesson? The technology was new, but the feeling came from two very old things: a performer's body, and a sound from nineteen fifty four. When you build something new, keep one thing from the original. For Lanternfall, that might be the way Secret of Mana's characters move, or one sound you love. It keeps the new thing feeling like it has roots.

== Director ==

Director! And today, a pairing you'll appreciate, because on Friday last week we talked about the miniatures of the original Blade Runner. Today, the sequel. Denis Villeneuve and the cinematographer Roger Deakins, on Blade Runner Twenty Forty Nine.

It was their third film together, after Prisoners and Sicario, so they already trusted each other. And they planned it obsessively. They storyboarded with the production designer, Dennis Gassner, in Montreal, then with a storyboard artist in Los Angeles, right up until a few days before shooting. Deakins said it was storyboarded more precisely than almost anything he'd done, apart from his films with the Coen brothers.

The camera: mostly an ARRI Alexa XT Studio, with smaller Alexa Minis for tight spots, like inside the spinners, the flying cars. Deakins tested the bigger Alexa sixty five, and found it "almost too sharp". He shot with the full sensor, "open gate", so there'd be room for an IMAX version. And usually it was just one camera, on a dolly or a crane, so every shot was lit and composed for that one angle.

The lenses were ARRI Zeiss Master Primes, mostly between twenty seven and fifty millimetres, but going as wide as fourteen to make the sets feel bigger. Fifty millimetres, you'll notice. The same as your future X-M5 lens. I'm just saying, the spreadsheet with the tabs would approve.

And the sets were real. Most of it was shot at Origo Studios in Budapest, and most of what you see in the foreground and middle of each shot was physically built. Computer graphics mainly extended the backgrounds. And Deakins said: "I think it benefits you to do as much as possible in camera."

Which brings us to the lighting, and three scenes.

First, Wallace's office, with that rippling, golden water light across the walls. That wasn't added later. There was real water above the ceiling, with ten K lamps bounced off it, and wave machines rippling it, so the light moved across the walls. Then the actors were lit by rings of small Fresnel lamps, hundreds of them, on a dimmer chaser, so the light slowly travelled around the room like a sun circling. And here's the secret... Wallace is blind. Deakins thought it was "ironically interesting"... to bathe a man who can't see... in moving sunlight.

Second, Las Vegas, that thick orange haze. Again, in camera. A custom filter, a mix of two colours, called Moroccan Pink and Orange, gelled lights, and for the casino exterior, two hundred and fifty space lights hung over the stage. The interior was lit through gelled windows with about twenty big lamps. No colour grade invented that orange. It was there in front of the lens.

Third, the smog of Los Angeles. The special effects supervisor, Gerd Nefzer, rigged sprinklers and nozzles to fill the stage with mist. Deakins wanted "an extremely smoggy version of Beijing". Not the most romantic brief. But it looks incredible.

And the payoff: Deakins finally won the Oscar for Best Cinematography for it, at his fourteenth nomination. The film won for visual effects too.

So, the practical lesson for you, with Final Cut Pro and the camera you're about to buy. Get the look on set, not in the grade. Choose one colour for a scene before you film it, and then put that colour in front of the lens: a coloured light, a window at golden hour, a street full of neon, some steam or mist. Deakins said: "If I want to soften the image, I do it by lighting or exposure on set." The grade should finish a look, not invent it. And light with one strong, believable source, like a window, or a lamp, or the sun, rather than lots of little ones. That's Deakins in one sentence.

So, my question. If you lit one scene in your travel videos with a single colour, like Deakins' orange Las Vegas, where would you film it, and what colour would it be? My answer? A Sydney train platform at six in the morning. And the colour is "tired". It's a very popular colour on the T8. Tell me on your answer page.

== Workshop ==

Workshop! Tonight's build step for Lanternfall, about an hour: a radish you can pull. Here we go.

Step one, the button. Open Project Settings, then the Input Map. Add an action called "interact", and give it the E key, and the bottom face button on your controller.

Step two, the radish. Make a new scene, and choose Area2D as the root. Rename it Radish. Add two children: a Sprite2D, with any little test image for now, and a CollisionShape2D, with a small circle. Then, in the Inspector, under Collision, turn off layer one, and turn on layer two. That's the "interactable" layer. And in the Node tab, under Groups, add it to a group called "interactable".

Step three, the radish script. Attach a script. At the top, write signal pulled, and a variable called is pulled, set to false. Then a function called interact. Inside: if it's already pulled, return. Otherwise, set is pulled to true, call pulled dot emit, and then the pop: create a tween, tween its scale up to one point three over a tenth of a second, then down to zero over about point one five, and finally, tween callback, queue free. Five lines, and a radish has a whole life story. Born, pulled, popped. Very moving. I might need a minute.

Step four, the farmer's reach. Open your Player scene from yesterday. Add a child Area2D called Reach, with a circle collision shape, a bit bigger than the farmer's feet. In its Collision settings, turn off its layer, and set its mask to layer two only, so it only sees interactables.

Step five, the player script. In physics process, after your movement lines, add: if Input dot is action just pressed, interact. Then loop over Reach dot get overlapping areas. For each one, if it's in the group "interactable", call its interact function, and break, so you only use one thing at a time.

Step six. Drag three or four radishes into your Farm scene, press play, walk up to one, and press E.

How do you know it works? Three checks. The radish pops and disappears. Pressing E when you're not near one does nothing. And mashing E on one radish only pulls it once. If nothing happens at all, it's almost always the layers: check the radish is on layer two, and the Reach mask is on layer two. And if you want a tiny bonus: in the Farm scene, connect one radish's pulled signal to a function that prints "pulled". Tomorrow, that print becomes a counter. And the counter becomes a monster. Then commit it to Git: "farmer pulls a radish". Day four, done.
