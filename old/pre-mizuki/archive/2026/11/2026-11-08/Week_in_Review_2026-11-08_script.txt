On Tuesday, for your tiger crest, we met the guardian tigers. Byakko, the White Tiger, is one of the Four Symbols, the four great guardian beasts of East Asian tradition, watching over the west. And in Japan, there's a lovely twist: there were no tigers in Japan, so for centuries painters who'd never seen one worked from skins and from imagination. Some of their tigers ended up looking suspiciously like very large, very grumpy house cats. Which, honestly, is exactly what I'd want defending my farm. A tiger that might also knock your cup off the table. For defence.

On Wednesday, the zashiki-warashi, our Yokai of the Week. A small child spirit that lives in old houses, mostly in the north of Japan, especially Iwate. You rarely see it. You might hear footsteps, or find a pillow moved in the night. And the belief is that while it lives with you, the house prospers. But if it ever leaves, the luck leaves too. Which is why, in Lanternfall, it became a hidden house-luck number, ticking up when you're kind and down when you're not. A spirit you never quite see, quietly keeping score.

And then the chochin obake, the lantern ghost. An old paper lantern that splits open into a mouth, with a single eye and a long tongue. It belongs to a family of yokai called tsukumogami: everyday objects that, according to the stories, come to life once they reach a hundred years old. Umbrellas, sandals, teapots, lanterns. For your game, it gave us the rule for Lanternfall night: gold lanterns are gifts, red ones are trouble.

And tsukumogami might be the most useful idea of all for you. Remember your answer, weeks ago, about summoning yokai with random personalities, and the prankster that hides your hoe somewhere stupid? What if it's the hoe itself? Your farmer's tools, if you keep them long enough, start to wake up. A hundred in-game days with the same watering can... and one morning, it has opinions. Very strong opinions. Mostly about where you've been watering.

And when you put it all together, there's a lovely shape to your yokai. A wall that wants cake. A tiger that guards. A child who keeps the house lucky. A lantern that remembers. Almost none of them are simply monsters. They're more like neighbours with strange rules. Which is very Japanese folklore. And very you. Even your goblins are fertiliser by spring.

And on Lanternfall night... when the gold lanterns fall... the zashiki-warashi comes out to watch from the farmhouse step. The nurikabe waits at the edge of the field, hoping for cake. And somewhere in the dark... one red lantern hasn't blinked all night. It's just... very excited to see you.
