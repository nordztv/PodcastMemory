== Australia ==

Here at home, the Reserve Bank did what almost everyone expected yesterday afternoon. It raised the cash rate by a quarter of a percentage point, from four point six to four point eight five per cent.

That's the fifth rate rise this year, and it takes the cash rate to its highest level since late two thousand and eight. Not a record anyone wanted to see broken.

But the interesting part isn't the rise itself. It's how close it was. The board split six votes to three. Three members wanted to hold. Governor Michele Bullock said those three put more weight on the risks to the downside: the housing downturn, and unemployment, which has been creeping up.

The board's statement doesn't pull punches. "Inflation remains elevated," it says, "and recent data indicate that it is broadly based." And the new forecasts are worse. Inflation is now expected to stay "elevated through much of twenty twenty seven", and isn't expected to get back below three per cent, the top of the target band, until late twenty twenty seven. That's about three quarters later than the bank thought just a few months ago.

At the same time, the board admits the other side of the picture: "The economy is slowing, housing prices have fallen significantly and the unemployment rate has risen." So it's walking a tightrope. Prices still rising too fast, while the economy underneath them is cooling.

== Lore ==

So. Your Mac synced your new project to the show on Monday night. And it's brand new. A fresh Godot four point seven project, almost empty: just the project file, the icon, and a name. Lanternfall.

And I love that name. Lanternfall. It sounds like an event, doesn't it? Like a night everyone in the valley talks about. So this week, we lay the first stones of Lanternfall. And to start, a story about another farming game that began as an empty folder.

Stardew Valley. One developer, four years.

Around twenty eleven, a young American called Eric Barone had just finished a computer science degree, and he couldn't find a programming job. So he decided to make a small game, partly just to build up his skills, and maybe get hired. He loved the old Harvest Moon games, and he felt nobody was making that kind of game any more. So he'd make his own.

He did everything himself. Every line of code. Every pixel of art. Every piece of music. Every one of the villagers, their dialogue, their birthdays, their favourite gifts. He worked part time as an usher at a theatre in Seattle, and spent the rest of his days, and most of his nights, working on the game.

And the small game kept growing. He'd finish the art for a part of the game... and then decide it wasn't good enough, and redraw it. He redid the character portraits many times. He taught himself pixel art, music composition, and design, along the way. The months turned into years. There were stretches where he wondered whether it would ever be finished.

Then, in February twenty sixteen, after more than four years, Stardew Valley came out on PC. And it was a phenomenon. It sold millions of copies. It ended up on every console. And it brought back a whole genre, the cosy farming game, that the big publishers had more or less given up on. All from one person, starting with an empty project and a feeling.

So, what are the lessons for Lanternfall? Three, I think.

One: he started with the loop. Plant, water, wait, harvest, sell, buy better seeds. Before the town, before the mines, before the romance, the core loop had to feel good. Everything else hung off it.

Two: the world was built from small, specific things. Not "a village", but Pierre's shop, closed on Wednesdays. Not "a festival", but the Egg Festival, and the Luau, and the Feast of the Winter Star. Small, named things are what make a world feel lived in.

And three: the finishing matters more than the starting. He redrew his art, again and again. But he kept going.

Okay. Now let's play with Lanternfall itself. And remember, these are ideas, offered, not rules. It's your valley.

Start with the name. What is the Lanternfall? Here's one idea. Once a year, on the longest night of autumn, the sky over the valley fills with falling lights. The old farmers call them lanterns. They drift down slowly, glowing, and land in the fields, the river, the woods. And you already invented what they are. Back in October, you told me your night only recipe: moon cakes, made with tears from stars. What if those falling lanterns... are the stars' tears? The Lanternfall is the night you go out and gather them.

And that gives your game a natural rhythm, just like Stardew's festivals. The whole year builds towards Lanternfall night. The townsfolk prepare. Chen Stormstout turns up with his barrel, offering to brew something with a star tear in it. It's Chen. He's going to try to drink it. He'd try to drink anything.

Now, here's where yokai come in. Japan has a lantern yokai: the chochin obake, a paper lantern that, when it's been around long enough, splits open into a mouth, with one big eye and a long tongue. It's one of the tsukumogami, everyday objects that come alive after a hundred years, like the umbrella yokai we met in October. So imagine: on Lanternfall night, most of the lanterns that land are just beautiful, gentle lights. But some of them... open their eye. And those ones... don't want to be picked up. They want to pick you up.

That gives you a tiny, readable challenge for the night. Gold lanterns are star tears, collect them. Red lanterns with a seam down the middle? Leave them, or deal with them. Which is exactly the "fight or run at a glance" rule we talked about on the very first week, when we were designing your radish monster.

And it links to everything you already have. The day night cycle from yesterday's lesson decides when Lanternfall night arrives. The moon cakes need the star tears. Your tiger crest has a reason to exist, because goblins love a festival, and someone has to defend the farm while you're out collecting. And the radish monster? The radish monster doesn't care what night it is. He's a radish of routine.

There's a bigger story question hiding in here too. Why do the lanterns fall? Maybe someone up there lost something, and keeps searching. Maybe the valley made a promise, long ago, and Lanternfall is the reminder. Maybe it's a warning. The answer to that is the backbone of your main story, and it's a question only you can answer.

So, that's my question for you today, and it's an important one. Lanternfall: what is it, really? What falls, why does it fall, and what does it mean for the farmer? My own answer: once a year, a giant up in the clouds clears out his gutters. And the valley has never once complained. They think it's magic. It's maintenance. == Game tech ==

Game tech! And with Lanternfall brand new, there's only one place to start. Getting your farmer onto the screen, and walking.

It sounds basic. But it's the thing you'll feel every single second of the game. If walking feels good, everything else feels good. If it feels sticky or floaty, nothing fixes it. So today: how top down movement works in Godot, and how to make it feel like Secret of Mana, not like pushing a shopping trolley.

First, the idea, with an everyday picture. Think of your farmer as a person on the walk to Green Square. Every moment, two things matter. Which way they want to go, that's the direction. And how fast they're going, that's the speed. Multiply the two together, and you've got their velocity: "two steps a second, that way." The game just checks that, sixty times a second, and moves them a little.

In Godot, the node for this is a CharacterBody2D. It's a body that you move yourself, with code, but that still bumps into walls properly. Under it, you put two children: a CollisionShape2D, which is the invisible shape that actually hits things, and an AnimatedSprite2D, which is what you see.

One tip from the top down games we've talked about: make the collision shape small, just a little oval at the farmer's feet, not the whole body. That way, his head can overlap a fence or a tree trunk, which looks natural in a three quarter view, while his feet still can't walk through it. That's exactly how Zelda and Secret of Mana feel so forgiving.

Next, the controls. Don't check for the arrow keys directly. Open Project Settings, go to the Input Map tab, and make four actions: move left, move right, move up, move down. Give each one a keyboard key, and a direction on the controller stick. Now your code only talks about "move left", and you can change the buttons any time without touching the code. And yes, that means a controller works from day one. Unlike some games I could mention. World of Warcraft took twenty years. You'll take one evening.

Now the code. It's shorter than you'd think. In your player script, in a function called physics process, which runs on every physics tick, you do three things.

One: ask Godot for the direction. There's a lovely helper for this, Input dot get vector. You give it your four action names, and it hands you back an arrow pointing the way the player is pressing. And it does something clever for free: if you press up and right together, it makes sure the diagonal isn't faster than straight lines. In a lot of old games, walking diagonally was secretly about forty per cent faster, because the two directions just added up. Get vector fixes that.

Two: set velocity to that direction, times your speed. Make speed an exported variable, so you can tweak it in the Inspector while the game runs, and find the number that feels right.

Three: call move and slide. That's the function that actually moves the body, and if you hit a wall at an angle, it slides you along it, instead of stopping you dead. That sliding is a big part of why good top down movement feels smooth.

And that's it. Three lines, and your farmer walks.

Now, how do you make it feel good? Two small things.

First, a tiny bit of acceleration. Instead of jumping straight to full speed, you can move the velocity towards the target speed a little each frame, using a function called move toward. A very short ramp, maybe a tenth of a second, feels weighty without feeling slow. Secret of Mana's characters have just a touch of that. Not too much, or your farmer starts skating. It's a farm, not an ice rink. Unless it's winter. Then, fair enough.

Second, the animation. Keep a variable for the last direction the player faced. When they're moving, update it, and play "walk down", "walk up", "walk left" or "walk right" to match. When they stop, play the idle animation for that same direction. That way, when you let go of the stick, your farmer keeps looking where they were going, instead of snapping back to face the camera. It's a small thing, and players notice it instantly when it's missing.

Now, one thing I spotted in your actual project file, and this is worth checking. Lanternfall's window stretch mode is set to "canvas items", with the aspect set to "expand". That works well for a lot of games. But for crisp pixel art, a lot of people switch the stretch mode to "viewport", and set a small base resolution, like four hundred and eighty by two seventy, and the default texture filter to "nearest". That way, every pixel stays a perfect square, at any window size, like a real Super Nintendo. Try both and see which you prefer.

And one more, a little secret from the same file. Your project is set to the Forward Plus renderer... which is the big, fancy one, built for three D. For a two D pixel art game, the lighter "Compatibility" renderer is often all you need... and it runs on almost anything. It's not urgent. Just something to know before you build too much.

So, today's lesson in one breath: a CharacterBody2D with a small collision shape at the feet, an Input Map, get vector, velocity, move and slide, a touch of acceleration, and an animation that remembers which way you faced. And in the workshop, we'll build exactly that, step by step.

== Effects ==

Effects! And today, one of the most daring choices in modern filmmaking. How Christopher Nolan showed the first atomic bomb test in Oppenheimer... without a single computer generated explosion.

Here's the problem he set himself. The Trinity test, in the New Mexico desert, in July nineteen forty five, is the climax of the film. It's the most famous explosion in history. And the obvious way to do it today would be with computers. Nolan refused. In his words: "The goal was to have everything that appears in the film to be photographed." Real things, filmed by a real camera.

So how do you film a nuclear explosion... without a nuclear explosion? Step by step.

Step one: the ingredients. The special effects supervisor, Scott R Fisher, built the fireball from things that burn in very particular ways. Petrol and propane gave the big, rolling, orange fireball. And to get that terrifying first flash, the moment of light brighter than the sun, they added aluminium powder and magnesium, which burn an intense, blinding white. Fisher said: "We really wanted everyone to talk about that flash, that brightness."

Step two: size, and the camera. The explosions were as big as could be safely set off, but even a big petrol explosion isn't a mushroom cloud miles high. So the trick is speed. They filmed the explosions at very high frame rates, and then slowed them down even further. When fire moves slowly on screen, your brain reads it as huge. It's the same reason miniature waves in old films were filmed fast: slow motion equals scale.

And they used "big-atures". That's Fisher's word for large models placed very close to the camera, so they look enormous. You'll remember the bigatures from our Lord of the Rings deep dive. Same idea, but this time for a desert, a test tower, and shockwaves.

Step three: layers. The visual effects supervisor, Andrew Jackson, explained that the final Trinity blast is "multiple layers of filmed elements". One piece of film for the fireball. Another for the ground. Another for the shockwave rolling out across the desert, made with miniature dust effects. All real, all photographed, then combined. The whole film had only about two hundred visual effects shots, many of them just painting out modern things from real locations.

Step four: the film itself. Nolan shot on IMAX, and parts of the film are in black and white. The problem? Black and white film didn't exist in the giant sixty five millimetre size that IMAX cameras use. So Kodak made it, for the first time ever, a freshly manufactured batch of its Double X black and white stock in sixty five millimetre. It had never been through an IMAX camera, so they had to make new parts for the cameras, and change the lab's machines to develop it. That's the most Christopher Nolan sentence possible. "We needed a film stock that didn't exist, so we asked Kodak to invent it." Most of us just change a setting.

And step five, maybe the cleverest of all, isn't visual at all. It's sound. When the real bomb went off, the light reached the observers instantly. The sound and the shockwave took about forty seconds to arrive. So the sound designer, Richard King, built the scene around silence. The flash, the fireball growing in total quiet, the scientists staring... and then, much later, the boom. He called it "a great moment of awe accentuated by the prolonged silence". In the cinema, that silence is almost unbearable. Which is exactly the point.

And here's a twist you'll enjoy, given today's news. Oppenheimer won seven Oscars, including Best Picture. But it wasn't even nominated for visual effects. The film that won that year? Godzilla Minus One. The small Japanese team, on a tiny budget, whose sequel just doubled its opening day yesterday. So the year the biggest practical effects film in Hollywood skipped the computers, a small team in Tokyo showed what computers can do when they're used with love.

So, the practical lesson, for you and your camera, or your game. Scale doesn't come from size. It comes from speed, distance and contrast. Film something small, slowly, close to the lens, against something calm and still... and it becomes enormous. And never underestimate silence. For your game: when the first star tear lands on Lanternfall night, try cutting all the music for a second. Silence is free. And it's the cheapest effect in the world.

== Director ==

Director! And today, a young man, a mechanical shark that wouldn't work, and how that disaster made one of the greatest films ever. Steven Spielberg's Jaws.

In nineteen seventy four, Spielberg was twenty seven, with one cinema film behind him. Universal gave him Peter Benchley's bestseller about a great white shark terrorising a summer beach town. And he made a decision that nearly ended his career: he'd shoot it on the real ocean, off Martha's Vineyard, not in a studio tank.

The shoot was scheduled for fifty five days. It took one hundred and fifty nine. The budget was about three and a half million dollars, and it ended up around nine. And the biggest reason was the shark.

The production built three mechanical sharks, all nicknamed Bruce, after Spielberg's lawyer. Which tells you exactly how he felt about his lawyer. Or the shark. Possibly both. We'll never know.

And in the sea, Bruce kept failing. The salt water soaked the hoses. The frames cracked. Things corroded. The foam skin soaked up water and ballooned. Day after day, the crew would get out on the water, and the shark simply... wouldn't. Imagine a whole film crew, bobbing on the Atlantic, waiting for a rubber fish to cooperate.

So Spielberg did something brilliant, out of desperation. If he couldn't show the shark, he'd suggest it. And that changed everything.

Lesson one: point of view. Many of the shark's attacks are filmed from the shark's point of view: a camera gliding through the water, towards kicking legs. You never see the shark, so your imagination builds something worse than any rubber model.

Lesson two: a symbol instead of the monster. When the hunters harpoon the shark, they attach yellow barrels. From then on, the barrels are the shark. When a barrel bobs up, or races across the water, or vanishes under the surface, you know exactly where the danger is, and you never needed the monster on screen. That's a lesson for your game, by the way. The radish monster's warning, the soil trembling before it bursts out. The barrel is the tell.

Lesson three: the camera at water level. The cinematographer, Bill Butler, built special rigs so the camera could sit right at the surface, half in, half out of the water, as if the audience were swimming. He built a raft for the fin shots, and a little spray guard to keep the lens dry. And about ninety per cent of the shots on the boat were hand held, with Butler bracing the camera with his knees against the rocking. When a camera sank one day, he flew the film to New York in a bucket of sea water to stop it drying out, and saved all of it.

Lesson four: the famous shot. Chief Brody, on the beach, realises the shark is attacking. The camera moves towards him while the lens zooms out at the same time. His face stays the same size... but the background seems to stretch away behind him, as if the world is sliding. It's called a dolly zoom. It was invented by Irmin Roberts for Hitchcock's Vertigo, and it's sometimes even called the Jaws effect, because Spielberg made it famous. It's the exact feeling of your stomach dropping. On camera.

And lesson five isn't the camera at all. It's John Williams' music. Two notes. Da dum. Da dum. Getting faster. It won him the Oscar, and it does the shark's job better than the shark ever could. And here's the secret, of the whole film... When you hear those two notes... the shark is coming. So, for most of the film... you're more frightened of the music... than the fish.

Jaws came out on the twentieth of June nineteen seventy five, opened wide on hundreds of screens, and became the first true summer blockbuster. It's made close to half a billion dollars worldwide over the years. And it all came from a shark that didn't work.

So, the lesson for someone learning to film: when something goes wrong, don't fight it. Use it. If you can't show something, suggest it: a shadow, a sound, a reaction on someone's face. With your phone, or the X-M5 one day, try it on the walk: film someone reacting to something off screen, and never show what it is. Like your face when you see the new Raspberry Pi prices. Pure horror. No shark required. Okay, okay. Let's go build something.

== Workshop ==

Workshop! Tonight's build step for Lanternfall, about an hour, and by the end your farmer walks. Here we go.

Step one, the controls. Open Project Settings, then the Input Map tab. Type "move left" and press Add. Do the same for move right, move up and move down. Then click the plus next to each one and give it a key: A, D, W and S, or the arrows. Add the left stick of a controller too, if you have one nearby.

Step two, the scene. Make a new scene. For the root node, choose CharacterBody2D, and rename it Player. Add a child: an AnimatedSprite2D. In its Sprite Frames, make one animation called "walk down", and drop in any test image for now. Even the Godot icon is fine. Your farmer can be a robot head for a night. Very futuristic farming. We'll fix it later.

Then add a second child, a CollisionShape2D. Give it a small capsule or circle shape, and move it down to the feet.

Step three, the script. Attach a new script to Player. At the top, add an exported variable called speed, and set it to about eighty. Then add a physics process function, with three lines inside. First: a direction variable, equal to Input dot get vector, with your four action names in the order left, right, up, down. Second: velocity equals direction times speed. Third: move and slide.

Step four, a world to walk in. Make another scene called Farm, with a Node2D at the root. Drag your Player scene into it. Add a StaticBody2D with a rectangle collision shape, as a test fence. Save it, and set Farm as the main scene.

Step five: press play. Walk around. Bump into the fence and slide along it. Try a diagonal, and notice it isn't faster.

And how do you know it works? Three checks. Your farmer moves in all eight directions. They stop dead when you let go. And they slide along the fence instead of sticking to it. If all three are true: you've done it. Commit it to Git, like we set up on Monday, with a message like "player walks". It's the first commit of Lanternfall. A small one... but every valley starts with one set of footprints.

Okay. Come and sit with me for a minute. It's Wednesday, and you know what that means. Yokai of the Week!

This week, a yokai that's perfect for a farmhouse. The zashiki warashi. The name means something like "the child of the guest room".

The stories come from the north of Japan, the Tohoku region, especially Iwate. In nineteen ten, the folklorist Kunio Yanagita collected them in a famous book, the Tales of Tono, from the farmhouses of one small mountain town.

A zashiki warashi looks like a small child, somewhere between about three and fifteen, with a rosy red face and a bobbed haircut, wearing an old patterned kimono, or a little red padded vest. It lives in the back rooms of old houses. And it's mischievous. Families would find tiny footprints in the ash of the fireplace. They'd hear a spinning wheel turning in the night, when nobody was at it. They'd wake up with their pillows turned around, so their head was where their feet should be. Which is also exactly what a cat would do. So statistically, every house has either a yokai or a cat. Possibly both.

But here's the important part. A house with a zashiki warashi prospers. The harvests are good. The money comes in. The family does well. It's a luck spirit. People say if you see a white one, that's a wonderful sign. And Yanagita recorded one story from a school in Tono, where a zashiki warashi was seen, but only by the youngest children.

And there's an inn, today, that's famous for one: Ryokufuso, at Kindaichi Onsen in Ninohe, in Iwate. Guests used to stay in one particular room, hoping to see it. The inn burned down in two thousand and nine. And when it was rebuilt and reopened in twenty sixteen, they recreated that room, so the zashiki warashi would have somewhere to come home to. Some families in the area still leave toys out, in a little room, to make one feel welcome.
