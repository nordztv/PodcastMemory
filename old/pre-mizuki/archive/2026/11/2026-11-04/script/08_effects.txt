[excited] Effects! And today, one of the most daring choices in modern filmmaking. How Christopher Nolan showed the first atomic bomb test in Oppenheimer... without a single computer generated explosion.

[thoughtful] Here's the problem he set himself. The Trinity test, in the New Mexico desert, in July nineteen forty five, is the climax of the film. It's the most famous explosion in history. And the obvious way to do it today would be with computers. Nolan refused. In his words: "The goal was to have everything that appears in the film to be photographed." Real things, filmed by a real camera.

[curious] So how do you film a nuclear explosion... without a nuclear explosion? Step by step.

[calm] Step one: the ingredients. The special effects supervisor, Scott R Fisher, built the fireball from things that burn in very particular ways. Petrol and propane gave the big, rolling, orange fireball. And to get that terrifying first flash, the moment of light brighter than the sun, they added aluminium powder and magnesium, which burn an intense, blinding white. Fisher said: "We really wanted everyone to talk about that flash, that brightness."

[calm] Step two: size, and the camera. The explosions were as big as could be safely set off, but even a big petrol explosion isn't a mushroom cloud miles high. So the trick is speed. They filmed the explosions at very high frame rates, and then slowed them down even further. When fire moves slowly on screen, your brain reads it as huge. It's the same reason miniature waves in old films were filmed fast: slow motion equals scale.

[happy] And they used "big-atures". That's Fisher's word for large models placed very close to the camera, so they look enormous. You'll remember the bigatures from our Lord of the Rings deep dive. Same idea, but this time for a desert, a test tower, and shockwaves.

[calm] Step three: layers. The visual effects supervisor, Andrew Jackson, explained that the final Trinity blast is "multiple layers of filmed elements". One piece of film for the fireball. Another for the ground. Another for the shockwave rolling out across the desert, made with miniature dust effects. All real, all photographed, then combined. The whole film had only about two hundred visual effects shots, many of them just painting out modern things from real locations.

[impressed] Step four: the film itself. Nolan shot on IMAX, and parts of the film are in black and white. The problem? Black and white film didn't exist in the giant sixty five millimetre size that IMAX cameras use. So Kodak made it, for the first time ever, a freshly manufactured batch of its Double X black and white stock in sixty five millimetre. It had never been through an IMAX camera, so they had to make new parts for the cameras, and change the lab's machines to develop it. [teasing] That's the most Christopher Nolan sentence possible. [punch] "We needed a film stock that didn't exist, so we asked Kodak to invent it." [giggling while talking] Most of us just change a setting.

[thoughtful] And step five, maybe the cleverest of all, isn't visual at all. It's sound. When the real bomb went off, the light reached the observers instantly. The sound and the shockwave took about forty seconds to arrive. So the sound designer, Richard King, built the scene around silence. The flash, the fireball growing in total quiet, the scientists staring... and then, much later, the boom. He called it "a great moment of awe accentuated by the prolonged silence". [warm] In the cinema, that silence is almost unbearable. Which is exactly the point.

[curious] And here's a twist you'll enjoy, given today's news. Oppenheimer won seven Oscars, including Best Picture. But it wasn't even nominated for visual effects. The film that won that year? Godzilla Minus One. The small Japanese team, on a tiny budget, whose sequel just doubled its opening day yesterday. [happy] So the year the biggest practical effects film in Hollywood skipped the computers, a small team in Tokyo showed what computers can do when they're used with love.

[warm] So, the practical lesson, for you and your camera, or your game. Scale doesn't come from size. It comes from speed, distance and contrast. Film something small, slowly, close to the lens, against something calm and still... and it becomes enormous. And never underestimate silence.
