[curious] Here's the setup. In nineteen ninety four, Wong was stuck in the long, painful editing of a big martial arts epic, Ashes of Time. [calm] It's two stories, about two lovesick policemen. [calm] So, the techniques. First: handheld, almost all the time, and mostly with available light. The neon signs, the shop lights, whatever was there. That's partly a style, and partly because they were shooting in real places without permission. The first story's crowded scenes are in Chungking Mansions, a huge, chaotic building of guesthouses and shops. It was considered a fire hazard, so they had no permits, and Wong had an assistant director live there beforehand, to work out the exits. Some of the people you see in the background really lived there.

[excited] Second, the famous look. When Brigitte Lin runs through the crowd, she's sharp, but everything around her smears into streaks of colour, stuttering, like a memory. [curious] How? Two things together. They shot at a low frame rate with a slow shutter, so every frame has a lot of motion blur. Then, in the lab, they step printed it: each frame printed several times over, to bring it back up to normal speed. So the film plays at the right speed, but it moves in little jumps, and every jump is smeared. [impressed] It's a whole emotional state, made in a film lab.

[thoughtful] Third, the lenses. Wong and Doyle are famous for going very wide and very close to their actors, so faces fill the frame and the city bends around them. [curious] Wide and close for intimacy. Long and far for loneliness. Same film, two opposite tools.

[warm] And the locations are half the charm. The snack bar was a real place in Lan Kwai Fong, called Midnight Express. And Tony Leung's flat, the one Faye Wong breaks into?
