"I never said that. Torishima ordered me to do it." Every great partnership starts with somebody being volunteered. Horii remembered thinking, "isn't this guy a famous composer?"

But here's the part that matters for you. The problem Horii had wasn't the story. It was that nobody watching knew how to play this kind of game. So look at how Dragon Quest begins. You start in the king's throne room, in Tantegel Castle. The king talks to you. There are three treasure chests. And the door out of the room is locked.

That's not an accident. Horii said, in an interview in two thousand: "That's why I locked the exit from the king's room in the beginning." If players could just walk out, he said, they wouldn't know where to go, and they might wander somewhere dangerous. So the room is a lesson. You have to talk to the king, which teaches you talking. You have to open the chests and take the treasure, which teaches you searching and items. One of the chests has a key. You use the key on the door. Then you find the stairs. And here's a little secret about it. Horii was also writing articles in Shonen Jump before the game came out... teaching kids what an R P G even was. So millions of players walked into that throne room... already knowing the king would be waiting for them.

Tonight's lesson is the one I should have started with: how a Godot project actually fits together. Once this clicks, everything else this week makes sense.

Here's the everyday version. Think of a Godot game as a set of Lego models. Each little piece is a node. A node does one job. A Sprite two D node shows a picture. A Collision Shape node gives something a solid outline. A Camera two D node is the camera. An Audio Stream Player plays a sound. On their own, they're just bricks.

When you snap some bricks together and save them, that's a scene. Your farmer will be a scene: a body node, with a picture, a collision shape and maybe a camera inside it. A tree will be a scene. The farmhouse will be a scene. And here's the bit people miss at first: a scene can contain other scenes. Your Farm scene will hold the farmer scene, the tree scenes and the house scene, like a big Lego diorama made of smaller finished models.

Every scene has one node at the top, called the root. Everything else hangs under it, like a family tree. That's why the panel on the left in Godot is called the Scene Tree. The parent, children, grandchildren. And the order matters in one simple way: in two D, children are drawn on top of their parents, and lower items in the list are drawn on top of higher ones. So if you ever wonder why your farmer is hiding behind the grass, it's usually the order in that list.

Then there's the main scene. When you press play, Godot needs to know which scene to start with. That's the main scene, and you set it once, in Project Settings, under Application, then Run, then Main Scene. Or the first time you press F five, Godot asks you, and you just pick the current one. F five runs the main scene, the whole game. F six runs only the scene you have open right now, which is perfect for testing one piece by itself.

Now, a home for everything. Godot calls your project folder res, short for resource, and you'll see paths like res slash scenes slash farmer dot T S C N. Before you make anything, make four folders: scenes, scripts, art and audio. Some people keep each scene and its script together instead, and that's fine too. What matters is choosing one way and sticking to it. You're a man with eight hard drives of carefully sorted films. I know you can organise four folders. I have complete faith in you.

And now the setting that makes the biggest difference to a pixel art game, before you've drawn a single thing. By default, Godot smooths images when it scales them up. For a photo, that's lovely. For pixel art, it's a disaster: your crisp sixteen pixel farmer turns into a blurry little smudge. So in Project Settings, go to Rendering, then Textures, and set the Default Texture Filter to Nearest. Now every pixel stays a sharp little square.

Then the game size. Pixel art games usually run at a small resolution, and Godot scales the whole thing up to fit your screen. In Project Settings, under Display, then Window, set the Viewport Width to three hundred and twenty and the Height to one hundred and eighty. That's a classic size, and it scales perfectly to a ten eighty p screen. Because three hundred and twenty pixels is tiny on a Mac screen, also set the Window Width Override to twelve eighty and the Height Override to seven twenty, so the window opens at a sensible size. Then, under Stretch, set the Mode to viewport, and the Scale Mode to integer. That last one means Godot only ever scales by whole numbers, two times, three times, four times, so no pixel ever ends up wider than its neighbour.

Why three hundred and twenty by one hundred and eighty? Think about Secret of Mana. The Super Nintendo drew its pictures at about two hundred and fifty six pixels wide. Small canvases are what make those games feel cosy and readable: every tree, every enemy, every little sparkle, is a clear shape. Choosing your size now means every piece of art you make later is drawn for the right canvas. Changing it in two months is painful. Choosing it tonight takes thirty seconds.

So here's tonight's tip list, short and sweet.

One. Nodes are bricks, scenes are models, and scenes can hold other scenes.

Two. F five plays the main scene, F six plays the scene you're looking at.

Three. Nearest filter, three twenty by one eighty, viewport stretch, integer scale. Do it now, before any art.

Four. Pick a folder layout and never argue with yourself about it again.

And that's all the theory you need for tonight. The workshop has the exact clicks, and by the end of it, you'll press F five and see the very first frame of Lanternfall.

EFFECTS

Effects! And today, the film that made zombies run, twitch and snap in a way nobody had seen before. Train to Busan.

Quick refresher. Two thousand and sixteen, from South Korea, directed by Yeon Sang-ho. A zombie outbreak breaks out while a high speed train is heading from Seoul to Busan, and the passengers have to survive the trip. It cost about eight and a half million US dollars, premiered at Cannes at midnight, sold more than eleven million tickets in Korea, and took nearly a hundred million dollars around the world. And it's turning ten, with that new four K set coming in December.

The interesting thing about the effects in Train to Busan is that most of them aren't effects at all. They're bodies. So let's go through how it was built, piece by piece.

Piece one: the director. This was Yeon's first live action film. Before it, he only made animated films, including a grim animated zombie film called Seoul Station. And you can feel the animator in how he thinks about zombies. An animator doesn't think, "what does this monster look like?" They think, "how does it move?" That question drives everything else.

Piece two: the movement. The choreographer, Park Jae-in, was a former rhythmic gymnast. She'd already designed the terrifying possession movements in The Wailing, which we covered a couple of weeks ago. She told the Korean Film Council that she trained twenty to thirty actors at a time, in a studio about the size of a train carriage, so they could rehearse in exactly the space they'd be filmed in. And the dancers included performers from Korea's bone breaking dance scene, a street dance style built on making your joints look like they bend the wrong way. One of them, Jeon Young, has said he studied rabid animals, and the eerie doll figures from Ghost in the Shell Two. Yeon described what the actors had to learn as "very strange dances", with movements like having broken elbows.

Here's the clever part. Park says Yeon was always thinking about groups, not individual zombies. Some zombies are fast. Some move only in the dark. A few stand out in the chaos so your eye has somewhere to go. And the zombies keep their eyes locked on their target, even while they're crashing into walls and each other. Jeon has said that idea came from a video game: Dark Souls. So, to be clear: the scariest zombies in modern cinema were partly inspired by the game where the enemies are also, constantly, trying to kill you, with perfect focus. It's a compliment to Dark Souls, really.

Piece three: the bodies were real, and fit. Several actors who played the soldiers had just come off a film called The Tiger, where they'd been running up and down mountains, so they could handle take after take of sprinting, falling and piling on top of each other. That's why the zombie waves have such a physical weight. Those are real people throwing themselves at a train door.

Piece four: the make-up. That came from a Korean studio called Cell, which has gone on to do the creatures and zombies for Kingdom, Peninsula and Okja. For Train to Busan, they airbrushed veins onto the skin, and the look changed with each stage of the infection, so you can read how far gone someone is just from the colour of their skin and the veins on their neck. It's a neat bit of visual storytelling. The make-up is a countdown clock.

Piece five: the train itself. They built a train set copied from the inside of a real Korean K T X carriage. And instead of a green screen outside the windows, Yeon used L E D screens projecting the moving scenery, behind the set. The Korea Times reported it was the first Korean film to do that, because Yeon felt green screen made it hard to show reality. And it works. The actors could see the countryside flying past. The light from the screens fell on their faces. That's the same idea big Hollywood shows like The Mandalorian later made famous, on an eight million dollar Korean zombie film.

Piece six: the digital part. For the biggest moments, like the huge swarms and the zombies piling over each other to chase a train, the visual effects house Digital Idea added and extended crowds digitally. And there's one shot people always ask about: the deer at the start. That was real footage, combined with three D modelling. But the digital work is there to make the crowds bigger, not to replace the people. Every close up is a human.

So the practical lesson, for you, from all this. Movement sells a monster more than its design. A plain actor with grey make-up, moving with broken elbows and locked eyes, is scarier than a perfect C G I creature that moves smoothly. And that's very good news for a game. In Lanternfall, a goblin made of sixteen pixels can be genuinely unsettling if it moves wrong: a jerky little hop, a pause where it just stares at the farmer, then a sudden dash. Animate the walk before you draw the face. The face can wait. The face is always the easy part. Don't tell the artists I said that.

And if you want homework: when the new four K arrives, watch the scene where the zombies pour through the carriage doors with the sound off. You'll see the choreography. Every body has a job. And every single one of them... is looking right at you. They never blink. They're very polite about it.

DIRECTOR

The director! And after yesterday's Heat, which you gave a five, thank you, let's stay with crime, and go to Hong Kong. Johnnie To, and Election. Two thousand and five.

Johnnie To is one of the great Hong Kong directors. In nineteen ninety six, he co-founded a production company called Milkyway Image, and it became the home of some of the smartest crime films ever made in Hong Kong. Election went into competition at Cannes, and won Best Film, Best Director, Best Actor and Best Screenplay at the Hong Kong Film Awards.

The story. In a triad society called Wo Lin Shing, the members elect a new chairman every two years. Like a company board. The elders vote, they drink tea to seal it, and the winner receives a symbol of authority, an ancient carved baton called the Dragon Head Baton. This time, the quiet, smiling Lok, played by Simon Yam, wins the vote. And the loud, volatile Big D, played by Tony Leung Ka-fai, refuses to accept it. And the baton? It's been hidden somewhere across the border in mainland China. So the whole film becomes a race to get it back.

Now here's what makes it so unusual for a gangster film. There are no gunfights. None. The violence, when it comes, is with choppers, logs and rocks. It's slow, clumsy and ugly. One Hong Kong reviewer said that after the fifth or sixth blow, there's simply nothing glamorous about it. And most of the time, there's no violence at all. There's waiting. Meetings. Phone calls. Men around tables, deciding who lives.

So how did To shoot it? Let's look at the tools.

The camera. His cinematographer, Cheng Siu-keung, uses a wide screen frame and a camera that glides. It drifts slowly through rooms and around tables, never in a hurry, which gives the whole film what Variety called a "constantly simmering feel". You never feel safe, because the camera never settles.

The light. Election lives in the dark. Rooms lit by a single lamp, faces half in shadow, then sudden blasts of bright light. The contrast tells you who has power in a scene, and who's hiding something. It's beautiful, and it's always a little bit threatening.

The rituals. To spends real time on the ceremony of the triads: an elder slowly making tea, a young member reciting the triad oath while he's being beaten, the careful passing of the baton from one person to the next, in a kind of relay, to get it across the border. These rituals are where the tension lives. The more polite everyone is, the more frightened you are.

And there are two scenes people never forget. Early on, Big D has two elders who won't vote for him put into wooden crates, and rolled down a hillside. And the ending, which I won't spoil completely, happens on a peaceful family fishing trip by the water, and ends with a blood red sunset. It's the calmest setting in the film, and the most frightening scene in it.

One fascinating thing about how To works: he often shoots without a finished script, and improvises as he goes. But he said Election and its sequel were the opposite. He spent over a year on them, and called it very intense, with the research and the writing being the hard part. After that, he said, his next film, Exiled, felt "like a vacation". Imagine making a film about a triad war, and that being the relaxing one. That's a man who needs a hobby. A gentle one. Maybe farming.

A lot of critics read the Election films as a story about Hong Kong after nineteen ninety seven, about who really holds power and who's just being allowed to think they do. There's even a mainland China version of the film that To didn't want, with about ten changes, including an added undercover cop, and every criminal facing arrest at the end. The sequel, Election Two, came out in two thousand and six. It's sometimes called Triad Election, and it's even more political. It never got a mainland release.
