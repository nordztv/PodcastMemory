Game tech, and it's review day. This week in Lanternfall we've built two big foundations: a farmer who walks, and a world you can reach out and touch. So let's check what "done" looks like, how to test it, and the bugs that usually bite.

Now, a five-minute test. Think of it like a pilot's checklist before take-off.

One. Walk in all eight directions. Diagonals should be the same speed as straight lines. If diagonals feel faster, you're probably adding the directions yourself instead of using Input dot get vector, which keeps the length right for you.

Two. Walk into the fence from every side, and slide along it. If the farmer snags on corners, or gets stuck, the collision shape is usually too big or too square. Shrink it, or round it off with a capsule.

Three. Walk up to a radish and press interact once. It should pop exactly once. Four. Stand between two radishes and press interact. Only one should come up. If both do, you're looping over everything in reach and pulling it all. Pick the nearest one and stop there.

Five. Press interact standing in an empty field. Nothing should happen, and nothing should crash.

And now the three bugs that catch almost everyone, so you can spot them fast.

The silent area. You walk right up to the radish, and nothing. No error, no pull. Nine times out of ten that's collision layers and masks. The radish lives on one layer. The ghost radish. You pull a radish, it disappears, and then the game complains about something that no longer exists. That's usually a reference held onto after queue free. Let the radish announce it's leaving, through the pulled signal, and let anything holding onto it let go.

And the shimmer. Pixel art that wobbles or blurs as the farmer moves. On Wednesday we talked about the texture filter set to nearest, and the viewport stretch setting, in the project settings. Turning on pixel snapping can help too.

EFFECTS

Effects. And since you told me you loved Lady Snowblood's fight scenes, I've picked one of the most beautiful fights ever filmed. The bamboo forest, from Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon.

Ang Lee's film came out in two thousand. Chow Yun-fat plays Li Mu Bai, a great swordsman. Zhang Ziyi plays Jen, a young aristocrat who has stolen his sword, the Green Destiny. And in one scene, he chases her into a bamboo forest, and they fight... at the very tops of the bamboo. Swaying. Balancing. Floating from stem to stem, as if they weigh nothing at all.

So how do you do that without a computer-generated person?

Step one: real bamboo. The scene was filmed in a real bamboo forest in China, with stems tall and thin enough to bend when someone stands near the top. The movement you see, the sway, the bounce, is mostly real. That's why it feels so different to a digital forest.

Step two: wires. The actors wore harnesses under their costumes, attached to thin cables. The cables ran up to cranes, out of shot, and teams of crew on the ground pulled the lines, by hand and with pulleys, to lift, drop and swing them. This is wire-work, and it comes from the Hong Kong film tradition. The fights were choreographed by Yuen Woo-ping, a master of it, who also did the fights in The Matrix.

Step three, and this is the hard part: the performance. Hanging on a wire, high up among the treetops, your body wants to spin, flail and grab. The actors had to look calm, even graceful, and still do the sword moves. And Chow Yun-fat looks like he's out for a stroll.

Step four: removing the evidence. The wires were taken out afterwards, frame by frame, digitally. So the computer work isn't creating the magic. It's erasing the strings. Everything you see moving was really moving.

And step five: the music and the sound. A soft, rushing wind in the leaves. Tan Dun's score, with Yo-Yo Ma's cello. The fight is almost quiet. It's closer to a dance than a battle.

The result: the film won four Oscars, including best foreign language film and best cinematography, for Peter Pau, and it introduced a whole generation outside Asia to wuxia, the Chinese tradition of martial heroes who can do the impossible.

And there's a link to this week. Lady Snowblood's fights are fast and bloody and grounded. Crouching Tiger's are slow and floating and dreamy. Two completely different answers to the same question: how do you make a sword fight feel like art?

And here's the lesson for you, in two ways.

For your camera: you don't need wires to borrow this. When you film someone moving, film the environment moving too. Leaves, grass, a scarf, water. Real motion around a person makes the scene feel alive, in a way a still background never does.

And for Lanternfall: wire-work is really just a curve. A jump that rises fast, hangs for a moment at the top, then drifts down. In Godot, that's a tween with an ease-out on the way up and an ease-in on the way down. That little hang at the top... is the whole magic.

And one last thing... watch the bamboo fight again, and look at their feet... they almost never land. That was the point.

DIRECTOR

Director time. And let's stay with the idea of a duel, but go somewhere very different: Italy, by way of Spain, by way of the American West. Sergio Leone, and The Good, the Bad and the Ugly.

It came out in nineteen sixty six, the last of Leone's three westerns with Clint Eastwood. Three men, Eastwood's Blondie, Lee Van Cleef's Angel Eyes and Eli Wallach's Tuco, chase a fortune in buried gold across the American Civil War. It was shot mostly in Spain, with an Italian crew, and photographed by Tonino Delli Colli.

So what made Leone's camera so special? Three things.

One: the widest wides and the closest close-ups. Leone shot in Techniscope, a very wide, cheap widescreen format, and he used that width in two opposite ways. Huge landscapes, with tiny figures in them. And then faces, so close that a pair of eyes fills the whole frame from side to side. There's almost nothing in between. It's like going from a map of the world straight to a microscope.

Two: time. Leone was happy to make you wait. The famous ending is a three-way standoff in a circular cemetery called Sad Hill. Three men, three guns, nobody moving. Leone cuts between their eyes, their hands, their holsters, and the cuts get shorter and shorter as the music builds. In real time, almost nothing happens. On screen, it's unbearable. And a little secret about that cemetery... it wasn't real. It was built for the film, in the Spanish countryside... and decades later, fans dug it back out of the overgrowth to restore it.

Three: the music. Ennio Morricone's score isn't background. It's a character. That coyote-howl theme, the trumpet in the final duel. Leone often built his scenes around the music, rather than adding the music at the end. The duel is, in a way, edited like a song.

And there's a thread back to this week, too. Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill borrows from both Lady Snowblood and from Leone's westerns: the revenge story, the close-ups, the big music cues. So the woman with the umbrella and the man with no name ended up in the same film, thirty years later.

Now, the practical lesson for someone learning to film. And this one works beautifully on a trip. Like, say, this weekend in Brisbane.

Try a Leone pair. First, the widest shot you can get: the river, the bridges, the city, with one small person in it. Hold it for a good five seconds. Then cut straight to the tightest shot you can get: a hand on a railing, someone's eyes, a coffee cup being lifted. Skip the medium shot completely. That jump, from huge to tiny, is the whole style.

And then, at home, put a piece of music on the timeline first, and cut your shots to its beats, getting shorter as it builds. In Final Cut, you can add markers on the beats while the music plays, then snap your cuts to them.

And there's a version of this on your phone right now. Wide shot of the river. Extreme close-up of your coffee. Cue the trumpets. Okay, maybe hum the trumpets. Quietly. People are watching.
