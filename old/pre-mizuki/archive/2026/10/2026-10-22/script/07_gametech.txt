Tilt a Camera three D down at the plane, low and at an angle. Then move and rotate the camera, not the ground. That's it. You get the classic swooping, tilting world map. If you'd rather stay purely in two D, the other way is a shader on a TextureRect: for each row of pixels below the horizon, you work out how far away that row is, divide by that distance, rotate the coordinates, and sample your map texture there. Rows near the horizon get squashed and tiny, rows near the bottom stay big. It's a few lines of shader code, and it's lovely for a flying sequence or a world map screen.

Now, how the real thing worked.

The Super Nintendo draws its picture in layers, as we said yesterday. Normally those background layers can only scroll: slide left, right, up, down. Mode seven is a special mode where one background layer gets superpowers. It can be rotated and scaled. Spun round, zoomed in, zoomed out. Under the hood, the console applies a little bit of maths, a matrix, to every pixel, which tells it where in the big background map that pixel should come from.

That on its own gives you a flat, spinning, zooming map. Pretty, but flat. Think of a photo on a turntable under a magnifying glass.

The magic is the next step. The Super Nintendo draws the screen one line at a time, from top to bottom. And it has a feature called H D M A, which lets it quietly change settings between lines, while the picture is being drawn. So programmers changed the zoom on every single line. Lines near the top of the ground are zoomed far out, so they look distant and tiny. Lines near the bottom are zoomed in, so they look close and big. And your eye reads that as perspective. A flat floor, stretching away to a horizon.

[happy] That's F-Zero. That's Super Mario Kart. That's Pilotwings. It's not three D at all. It's a flat picture being re-zoomed two hundred and twenty four times every frame. [punch] The Super Nintendo didn't render a world. It just lied very convincingly, sixty times a second.

There were limits. Mode seven only works on one background layer, so the floor is all you get. Everything standing on it, the karts, the characters, the trees, has to be sprites drawn on top, which is why the pipes in Mario Kart always look a bit like cardboard cutouts.
