It came out in Japan in July nineteen ninety one, on the Super Famicom. It was the series' first game on the new console, directed by Hironobu {Sakaguchi|Sah kah goo chee}, with the story and design led by Takashi {Tokita|Toh kee tah}, music by Nobuo {Uematsu|Oo eh mah tsoo}, and character art by Yoshitaka {Amano|Ah mah no}. In America it came out later that year as Final Fantasy two, because the real two and three hadn't been released there. Which confused an entire generation of kids for about a decade.

It also introduced something that defined the series for years: the Active Time Battle system, designed by Hiroyuki Ito. Before that, turn-based battles waited politely for you to choose. In four, enemies keep acting while you're scrolling through menus. Suddenly every second counted. That tiny change made battles feel urgent, and it lasted right through to Final Fantasy nine.

But the real revolution was the story. Earlier Final Fantasy heroes were mostly blank warriors of light. Four gave you a hero with a past, and a guilty conscience.

Cecil Harvey is a dark knight, and the captain of the Red Wings, the airship fleet of the kingdom of Baron. And the game opens with him doing something terrible. Under orders from his king, he raids the peaceful town of {Mysidia|Mih sid ee ah} to take its crystal, and his soldiers cut down people who barely fight back. Then, on the flight home, Cecil does something no Final Fantasy hero had really done before. He doubts. He questions whether this is right.

The king doesn't like questions. He strips Cecil of his command, and sends him and his friend Kain, a dragoon, on a simple errand: deliver a ring to a village called Mist. And when they arrive, the ring opens, and a swarm of bomb monsters bursts out and sets the whole village on fire. The king had used them as the delivery men for a massacre.

In the ashes of Mist, Cecil meets a little girl called {Rydia|Rid ee ah}. Her mother was a summoner, and she died because of what happened. Rydia's grief summons a titan that splits the ground and separates Cecil from Kain. And Cecil, the man who just helped destroy her home, chooses to protect her. That's the moment he turns away from Baron.

What follows is one of the most tangled casts of the era. There's Rosa, the white mage who loves him. Tellah, an old sage hunting the man who caused his daughter's death. The twin child mages {Palom|Pah lom} and {Porom|Por om}. The monk Yang. Edward, the bard prince. Cid, the engineer. And Kain, who is turned against Cecil, again and again, by a sorcerer in black armour called {Golbez|Gol bez}. Kain is basically the series' patron saint of the friend who betrays you. You lose count of how many times.

And then, the mountain. To have any chance against Golbez, Cecil climbs Mount Ordeals, to shed the darkness he's carried his whole life and become a paladin. At the summit, he faces a reflection of himself, the dark knight he used to be. And here's the beautiful bit of design. You can't win that fight by attacking. If you swing at him, it does nothing. The only way through is to defend, and endure, and refuse to fight your old self. The game makes you play the theme. Redemption isn't about beating your past. It's about not becoming it again.

When the light fades, Cecil is a paladin, starting from level one. Your powerful dark knight, gone. All that strength, traded for a fresh start. It's one of the boldest things a nineteen ninety one game could do to a player.

The game is also famous for its sacrifices. Tellah casts a forbidden spell that costs him his life. Palom and Porom turn themselves to stone to save the party from a crushing room. Yang throws himself into a machine to stop it. Some of those later turn out not quite so final. But at the time, for kids playing on a Super Nintendo, it was a shock: party members could leave, and they could die.

And the twist at the end is pure Final Fantasy. Golbez, the man who's been hunting Cecil all game, is his older brother. Their father was one of the Lunarians, an ancient people who live under the surface of the moon. Golbez himself is being controlled by another Lunarian called {Zemus|Zee mus}. So the final act takes Cecil, by an ancient whale-shaped ship, to the moon. Literally. Final Fantasy went to the moon before it was cool.

What makes four matter so much, beyond nostalgia, is that it's the moment the series became character drama. Cecil questioning his orders. Rydia's grief. Kain's jealousy. It's the template for Cloud's identity crisis in seven, and Squall's walls in eight, and a whole generation of conflicted Final Fantasy leads.
