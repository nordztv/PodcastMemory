Tech. And this week, the story of the chip industry is a strange split screen. On one side, record breaking results. On the other, a market that suddenly got nervous.

Let's start with the records, because they're genuinely staggering.

TSMC, the Taiwanese company that manufactures the most advanced chips for almost everyone, Apple, Nvidia, AMD, reported its third quarter. Revenue was about forty six point seven billion US dollars, up forty one per cent from a year ago in US dollar terms. Profit was up almost sixty nine per cent. And here's the number that tells you what kind of business this is. Its net profit margin was fifty one per cent. For every dollar that came in, more than half was profit. Chips made on TSMC's newest two nanometre process are already eight per cent of wafer revenue, and three nanometre is a third. Seventy nine per cent of everything it makes is seven nanometre or smaller. And it's guiding for even more next quarter, somewhere between fifty two and fifty four billion dollars.

Then there's ASML, the Dutch company that makes the machines TSMC uses. If TSMC is the world's chip kitchen, ASML makes the only ovens that can cook the most advanced recipes. Their extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, called E U V, are the only way to print the tiniest chip features, and ASML is the only company on Earth that makes them. Record sales of eleven point seven billion euros, up fifty five per cent. They shipped seventeen E U V machines in the quarter, almost double last year. But the most interesting line was a warning. ASML says it won't be able to make enough machines to meet demand in twenty twenty seven or twenty twenty eight, even after boosting capacity by thirty per cent. Think about that. The bottleneck for artificial intelligence is now, partly, a factory in the Netherlands. Also notable, China's share of ASML's sales has fallen from forty two per cent a year ago to twenty three per cent, as export controls bite.

Samsung pre-announced its own quarter and forecast a record operating profit of around one hundred and seven trillion won, roughly eighty billion US dollars, driven by the AI memory boom.

So why did chip stocks fall this week? On Wednesday, American inflation came in hotter than expected. When inflation runs hot, markets start worrying that the US Federal Reserve might raise interest rates rather than cut them. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, and the AI build out runs on enormous amounts of borrowed money. So Nvidia fell, Micron fell, and the AI cloud company CoreWeave fell more than sixteen per cent in a day over worries about its debt.

Korea got hit hardest. On Thursday, the Kospi dropped as much as seven per cent during the day, enough for the exchange to trigger its emergency trading curbs. SK Hynix fell more than eleven per cent and Samsung more than nine. For SK Hynix there were two extra worries. Korean media reported that some of its equipment orders for H B M four, the next generation of the stacked high bandwidth memory that sits right next to AI chips, may slip from this year into next. And China's ChangXin Memory announced progress on a new, denser kind of D RAM. Now, there's no evidence SK Hynix's production schedule is actually in trouble, and the company says demand for H B M four is strong. Its head of research said AI inference could grow AI infrastructure around twenty times by twenty thirty. And there's an interesting forecast doing the rounds in Seoul, that Samsung could overtake SK Hynix as the top H B M supplier next year. That rivalry is one to watch.

Nvidia. Jensen Huang told Bloomberg on Friday that the market turbulence will pass, and that the long term benefits of AI are what matter.
