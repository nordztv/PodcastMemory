Movies. Let's start with a film close to home for you, because it's Korean, it's a monster movie, and it's from one of the best genre directors alive.

Hope, from Na Hong-jin. If you've seen The Chaser, The Yellow Sea or The Wailing, you know his style. Long, punishing, muddy, frightening films where the police are always one step behind and the countryside feels like it's hiding something. Hope is his biggest swing yet. It's set in a remote harbour town near the demilitarised zone, called Hope, where something is ripping apart cattle and tearing through the town. Hwang Jung-min, who was unforgettable as the shaman in The Wailing, plays a local cop. Zo In-sung plays a local man who leads a group of hunters into the forest after the creature. And here's the surprise. Michael Fassbender and Alicia Vikander are in it too, in motion capture roles connected to the creatures and where they come from. It premiered at Cannes, and it was a box office sensation in Korea. It's now on digital through Mubi.

Reviews are split in an interesting way. Time Out gave it three stars out of five, and the reviewer basically says the first hour is fantastic, a creature kept hidden in the shadows, real midnight movie energy, but that it goes off the rails later, with repetitive chases, a forest that doesn't feel connected to the village, and some cheap looking effects. When it works, he compares it to Predator and Tremors. When it doesn't, to Prometheus. Honestly, that sounds like exactly the kind of movie that's worth seeing for yourself.

Also new in cinemas, Whalefall. Directed by Brian Duffield, who made No One Will Save You, adapted from Daniel Kraus's novel. A young diver goes looking for his father's remains off the California coast, gets swallowed by a sperm whale, and has about an hour of oxygen to get out. Josh Brolin co-stars. It's one of those one line premises that makes your chest tighten just hearing it. Now, retro. This week Warner Brothers put Ken Russell's The Devils from nineteen seventy one back into cinemas, in the director's cut, restored. It's a film about hysteria, religion and power in seventeenth century France, starring Oliver Reed and Vanessa Redgrave. It was so controversial that for decades it mostly existed in cut, censored versions. This release is the first N C seventeen film in Warner Brothers' one hundred and three year history. If it ever plays near you, it's an experience.

And Arrow Video just announced its November slate, and it's a good one for you.

Clash of the Titans, from nineteen eighty one, with a new 4K restoration from the original camera negative, Dolby Vision, and a new feature length documentary called Courage and Imagination. This was Ray Harryhausen's last film. Every creature, Medusa, the Kraken, the scorpions, Bubo the mechanical owl, was animated by hand, one frame at a time. More on that kind of craft later.

The Dirty Dozen, from nineteen sixty seven, also in 4K from the camera negative, with a restored seventy millimetre sound mix.

WarGames and Fargo on 4K in the UK. And for the Fargo release there's an archival commentary from Roger Deakins, the cinematographer. If you're learning colour and light, a Deakins commentary is basically a free masterclass.

And two for the Hong Kong lovers. Shawscope Presents, Street Gangs of Hong Kong. Seven Shaw Brothers films from nineteen seventy three to nineteen eighty, newly restored in 2K from the original negatives. These are gritty urban crime films, the grandparents of the Hong Kong gangster movies of the eighties and nineties, of John Woo and Johnnie To. And a Nora Miao double feature, The Blade Spares None and The Hurricane. Nora Miao is a fascinating figure. She acted alongside Bruce Lee in Fist of Fury and The Way of the Dragon.

The Arrow releases land in the second half of November.

One more thing for Japan horror fans, because a lot of us discovered horror through that wave of films. Exit Eight, the movie based on the indie game about walking through an endless underground passage in Tokyo, looking for anomalies, has been one of the most talked about Japanese horror films of the past year. If you've ever played the game, you know how something so simple can become deeply unsettling. It's a perfect example of what Japanese horror does best. It doesn't throw a monster at you. It makes something ordinary feel wrong.
