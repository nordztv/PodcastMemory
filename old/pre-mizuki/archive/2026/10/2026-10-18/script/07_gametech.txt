Deep dive number two. Game technology. Three ideas today. How DLSS works, what frame interpolation really is, and the ancient art of parallax scrolling. Plus a couple of little tricks you've probably never heard of that will make your own game feel better.

Let's start with DLSS. It stands for Deep Learning Super Sampling. The basic problem it solves is this. Drawing a modern game at 4K is incredibly expensive. Four times the pixels of ten eighty p means roughly four times the work for lighting, shadows, reflections and ray tracing. So what if the game drew a smaller image, and something clever made it look like a big one?

The first version of DLSS, in twenty eighteen, with the RTX twenty series cards, was honestly not great. Nvidia trained a separate AI model for each game, and it worked on one frame at a time. It tried to guess missing detail from a single low resolution picture, and the results were often soft and smeary.

The breakthrough was DLSS two, in twenty twenty, and the key idea is time. Here's how it works. The game renders each frame at a lower resolution, but every frame, it shifts the camera by a tiny fraction of a pixel. A different tiny jitter each frame. That means each frame samples slightly different points in the scene. Over several frames, you've actually gathered far more detail than any single frame contains. The game also hands DLSS two extra pieces of information. Motion vectors, which say where every pixel moved since the last frame, and a depth buffer, which says how far away everything is.

Now the job is to combine the current frame with the history of previous frames to build one sharp, high resolution image. The hard part is knowing when to trust the history. If a character walks in front of a wall, the old pixels of the wall behind them are now wrong. If you use them anyway, you get ghosting, smeared trails behind moving objects. Older techniques used hand written rules to decide what history to throw away. DLSS replaced those rules with a neural network trained on enormous numbers of perfect, super high quality reference images. The network learned, from millions of examples, what to keep and what to reject. That's why DLSS can sometimes look sharper than the game rendered at native resolution with older anti aliasing.

And because it needs real speed, it runs on special parts of Nvidia's chips called tensor cores, which are built for exactly the kind of maths neural networks do.

Then came DLSS three in twenty twenty two, and that's where frame interpolation comes in. This is what you asked about.

Interpolation means creating something in between two things you already know. Your television probably does a version of this. It's called motion smoothing, and it's the reason films sometimes look weirdly cheap, like a soap opera, on a new TV. The TV takes two real frames and invents a frame in between, so twenty four frames per second becomes forty eight or more.

DLSS frame generation does this for games, but much more cleverly. The game renders frame one, then frame two. Before showing frame two, the GPU builds a brand new frame that sits halfway between them. It uses the game's motion vectors, plus a dedicated piece of hardware that tracks how things like shadows, reflections and particles move, which motion vectors miss. So if the game renders sixty frames per second, you see one hundred and twenty.

But there's a catch, and it's important. To create a frame between frame one and frame two, you have to wait for frame two to exist. So the game holds back a real frame for a moment. That adds a little delay between pressing a button and seeing the result. It also means the game still responds to your controls at the original sixty, even though it looks like one hundred and twenty. That's why Nvidia pairs it with a technology called Reflex, which trims delay elsewhere in the pipeline. And it's why frame generation feels great when the game is already running well, and not so great when the base frame rate is low. If the real frames are thirty, interpolation can make it look smooth, but it'll still feel like thirty in your hands, maybe slightly worse.

Then DLSS four, in twenty twenty five, with the RTX fifty cards. Two big changes. First, multi frame generation. Instead of one invented frame between real ones, it can create up to three. So one real frame, three generated. Second, Nvidia switched the AI model from an older type, a convolutional network, to a transformer, the same broad family of AI design behind modern language models. Transformers are better at looking at the whole image and its history at once, so fine details like hair, fences and text hold together better in motion.

And now DLSS five is appearing, starting with N B A 2K27 on the new RTX Spark laptops, with something Nvidia calls 3 D guided neural rendering. Nvidia hasn't explained all of it yet, but the direction is clear. Rather than just upscaling flat pixels, the AI is being fed more information about the actual three dimensional scene, so that it's helping to draw the image, not just enlarge it.

Fun connection for you. The Nintendo Switch two uses an Nvidia chip that supports DLSS. That's one reason some Switch two games can reach much higher resolutions than the console's raw power would suggest. AMD has its own version, called FSR, and Intel has XeSS. They all use the same ideas. Jitter, history, motion vectors, and increasingly, AI.

Now let's go back in time. Parallax.

Parallax is something your eyes understand without thinking. Look out of a train window. The fence posts right next to the tracks fly past. The trees further away move more slowly. The mountains on the horizon barely move at all. The difference in speed tells your brain how far away everything is.

Animators figured this out long before video games. Walt Disney's studio built the multiplane camera in the nineteen thirties. A huge vertical rig, with painted glass layers stacked one above another, and the camera looking down through all of them. By moving the layers at different speeds, they could make flat paintings feel deep. They tested it on a short film called The Old Mill in nineteen thirty seven, and then used it in Snow White.

In games, the arcade shooter Moon Patrol, from nineteen eighty two, is usually credited as one of the first to use parallax scrolling, with a moon buggy driving in front of mountains and hills sliding past at different speeds. Then the sixteen bit era turned it into an art. The Mega Drive had two separate background layers in hardware, and clever programmers learned to change the scroll speed of individual lines of the screen as the picture was being drawn. That's how Sonic the Hedgehog's Green Hill Zone has water and distant hills that seem to stretch away in strips. The Super Nintendo had up to four background layers in some modes, plus Mode seven, which could rotate and scale a whole background layer. That's how Secret of Mana gives you that swooping flight across the world on the back of Flammie.

And you can do this in your own game right now. In Godot four, there's a node called Parallax two D. You put your background art inside it and set its scroll scale. A scroll scale of one means the layer moves exactly with the camera, like the ground your character walks on. Zero point five means it moves at half speed, so it feels far away. Zero point one feels like distant mountains or sky. And here's the trick most beginners miss. Use a scroll scale bigger than one for a foreground layer. Some leaves, branches or pillars that pass in front of the player, moving faster than the ground. That single layer adds an enormous sense of depth.

For a top down game like yours, which looks down on the world like Stardew Valley, classic side scrolling parallax isn't used as much. But you can still use it. Cloud shadows drifting across the fields at a slightly different speed to the ground. A title screen. Or a Flammie style flying sequence. One warning with pixel art. If layers move at fractional speeds, pixels can shimmer or jitter. Godot has pixel snapping settings to help with that.

And finally, two tiny tricks you may never have heard of, that game developers use to make controls feel good.

The first is called coyote time, named after Wile E. Coyote, who runs off a cliff and hangs in the air before he falls. In platform games, if you press jump a split second after running off a ledge, the game lets you jump anyway, usually within a tenth of a second. Players never notice it. They just feel like the game is fair.

The second one is more useful for your kind of game. It's called corner correction, or collision nudging. In a top down game, if the player walks into a doorway but they're a few pixels off centre, they snag on the corner of the wall and stop. It feels terrible. So the game quietly slides them sideways, around the corner and through the door. The classic Zelda games and Secret of Mana style games use tricks like this everywhere. If your character ever feels sticky on corners, that's the fix.
