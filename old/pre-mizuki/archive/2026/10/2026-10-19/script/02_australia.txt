Australian politics now, and the federal government has spent the weekend explaining itself over a rather unexpected subject: paying your tax with a credit card.

Here's the background. Back in late September, the Australian Taxation Office decided it would stop accepting credit cards from the first of December. That was triggered by the Reserve Bank scrapping card surcharges from the first of October. Without a surcharge, the tax office would have had to swallow the card fees itself, and it said absorbing a cost of around two hundred million dollars wasn't tenable.

Business groups were not impressed. The A B C reports the Prime Minister stepped in after a text message from Andrew McKellar at the Australian Chamber of Commerce and Industry. The fix: the government will cover the cost so the tax office keeps taking cards until the middle of twenty twenty seven.

The awkward part came on Sunday. On Insiders, Assistant Treasurer Daniel Mulino conceded the government knew back in July that the tax office was considering the ban. Shadow Treasurer Tim Wilson pounced, saying the government had three months to fix this, and describing the relationship between the Prime Minister and Treasurer Jim Chalmers as a psychodrama. Chalmers denies he was rolled.

A quick, fair observation. The government's case is that it acted as soon as the impact on business became clear. But if it knew in July, the obvious question is why the fix only arrived after a public backlash. That's a question about process and timing, and it's one the government will keep getting asked. Equally, the opposition's psychodrama line is colourful, but it's a claim about personalities rather than the policy, and there's no evidence in the reporting that the outcome would have been any different.

That feeds into a bigger theme. A B C political analysis this weekend says Labor M Ps are getting restless at the mid point of the term. The list of self inflicted headaches includes this tax office mess, the capital gains changes, the new minimum tax on trusts and the card surcharge saga. Jim Chalmers has flagged another savings package before the end of the year. And the Prime Minister reportedly told caucus that Labor would be kicking with the wind in the fourth quarter. The next election is due by May twenty twenty eight, so roughly eighteen months away. On the other side, analysis also notes the Liberals under Angus Taylor are struggling to lift their primary vote much above twenty per cent, so neither side has much to celebrate.

Following up from yesterday. The plan to wind back the private health insurance rebate for over sixty fives, worth around three billion dollars, was blocked in the Senate last week by the Coalition, the Greens and the crossbench. Health Minister Mark Butler had said the money would go to aged care. The government reintroduced the bill straight away, but the A B C's reading is that it will be very hard to save without giving up a large slice of that money.

And on trusts: the government has introduced a bill to fix what it calls unintended consequences in its thirty per cent minimum tax on discretionary trusts. The government says these changes were always planned. Critics see it as a patch on a policy that wasn't ready. Watch this one when parliament next sits.

On the European Union trade deal, the Coalition's position is still, shall we say, a work in progress. Nationals leader Matt Canavan said the Coalition opposes the deal, then narrowed that to opposing it in its current form, citing climate commitments, steel and farmers. Bridget McKenzie says the Coalition is absolutely united. But two Liberal senators have said they back the deal. There's been no formal shadow cabinet or party room decision, and that's not expected until after a parliamentary committee reviews the deal early next year. Trade Minister Don Farrell says the deal is done and won't be renegotiated. So yesterday's follow up item, the Coalition party room decision, is now a next year story.

Over at the Greens, Senator Jordon Steele John is denying allegations of inappropriate conduct towards women, raised in a letter from seven party members to leader David Shoebridge. Steele John says the claims are false, says police investigated and laid no charges, and has criticised Shoebridge for going public. The W A Greens are setting up an independent process. As always with allegations like these, it's a matter for that process, and I'll leave it there.

Also this morning, Four Corners reports that twenty four women working in the federal public service allege they faced pregnancy discrimination inside government departments. And out off the coast of Western Australia, Greenpeace protesters occupied a platform at Woodside's Scarborough gas project, around two hundred kilometres offshore. Two are in custody.

Now to New South Wales.

The biggest Sydney story this weekend is a worrying one from Canterbury Hospital. An unmonitored email inbox was found holding around three thousand four hundred unread emails, including sixteen elective surgery referrals, some going back to late last year. Four of those were category one, meaning surgery should happen within thirty days, and twelve were category two, within ninety days. The Sydney Local Health District has apologised and launched a review. The A M A in New South Wales warned of significant risk to patients, and the opposition's Mark Coure wants an independent review. At the time of the reporting, Health Minister Ryan Park hadn't commented. I'll keep an eye on this.

In happier news for the inner city, the last ever greyhound race at Wentworth Park was run on Friday. The Minns government says the site will become an expanded fourteen hectare parkland with new sporting fields, handed to the City of Sydney after demolition, with early works before Christmas. The government also says up to two thousand five hundred extra homes are proposed nearby, as part of up to seven thousand three hundred across Ultimo and Pyrmont. More green space a short trip from your office, Scott. I'll allow it.

The Minns government also says its one billion dollar pre sale finance guarantee has now helped speed up almost one thousand three hundred new homes, with the latest projects in Moorebank and Kingsford. That's the government's own figure, so treat it as their claim.

For your commute, there's a new train timetable coming on the fifteenth of November, with extra services on the T4, T8 and South Coast lines, according to the government. The T8 is your Airport line through Green Square, so that might be one to watch. The state's fourth round of strata reforms has also passed parliament, and Clarence Valley Council up north has been suspended for three months.
