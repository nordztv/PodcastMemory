Right, Scott, time for the director's chair. And today it all ties together nicely. Sigourney Weaver is our Actress of the Month, and you've already got Aliens on your watch list from the movies segment. So let's go back to where her story started: Ridley Scott's Alien, from nineteen seventy nine.

Here's the thing people forget. When Ridley Scott made Alien, he'd only directed one feature film. Before that he'd spent years in television commercials. He made hundreds of them through the seventies, including the famous British bread ad with the boy pushing his bike up a cobbled hill. Commercials taught him to make every single frame look gorgeous, very fast, on someone else's money.

His first feature was The Duellists, a period drama about two officers in Napoleon's army who keep fighting each other for years. It looked like a painting, it won a prize at Cannes, and it got him noticed. Alien was only his second film. No pressure.

The script came from Dan O'Bannon and Ronald Shusett, and the pitch was basically "Jaws in space". What Scott brought was the look. And the way he got it starts with a pencil.

Scott storyboarded the film himself, shot by shot. These drawings got a nickname, Ridleygrams. They weren't stick figures either. They showed lighting, lenses, framing and mood. The well-known story is that when the studio saw those boards, they could finally picture the film, and the budget was roughly doubled. The exact figures vary depending on who's telling it, but the point stands. His drawings sold the movie.

And on set, he often operated the camera himself, especially the handheld shots. Most directors stand by a monitor and point. Scott wanted his own eye on the frame. When the camera drifts through the corridors looking for the crew, that's often the director's own hands you're feeling.

Now, the Nostromo. This is my favourite part. Scott didn't want a gleaming, clean spaceship. He wanted a working ship, a space truck. Grimy, scuffed and lived-in, with the crew grumbling about pay. The interior sets were built at Shepperton Studios, and many of them were connected into a continuous ship. The actors could walk from one section to the next, through real corridors, with ceilings, so the space felt cramped and real.

Then he filled it with smoke and haze, and lit it with practical lights: the lights you can actually see in the shot, like panels, monitors and strobes. The haze catches every beam. Faces fall in and out of shadow. The ship feels damp and alive, which is exactly how you want a place to feel when something is hiding in it.

The creature itself came from the Swiss artist H. R. Giger. His work mixes the mechanical and the biological, all bones, tubes and ribs, beautiful and deeply wrong at the same time. Giger designed the creature in its stages, and the alien world and the derelict ship as well. The giant dead pilot in his chair, often called the Space Jockey, is pure Giger.

And inside the suit was Bolaji Badejo, a very tall Nigerian design student. The story goes that he was spotted in a London pub. He was well over two metres tall and very slim. Put him in that suit and his proportions just don't look human, which was exactly the idea. He reportedly took movement classes to get that slow, unnatural way of moving.

But here's the real genius. Scott barely shows you the creature. It's mostly glimpses: a tail, a hand, a shape in the steam, a head lowering out of the dark. Partly that was clever, and partly it was practical, because a man in a rubber suit only stays scary for so long in full light. Your imagination does the rest, and your imagination is far nastier than any costume.

Then there's the chestburster scene. You know the one. John Hurt's character, Kane, at the dinner table. The cast knew roughly what was going to happen. What they reportedly didn't know was how much blood was coming, or quite how far it would spray. So many of those shocked reactions are real. Veronica Cartwright, who played Lambert, famously copped a face full of it.

Honestly, it's the most relatable office lunch ever filmed. Someone always ruins it. Usually it's Kevin from accounts, though, and he rarely has teeth in his ribcage.

Sound matters just as much as the pictures. Jerry Goldsmith wrote the score, and it's eerie and beautiful. But the finished film leans hard on sound design and silence. The hum of the ship, Jones the cat, dripping water, and the motion tracker beeping faster and faster. In the quietest moments you start holding your breath without noticing. And of course there was the tagline on the poster: "In space, no one can hear you scream." Eight words, and a whole genre was sold.

Here's the lovely link. Sigourney Weaver was a fairly unknown stage actor when she got the part of Ripley. Seven years later, James Cameron made the sequel, Aliens. That's the same Cameron whose liquid-metal T one thousand we took apart yesterday. Scott made a haunted house in space, and Cameron made a war movie. Same woman at the centre of both, and that's why she's our actress this month.

So, the lesson for you, Scott, and you can try it on the walk this morning.

Hide the monster. Whatever your subject is, the camera doesn't have to show all of it. Shoot a shape behind the leaves in the Botanic Garden. Shoot a silhouette against the light coming down through the trees in Hyde Park. Or shoot just a pair of shoes walking past the frame. Let the viewer finish the picture.

Second, look for haze and backlight. Early morning, steam from a coffee cart or mist off the grass, with the sun behind it. That's free Ridley Scott lighting. Put the light behind your subject, expose for the bright part, and let the shadows fall where they fall.

Third, listen. When you get the X M five, record a few seconds of just the sound wherever you film. The train arriving, birds, footsteps on the path. In the edit, sound is what makes a place feel real. Sometimes a little silence makes it feel tense.

And if you're doing all this while walking backwards to frame up a fern, Scott, please watch out for the joggers. I'm rather keen to keep you in one piece. Shoot less, suggest more. That's the whole lesson in four words.
