GAME TECH

Game tech now, and today it's procedural generation. That's making content with rules and randomness instead of placing every single thing by hand. And we'll do it the way you like: the Godot bit first, then the theory.

Here's the practical job. Your farm needs a world that feels alive every morning: weeds, rocks and sticks scattered across the fields, forageable plants in the woods, maybe a mushroom patch that moves. Stardew Valley does exactly this. Every day, the game sprinkles debris and forage around the map with some randomness, so the same farm never looks quite the same twice. You don't want to hand-place three hundred weeds. Nobody does. You want rules.

Step one in Godot four: make a RandomNumberGenerator, and give it a seed. A seed is just a number that kicks off the random sequence. Same seed, same sequence, every time. So if you build the seed from the save file's world number plus the day number, then day twelve always looks like day twelve, which makes bugs reproducible and save files tiny. You don't store three hundred weeds; you store one number and regenerate them.

Step two: decide where things can go. Loop over the cells of your TileMapLayer, and for each cell ask a few questions. Is it a grass tile? You can use the custom data layer we set up in the tilemaps episode to tag tiles as "grass", "tilled" or "path". Is it already occupied? Is it too close to the farmhouse door? Only then roll the dice: if the random number is under, say, three per cent, spawn a weed there.

Step three, and this is where it starts to look good: use noise instead of plain dice. Godot has a built-in FastNoiseLite resource. Noise gives you smooth randomness: values that change gently from one cell to the next, rather than jumping about. Sample the noise at each cell's position, and only spawn mushrooms where the value is high. Suddenly, instead of mushrooms dotted evenly like a sprinkle of salt, you get natural-looking clusters, damp patches in the woods. Set the noise's seed from the same day seed, and the patches move each day but stay consistent.

Step four: layer your rules. A good recipe is something like: noise decides the general area, a dice roll decides each individual spot, and a minimum distance check stops things overlapping. Rocks near the cliffs, weeds near the fences, rare flowers only where the noise is very high and the dice roll is very low. That combination of a big smooth pattern plus small random choices is ninety per cent of procedural generation in games.

And yes, this is exactly how your radish monster's world could work too. Use the day's seed to decide which field the radishes are angry in today. Some mornings... the vegetables wake up on the wrong side of the soil. Okay, moving on.

Now the theory, with an everyday analogy. Think of a seed like the number on a recipe card in a giant cookbook. The cookbook is your code. The seed just tells you which page to open. Anyone with the same cookbook and the same page number bakes exactly the same cake. That's why Minecraft players can share a seed and walk around the identical world, and why No Man's Sky could fit eighteen quintillion planets on a disc. It isn't storing planets. It's storing the cookbook.

Noise is the other big idea. And here's a little secret... Ken Perlin invented his famous Perlin noise for the film Tron in the early eighties, to make computer graphics look less clean and fake. And he later won... an Academy Award for it. Imagine a hilly landscape seen from above: high ground and low ground that roll smoothly rather than flicking up and down. Minecraft's mountains, valleys and caves come from layers of noise like that, stacked at different scales: big, slow noise for continents, smaller, faster noise for hills and bumps.

The third classic technique is templates. The game Spelunky, made by Derek Yu, builds each level from a grid of rooms. Each room is picked from a set of hand-made templates, and the generator makes sure there's always a path from the top to the exit. That's the secret of good procedural design: hand-made pieces, assembled by rules, with guarantees. Pure randomness makes boring or broken worlds. Randomness with constraints makes interesting ones. Diablo's dungeons work in a similar way, and so do roguelikes like Hades, where the rooms are hand-built but the order and rewards are rolled.

And the warning every designer learns: random isn't the same as fun. Players notice patterns. Too even, and it feels fake; too random, and it feels unfair. Lots of games quietly cheat, for example making sure you never get three bad drops in a row, or that a rare item is guaranteed after enough attempts. That's sometimes called a pity timer, and your radish counter from a couple of weeks ago is basically a cousin of it. That one was too easy.

Quick tips to finish. Always seed your generator, so you can reproduce what you see. Generate in layers, from big to small. Keep a "do not spawn" mask for paths, doors and spots the player needs. Test with lots of seeds: write a tiny loop that generates fifty days and saves a screenshot of each, and look for broken days. And remember the golden rule: procedural generation is a way to place your hand-made things, not a replacement for making them.

EFFECTS

Effects deep dive now. Last week we did the bigatures and forced perspective behind the Lord of the Rings. Today it's the trilogy's other great trick, and arguably the more important one: how Gollum was made, and how one performance changed visual effects forever.

When Peter Jackson started, the plan for Gollum was fairly traditional. He'd be a fully computer-animated character, voiced by an actor. Andy Serkis was hired for the voice. But in rehearsals, something happened. Serkis didn't just do a voice. He threw his whole body into it: crouching, twitching, contorting, crawling across the floor, coughing out that wet, rasping "gollum" sound, which he's said was inspired by his cat coughing up a fur ball. Right. Focus. Jackson saw that and realised the performance was the character. So Serkis went from voice actor to actually being on set.

That created a practical problem, because Gollum isn't a man in a suit. So the scenes were shot in several ways. First, Serkis would act the scene with Elijah Wood and Sean Astin, in a skin-tight suit, so the other actors had a real partner to look at, react to, wrestle with. Then the camera would repeat the same move with Serkis gone, giving the effects team a clean background. Sometimes they'd do a third pass with Serkis performing just off camera, so the hobbits' eyelines stayed right. Later, Serkis went onto a motion capture stage and performed the scenes again, with markers on his suit, so the computer could record how his body moved.

But here's the bit that people often get wrong... In the Two Towers, Gollum's face wasn't motion captured. The animators at Weta Digital studied Serkis's filmed performance... frame by frame... and hand-animated Gollum's face to match it. Every sneer, every flicker of hope, every collapse into self-pity. The body could be captured. The soul had to be copied by hand, by artists watching an actor.

Gollum also got a redesign along the way. An early version glimpsed in the first film looked more like a generic creature, and as Serkis's performance became central, the design was pushed closer to Serkis's own features, so the animators could transfer his expressions more naturally. Weta also put a lot of effort into his skin, using a technique called subsurface scattering, which lets light pass a little way into the skin before bouncing out, the way your ear glows red when the sun is behind it. That was a big deal at the time. It's why Gollum looks pale, clammy and almost translucent, rather than plastic.

And then there's the scene everyone remembers: Sméagol and Gollum arguing with each other. One character, two personalities, talking it out at night by a stream. Jackson shot it with simple, brilliant film grammar. When Gollum speaks, the camera favours one side; when Sméagol answers, it cuts to the other, as if they're two people in a conversation. Serkis played both, shifting his posture, voice and eyes between them. The animators matched him. The result is a fully digital character carrying a scene of real psychological drama, and that just hadn't been done before at that level.

I do think about the hours, though. The effects artists, in a dark room, for months... studying one man's face pulling the most miserable expressions imaginable... frame by frame... until they knew every twitch of it better than their own families' faces. Then going home... looking in the mirror... and quietly checking. Just in case.

By the Return of the King, Serkis even appears as himself, in a flashback showing Sméagol before the ring ruined him, which made the link between actor and creature explicit.

The legacy is enormous. Gollum is the reason performance capture became a craft rather than a technical trick. Serkis went on to play King Kong for Jackson, and then Caesar in the Planet of the Apes films, where the capture finally moved onto real locations, outdoors, with facial cameras on helmets recording his face directly. Avatar built its whole production around the same idea. And in games, every character whose face really acts, from Kratos to Ellie in the Last of Us, owes something to that crouching man in the skin-tight suit.

The lesson for you, if you're ever animating a character in your own game: animate from reference. Film yourself doing the action, even badly, and copy the timing. It's the same thing Weta did, and it's why their creature felt alive.

DIRECTOR

For the director deep dive, I'm changing the plan. With Ryu Murakami's death this week, it felt right to look at the film that carried his name around the world: Takashi Miike's Audition, from 1999. Fair warning, it's one of the most notorious horror films ever made, so I'll talk about how it works rather than dwelling on the nasty parts.

First, the director. Takashi Miike is famously, almost absurdly, prolific. He has made well over a hundred films and T V projects, sometimes several in a single year: yakuza films, family musicals, samurai epics, children's adventures, and some of the most extreme horror ever put on screen. Audition is the one that made him internationally famous.

The story starts like a gentle drama. Aoyama, played by Ryo Ishibashi, is a widower, a decent, lonely, middle-aged man whose teenage son thinks he should remarry. His friend, a film producer, comes up with an idea: hold auditions for a film that will never actually be made, and let Aoyama quietly choose a wife from the actresses who apply. It's a creepy idea, and the film knows it. One of the applicants stands out: Asami, a quiet former ballet dancer, played by Eihi Shiina, in her first major film role. Aoyama is smitten.

Here's the first thing to notice as a filmmaker. For almost an hour, Miike makes a calm, almost tender romantic drama. The camera is patient. Scenes play out in long, steady takes. The colours are soft and ordinary. It feels like a slightly melancholy Japanese family film. That's not padding. That's the trap. Miike spends an hour making you comfortable, so that you feel the floor drop away when it goes.

And the moment it starts to turn... is one of the great shots in horror. Asami, in her bare, empty apartment, sits on the floor in a white dress, head bowed, beside the telephone, waiting for Aoyama to call. Behind her is a large canvas sack. The camera just sits there, wide and still. Nothing moves. Then the phone rings. And as she slowly lifts her head and smiles... the sack lurches. That's it. No music sting, no fast cut, no jump scare. Just a static frame, held long enough for your eyes to explore the room, and one movement in the wrong place. People who've seen it never forget it.

The lesson there is restraint. When the camera doesn't move, the audience starts searching the frame, and that searching makes them anxious. A static wide shot can be scarier than any handheld chase, because you're the one doing the looking.

The second technique is how Miike breaks the film's reality. In the last act, as Aoyama starts digging into Asami's past, the film begins to slip between what's real, what's memory and what's a drug-induced nightmare. Scenes from earlier come back with small changes. Characters appear in places they couldn't be. Miike doesn't signal the shifts with dissolves or wobbly effects; he just cuts, so you can never be sure where you stand. You lose your footing at the same time as Aoyama does.

And then there's the finale, which is the part people talk about. I'll just say there are needles, and piano wire, and a sing-song little chant from Asami... "kiri kiri kiri"... that has haunted festival audiences for twenty five years. Eihi Shiina's performance is what makes it unbearable. She plays it sweet, soft-spoken and almost childlike, never like a movie monster. Asami is one of horror's great villains precisely because she never raises her voice.

When it toured festivals, Audition became legendary for walkouts, people leaving screenings in the final act, and also for winning over critics, who saw what was underneath: a film about loneliness, and about men who think they can audition a woman's life, and what happens when the woman turns out to have her own story entirely. That reading is why it's lasted. It's not just shocking; it has something to say, and the horror is aimed squarely at Aoyama's assumptions.

It was a huge influence. Directors like Eli Roth have praised it, and you can see its slow-burn-then-snap structure in a lot of horror since. And the source was Murakami's novel, which gave Miike that blend of quiet everyday life and sudden cruelty.

The practical lesson for you, learning to film: earn your shock. If you want a moment to land, whether it's a scare, a joke or a beautiful reveal in a travel video, make the minutes before it calm and steady. Hold a wide shot a little longer than feels comfortable. Let the viewer settle. Then change one thing. The contrast does the work, not the effect.

And maybe start with something gentler than piano wire. The jacarandas will do nicely. Okay, that was mine, I'm sorry.
