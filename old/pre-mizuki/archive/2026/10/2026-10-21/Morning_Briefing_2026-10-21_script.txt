Now, it's Wednesday, and that means it's time for the story behind this week's retro game: The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past.

It came out in Japan in November nineteen ninety one, in North America in April ninety two, and in Europe that September. It was made by Nintendo's E A D team, directed by Takashi Tezuka, produced by Shigeru Miyamoto, with music by Koji Kondo. And it shipped on an eight megabit cartridge. That's one megabyte. Your phone takes photos bigger than the entire game. At the time, though, that was a big cart. Most Super Nintendo games of the era were half that size.

What's clever is how much they squeezed out of that megabyte. The headline trick is the Dark World. Almost every place in the Light World has a twisted twin in the Dark World, with a similar layout but different, corrupted art, enemies and secrets. Link moves between them with the Magic Mirror. That design is beautiful on its own, because once you learn one map, the other becomes a puzzle: where does this cliff lead on the other side? But it's also efficient. Two worlds sharing one structure is a brilliant way to double your game without doubling your cartridge.

Then there's how the Super Nintendo draws it. The S N E S builds its pictures out of layers: background layers made of tiles, which are little eight by eight pixel squares, and sprites, the moving objects like Link and the enemies, drawn on top. A Link to the Past uses those layers for depth. That's how you get bridges you can walk under, upper floors in dungeons, and the way some rooms let you drop down a level. It's all layers stacked like sheets of glass, each one scrolling and drawing on its own. Let's start with where it came from, because the Witcher didn't begin as a game. It began as short stories and novels by the Polish writer Andrzej Sapkowski, from the mid nineteen eighties onwards. Geralt of Rivia is a witcher: a monster hunter for hire, mutated as a child into something not quite human, with cat-like eyes, slowed emotions, and a very dry sense of humour. The books are full of Slavic folklore, and they're surprisingly political. The monsters are often the least monstrous thing in the room.

CD Projekt, the company, started out in Warsaw in the early nineties, distributing and translating games in Poland. Its development studio, CD Projekt Red, made the first Witcher game in two thousand seven, and The Witcher two in twenty eleven. Both were well liked, but they were cult hits, a bit rough and a bit niche. The third one was the big swing. The plan was simple to say and almost impossible to do: an open world, as large and alive as the biggest Western R P Gs, but with the writing quality of a hand-crafted story game.

The game director was Konrad Tomaszkiewicz, and the team built it on a new version of its own engine, REDengine three. Development took several years, and the team grew to hundreds of people. And the story they settled on was personal. Not saving the world first, but finding a girl. Ciri, Geralt's adopted daughter, his "child surprise" from the books, is being hunted by the Wild Hunt, a host of spectral riders from another world, led by their king, Eredin. 7. GAME TECH

Game tech, and today I'm keeping it hands-on, because this one goes straight into your project. Tilemaps, and how Godot can paint your grass, paths and cliffs for you.

Here's the idea in one sentence. Instead of drawing a whole level as one big picture, you cut your art into small squares called tiles, then build the world on a grid. Remember A Link to the Past from earlier? That's exactly how the Super Nintendo drew it: eight by eight pixel tiles, laid out on layers. Your game will work the same way, just with a lot more memory to play with.

So let's go straight to the practical bit. In Godot four, the node you want is TileMapLayer. If you've seen older tutorials using a single TileMap node with layers inside it, that's the old way. Now each layer is its own node, so you might have one for the ground, one for paths and water, and one for things like fences, stacked on top of each other.

Step one: make a TileSet. In the TileMapLayer's inspector, create a new TileSet, set the tile size, say sixteen by sixteen pixels, and drag your tile sheet in. Godot will offer to slice it into tiles for you. Say yes.

Step two: terrains. This is the magic bit. Without terrains, when you paint a patch of grass next to dirt, you have to pick the right edge tile and the right corner tile by hand, every single time. With terrains, you tell Godot what each tile looks like at its edges, and it picks the right one for you as you paint. That's called autotiling.

You set it up in the TileSet panel. Add a terrain set, and choose its mode. There are three: match corners and sides, match corners, or match sides. Then add a terrain inside it, say, "Grass". Then in the Paint tab, you select each tile and click on the little edge and corner zones, called peering bits, to mark which parts of that tile are grass. It's fiddly the first time, maybe half an hour for a set, but you only do it once.

Which mode? "Match corners and sides" is the most complete, but it needs a big set, up to forty seven tiles for every possible combination. "Match corners" can get away with about sixteen tiles and is much easier to draw. If you're drawing your own pixel art, start with corners. Your future self will thank you.

Step three: paint. In the TileMap panel at the bottom, switch to the Terrains tab, pick Grass, and draw. Godot works out the edges and corners as you go. It's very satisfying.

Now, the two features that make this really useful for a farming game.

First, physics. In your TileSet, add a physics layer, and then draw collision shapes on the tiles that should block the player: water, cliffs, fences. Now the level is solid without you placing a single collision shape by hand.

Second, custom data. In the TileSet, you can add custom data layers, which are just labels on tiles. Add a boolean called "can_till", and tick it on your dirt and grass tiles. Now in code, when the player uses the hoe, you can ask the map: what tile is under me, and can I dig it? You turn the player's position into grid coordinates with local_to_map, look up the tile data with get_cell_tile_data, and read the "can_till" value.

And here's my favourite trick. When the player tills a square, don't set a single tile. Use set_cells_terrain_connect, and give it the cell and your "Tilled soil" terrain. Godot will paint that square as tilled soil and update the neighbours, so a row of tilled squares joins up into one neat, connected patch of farmland, edges and all. That's the Stardew feeling, for about five lines of code.

One warning about tilemaps, though. Once you build a world on a grid, everything in it is on the grid. The trees are on the grid. The houses are on the grid. The villagers' little lives fit neatly into sixteen pixel squares. And sometimes, late at night, if you look closely at a farming game, you'll notice a villager standing perfectly still. Exactly in the centre of a tile. Facing the wall. They know where the grid is. They've always known.

So, to sum up. TileMapLayer nodes, one per layer. A TileSet with a terrain, using the corners mode to keep your drawing manageable. Physics on the tiles that block. A "can_till" custom data label. And set_cells_terrain_connect when the hoe hits the ground. If you get just the terrain part working this weekend, painting a field will go from an afternoon's work to about thirty seconds. Which leaves more time for the important things. Like naming the turnips.

9. DIRECTOR

And now, to finish the deep dives, a single shot. One of the most famous fight scenes ever filmed: the corridor fight in Park Chan-wook's Oldboy.

Oldboy came out in Korea in two thousand and three. It's the middle film in Park's Vengeance Trilogy, between Sympathy for Mr. Vengeance and Lady Vengeance. The story: an ordinary, rather useless businessman called Oh Dae-su is kidnapped and locked in a private prison, a grubby hotel-style room, for fifteen years, with no idea who did it or why. Then one day he's suddenly released, and he has five days to find out. He's played by Choi Min-sik, in one of the great screen performances of the century. The film won the Grand Prix at Cannes in two thousand and four, from a jury led by Quentin Tarantino, who was, let's say, an enthusiastic fan.

The corridor fight happens when Dae-su finds the building where he was held. He walks into a long, narrow corridor, and it fills with thugs. He has a claw hammer. They have everything else. What follows is a fight of about three minutes, filmed in one continuous take, with the camera tracking slowly sideways along the corridor, like you're watching it through a wall.

And if you've played video games, you'll recognise it immediately. It's a side-scrolling beat 'em up. The camera moves left to right, the hero moves left to right, the enemies come at him in waves from both sides. People have compared it to games like Double Dragon and Final Fight for over twenty years, and it's a big part of why gamers love this scene. It's a film shot that thinks like a game screen.

How did they do it? The camera was on a dolly running along a track, parallel to the corridor, with the set built so the camera could look in from the side. The cinematographer was Chung Chung-hoon, who'd go on to work with Park for years, and later in Hollywood. The fight was choreographed and rehearsed in great detail, because in a single take, there's nowhere to hide. If one extra misses a mark at minute two, everyone starts again.

And they did start again. A lot. The shoot is widely reported to have taken around seventeen takes over three days. That's three days of Choi Min-sik swinging, falling, getting up and swinging again. And you can see it in the performance. By the end of the take, he isn't acting tired. He's tired. His punches get sloppier, he leans on the wall, he gasps. It's not a superhero fight. It's a middle-aged man running out of breath while trying not to die. That's what makes it so gripping.

There's one small digital touch. At one point, Dae-su takes a knife in the back and keeps fighting with it sticking out of him. That knife was added digitally, because you can't safely do that for real, take after take. Otherwise, it's people, a corridor, a camera, and a lot of bruises.

And the sound is doing a lot of work too. There's no rapid editing to tell you how hard each hit is, so the thuds, the grunts and the music carry the rhythm. And Park lets the score's strings sweep over the brawl, which makes the brutality feel strangely elegant. It's violence with a dance step.

I do worry about the extras, though. Seventeen takes. Three days. Every take, you come at the man with the hammer. Every take, you lose. And then the assistant director calls "reset", and you walk back to your starting mark, and you pick up your little piece of wood, and you wait. Somewhere in Seoul, there's a man who still flinches at the sound of a dolly track. He does not go to hardware stores.

So what's the lesson for someone learning filmmaking? Two things.

First, the long take is a promise to the audience: nothing here is faked by editing. And that promise changes the scene. But it only works if you design the space for it. Park didn't just point a camera down a hallway; the corridor, the doors, the walls and the track were planned so the action could flow in one direction across the frame.

Second, and this is one you can try on your walk, the sideways tracking shot is incredibly easy to do badly and very satisfying to do well. Next time you're filming, when you get that camera, or even on your phone today, walk parallel to your subject. Keep the same distance. Keep the camera level and at the same height. Let your subject move across the frame, not towards it. Try it alongside a friend walking through Hyde Park, or with the fence railings sliding past in the foreground. The background moving at a different speed from the foreground gives you instant depth, and suddenly a walk through the park looks like a scene from a film.

When the player is one or two pulls away from triggering it, make the next radish's leaves twitch, or give it a tiny shake and a sound, so the player can choose to stop, or push their luck. In Godot, it's simple: when the counter resets, pick a new hidden threshold with randi_range, say between five and twenty five. Each pull, add one. When the count is within two of the threshold, play the twitch animation on the radish under the cursor. And when it's reached, spawn the monster, scaling its health and damage with how many radishes it took to wake it. A small export variable on the monster, set from the count, does the trick. That little twitch is the difference between "that's unfair" and "I knew it, and I did it anyway".
