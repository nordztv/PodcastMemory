[happy] And now, to finish the deep dives, a single shot. One of the most famous fight scenes ever filmed: the corridor fight in Park Chan-wook's Oldboy.

Oldboy came out in Korea in two thousand and three. It's the middle film in Park's Vengeance Trilogy, between Sympathy for Mr. Vengeance and Lady Vengeance. The story: an ordinary, rather useless businessman called Oh Dae-su is kidnapped and locked in a private prison, a grubby hotel-style room, for fifteen years, with no idea who did it or why. Then one day he's suddenly released, and he has five days to find out. He's played by Choi Min-sik, in one of the great screen performances of the century. The film won the Grand Prix at Cannes in two thousand and four, from a jury led by Quentin Tarantino, who was, let's say, an enthusiastic fan.

The corridor fight happens when Dae-su finds the building where he was held. He walks into a long, narrow corridor, and it fills with thugs. He has a claw hammer. They have everything else. What follows is a fight of about three minutes, filmed in one continuous take, with the camera tracking slowly sideways along the corridor, like you're watching it through a wall.

[happy] And if you've played video games, you'll recognise it immediately. It's a side-scrolling beat 'em up. The camera moves left to right, the hero moves left to right, the enemies come at him in waves from both sides. People have compared it to games like Double Dragon and Final Fight for over twenty years, and it's a big part of why gamers love this scene. It's a film shot that thinks like a game screen.

How did they do it? The camera was on a dolly running along a track, parallel to the corridor, with the set built so the camera could look in from the side. The cinematographer was Chung Chung-hoon, who'd go on to work with Park for years, and later in Hollywood. The fight was choreographed and rehearsed in great detail, because in a single take, there's nowhere to hide. If one extra misses a mark at minute two, everyone starts again.

And they did start again. A lot. The shoot is widely reported to have taken around seventeen takes over three days. That's three days of Choi Min-sik swinging, falling, getting up and swinging again. And you can see it in the performance. By the end of the take, he isn't acting tired. He's tired. His punches get sloppier, he leans on the wall, he gasps. It's not a superhero fight. It's a middle-aged man running out of breath while trying not to die. That's what makes it so gripping.

There's one small digital touch. At one point, Dae-su takes a knife in the back and keeps fighting with it sticking out of him. That knife was added digitally, because you can't safely do that for real, take after take. Otherwise, it's people, a corridor, a camera, and a lot of bruises.

And the sound is doing a lot of work too. There's no rapid editing to tell you how hard each hit is, so the thuds, the grunts and the music carry the rhythm. And Park lets the score's strings sweep over the brawl, which makes the brutality feel strangely elegant. It's violence with a dance step.

[dark] I do worry about the extras, though. Seventeen takes. Three days. Every take, you come at the man with the hammer. Every take, you lose. And then the assistant director calls "reset", and you walk back to your starting mark, and you pick up your little piece of wood, and you wait. [beat] Somewhere in Seoul, there's a man who still flinches at the sound of a dolly track. [punch] He does not go to hardware stores.

So what's the lesson for someone learning filmmaking? Two things.

First, the long take is a promise to the audience: nothing here is faked by editing. And that promise changes the scene. But it only works if you design the space for it. Park didn't just point a camera down a hallway; the corridor, the doors, the walls and the track were planned so the action could flow in one direction across the frame.

Second, and this is one you can try on your walk, the sideways tracking shot is incredibly easy to do badly and very satisfying to do well. Next time you're filming, when you get that camera, or even on your phone today, walk parallel to your subject. Keep the same distance. Keep the camera level and at the same height. Let your subject move across the frame, not towards it. Try it alongside a friend walking through Hyde Park, or with the fence railings sliding past in the foreground. The background moving at a different speed from the foreground gives you instant depth, and suddenly a walk through the park looks like a scene from a film.

Just maybe without the hammer.
