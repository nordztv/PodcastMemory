Story and lore for Lanternfall. New week, new theme. And this week's theme is: the soil remembers.

Because this week, we're going to wake up your radish monster. So I wanted to start with the oldest monster vegetable in the world. Older than video games, older than Europe's cathedrals. The mandrake.

The mandrake is a real plant. It grows around the Mediterranean, it has a thick forked root that can look, if you squint, like a little person with arms and legs, and it really is poisonous. It's full of the same kind of chemicals as deadly nightshade. In small amounts, the ancient Greeks and Romans used it as an anaesthetic. Dioscorides and Pliny the Elder both describe giving it in wine to people before surgery. Get the amount wrong, though, and it was the last drink they'd ever have.

So people treated it with enormous respect. And the rituals for pulling it up are where it gets wonderful. Around three hundred years before Christ, Theophrastus wrote that you should draw three circles around the mandrake with a sword, and cut it while facing west. Then, in the first century, the historian Josephus described a root that was deadly to touch, so the trick was to tie a dog to it, and let the dog pull it up. And the poor dog would die in its place.

And then, in the Middle Ages, the story grew a voice. The mandrake... screamed. And anyone who heard the scream... would never hear anything again.

German folklore added one more layer. The Galgenmannlein, the little gallows man: a mandrake was said to grow beneath the gallows, from what dripped from the hanged. Carved mandrake dolls were sold as lucky charms for centuries, and plenty were fakes carved from cheaper roots. Even an emperor, Rudolf the Second, kept a pair. And of course, Harry Potter: Professor Sprout's class repotting baby mandrakes in earmuffs, because the cry of a grown one is fatal.

So, think about what the mandrake gives a game designer. A plant that's useful and dangerous at the same time. A rule for pulling it safely. A sound that warns you. And a dark origin: it grows from something it shouldn't.

Quick reminder of where we are. On Friday's build-along, the idea was a Game State autoload that counts radish pulls, with a hidden random number between eight and fifteen. Every pull calls a function called register pull, and when the count reaches that hidden number, it prints "Something stirs in the soil", and starts again. If you've built that, great. If you were too busy being in Brisbane, no problem, tonight's workshop gives you everything you need.

Tonight's lesson is a big one in Godot, and you'll use it everywhere: making things appear while the game is running. Monsters, dropped items, dust puffs, falling star tears. They're all the same trick. It's called instancing a scene.

Here's the everyday version. Think of a scene file as a cookie cutter. Your radish monster scene, saved as radish monster dot T S C N, isn't a monster. It's the cutter. Each time you press it into the dough, you get a new cookie: a new, separate monster, with its own health and its own position. Press it five times, five monsters.

In Godot, it takes three steps. First, you load the cutter. At the top of a script, you write a constant called radish monster, in capitals, the way constants usually are, equals preload, and the path to the scene in quotes. Preload means Godot reads the file once, when the script loads, so there's no hiccup later.

Second, you press the cutter. That's one line: a variable called monster equals radish monster dot instantiate, with brackets. Now you've got a brand new monster. But it's floating in nowhere. It's not in the game yet.

Third, you put it in the world, with add child. And then you set where it is, with its global position. The order matters a little here. The safe habit is: add it first, then set global position, because global position means "position in the world", and it only really knows where the world is once it's in it.

Now, the interesting design question: who does the spawning? The radish? Game State? The farm?

Here's what I'd do. Game State keeps doing the counting, because that's its job: it's the farm's memory. But it shouldn't be making monsters. Instead, when the count is reached, it sends a signal. Something like radish awakened, and it passes along a position, the spot where the last radish was pulled. Then your Farm scene listens for that signal, and it does the spawning, because the Farm is the one that owns the world.

It's like a fire alarm. Game State is the smoke detector. It doesn't put out fires. It just shouts. The Farm hears it and decides what to do. And later, other things can listen to the same signal: a sound, a screen shake, a villager who says "I told you so".

In code, in Game State, you'd write: signal radish awakened, and in brackets, where, colon, Vector two. When the counter hits the threshold, you call radish awakened dot emit, and pass in the position. In the Farm's ready function, you connect it: Game State dot radish awakened dot connect, and the name of a function on the farm. And that function is where you instantiate, add child, and set the position.

One more thing to know. Sometimes Godot gets upset if you add things to the world while it's in the middle of working out physics, for example inside an area's body entered signal. You'll see an error about the tree being locked, or flushing queries. The fix is a little word: call deferred. Instead of add child, with the monster in brackets, you write add child dot call deferred, with the monster in brackets. It means: "do this at the end of the frame, when you're not busy". Tonight we're spawning from a key press, so you probably won't need it. But when you see that error, that's the fix.

Now, the bit that makes it feel good: the burst. If the monster just appears, it feels like a glitch. It needs a moment of warning, and then a pop. Remember the lore segment: the mandrake had its scream. Your radish needs its tell.

So, two tweens. First, the warning: about half a second where the pulled spot shakes. A tween that nudges the sprite left and right a couple of pixels, a few times. Then, the burst: the monster starts at a scale of zero, and a tween grows it to full size in about a fifth of a second, with the transition set to Back. Back is a curve that overshoots a little and settles, so the monster seems to spring out, then land. It's the same trick cartoon animators call overshoot.

And one cheap trick that sells it: a frame or two of anticipation. Before it bursts, squash the soil down, so it looks like something underneath is pushing. Animators have done that for a hundred years. Your radish just has better reasons.

Quick tips list for tonight. Keep the monster as its own scene, never build it inside the farm. Name your signal for what happened, radish awakened, not for what should happen next, spawn monster. And print something when the signal fires, so you know the alarm works before you worry about the fire.

Effects! And since this is the week things burst out of the ground, there was only one film I could pick. Tremors.

Nineteen ninety. Kevin Bacon and Fred Ward as two handymen in a tiny desert town called Perfection, Nevada, and underneath them, giant worms called Graboids that hunt by sound. It was directed by Ron Underwood, his first feature, and written by S. S. Wilson and Brent Maddock. The working title was Land Sharks. Which is honestly the most accurate working title in history. It wasn't a big hit at first. It opened at number five, and made about sixteen point seven million dollars in the US. But it found its audience on video, and now it's one of the most loved creature features ever made. And the reason it still looks so good is that almost everything you see was physically there.

Let's go through how they built it, piece by piece.

Step one: the town. They shot for fifty four days around Lone Pine and Olancha in California, near the Alabama Hills, at the foot of the Sierra Nevada. Perfection was built from scratch near Olancha. That real desert light is a big part of why it feels so solid.

Step two: the monsters. The creatures were built by Amalgamated Dynamics, the company started by Alec Gillis and Tom Woodruff Junior, who'd come from Stan Winston's shop. They built four full-size Graboid heads, each about eight feet across, and one thirty-foot-plus dead Graboid for the scene where they dig one up. The skin was foam latex, baked in a custom eight-foot oven, over fibreglass heads and jaws.

Step three: making them move. In the pit, a full-size head was worked like a giant rod puppet. Puppeteers were underground beneath it, three on the head, three on the jaw cables, in the dark, wearing respirators, working from radio cues. So while Kevin Bacon was screaming on top, six people were in a hole underneath, quietly breathing through masks. Which is also how a lot of film crews feel. Sorry, that's an industry joke.

Step four: the burst. This is my favourite. When a Graboid explodes up out of the ground, that's a ten-foot pneumatic elevator, five feet wide, sitting in an eleven-foot-deep hole. On top was a foam lid painted like dirt and sprinkled with sand and vermiculite. Hit the air, and the creature, often with Tom Woodruff inside, punched straight up through the fake ground. Every reset took about forty five minutes, so every burst had to count. A second elevator handled the shots where people get dragged under.

Step five: the tongues. Inside the Graboid's mouth are three snake-like tentacles with their own little mouths. Three cable-operated ten-foot tongues were built on a spine of braided hydraulic hose, plus a hand puppet for close-ups, and nine simple stunt tentacles for anything rough. The design borrowed from snakes, elephant trunks and octopus.

Step six: the miniatures. For wide shots, the Skotak brothers' team used five quarter-scale animatronic Graboids, each needing six to eight operators, plus smaller hand puppets. They filmed them at high frame rates, and the falling Graboid at seventy two frames a second, which slows the motion down on screen so the creature feels huge and heavy. Small things fall fast. Big things fall slow. Your brain knows the difference.

And step seven, the simplest trick of all: the moving humps under the ground, as the Graboids tunnel towards people? Boat buoys, chained to a truck, dragged through buried trenches. That's it. A truck, a chain and some buoys. The slime was K Y jelly, and the blood was orange methocel, a thickener.

So, the practical lesson. Tremors is mostly a film about something you can't see. The Graboids are underground for most of it. And the scares come from the build-up: the humps, the shaking, the sound, then the burst. The reveal is short, and it's earned. Ron Underwood spent his effects budget on a few big moments and let your imagination do the rest.

Director

The director's chair. And today it's Michael Mann, and one of the most famous action scenes ever filmed: the downtown shootout in Heat.

Heat came out in December nineteen ninety five. Al Pacino as a detective, Robert De Niro as a professional thief, and the whole film builds to a bank robbery in downtown Los Angeles that goes wrong, and turns into a gunfight in the middle of the street, in broad daylight, between towers of glass and concrete.

I picked it for two reasons. You told me the one film your server would keep forever is the original John Wick. And you told me, back when you watched Aliens, that you loved "the sound of the guns". Well, if you love how gunfights sound and feel in modern action films, a lot of that road leads back to this scene.

Let's start with the place. Mann shot Heat entirely on real locations. Eighty five of them, all in and around LA, and no soundstages at all, across a hundred and seven days. The bank is a real building, the Citigroup Center, and the battle was filmed along Fifth Street downtown. The catch? They could only have those streets on weekends. So the shootout was filmed over several weeks of Saturdays and Sundays.

Then the rehearsal. A full-scale mock-up of that block of Fifth Street was built on the LA County Sheriff's shooting ranges, so the actors and camera crews could rehearse every move. And the actors trained with firearms for about three months beforehand, with former British SAS soldiers, including live-fire sessions, so that by the time they had blanks on set, they moved like a team. Watch how Val Kilmer changes his magazine, or how they cover each other as they retreat. It still gets called one of the most realistic gunfights ever put on film.

Now, the sound. This is the bit I love. Normally, film gunshots are added later, in post-production. Blanks on set sound like cap guns, so the sound team replaces them. But when Mann heard the recordings from the location, he kept them. The real sound of those blanks, bouncing off all those towers, was so frightening that it became the soundtrack of the scene. Each take used eight hundred to a thousand rounds. That's why it sounds different from almost any other movie gunfight: it's not a gun sound effect, it's a street full of echoes.

And the camera. It's handheld, and it feels chaotic, like news footage. But it's not chaotic at all. It was carefully choreographed, with several cameras running at once. The cinematographer, Dante Spinotti, made that possible by lighting whole areas instead of individual shots, so a camera could swing anywhere and still have a good image. He favoured wide shots and long takes, so you always know where everyone is. That's the secret of a good action scene: geography. You always know who's behind which car.

Which is also why so many shootouts in later films feel muddled. Lots of cuts, no idea where anyone is, and somehow everyone's run out of bullets at the same time. Heat would never.

And you can see the influence everywhere, from crime films to games. The John Wick films have that same love of clear geography, long takes and believable reloading. And games like Grand Theft Auto, and plenty of others, have borrowed Heat's bank job so many times it's practically a genre of its own.

Okay, the practical lesson for your own filming. Two things.

One: the real sound of a place is often better than anything you can add. When you're filming with the X-M5 someday, on a trip, record the location: the wind, the traffic, the echoes off buildings. Even thirty seconds of room tone with nobody talking. In Final Cut, laying that under a scene makes it feel real in a way music alone never does. Mann kept his echoes. You keep yours.

Two: light the space, not the shot. On a small scale, that means if you're filming somewhere with good light, a window, a lamp, a street at golden hour, set your subject up so the camera can move around and still look good. Then you can film loose and handheld, and you'll never be stuck. It's exactly what Spinotti did, on a slightly bigger budget.

And one little secret about Heat... Pacino and De Niro had famously never really shared the screen before. Their scene together, in the diner, is quiet... two men talking over coffee. The most famous gunfight of the nineties... and people still talk most about the coffee.
