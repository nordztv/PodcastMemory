So, this morning I promised you a horror trick. And since it's Friday the thirteenth, and you're walking past a lot of big dark windows right now... ...let's do Ringu.

Ringu came out in Japan on the thirty first of January, nineteen ninety eight, directed by Hideo Nakata, released by Toho. The story, if you've somehow escaped it: a cursed videotape, and anyone who watches it gets a phone call, and dies seven days later. It was made for about one and a half million U S dollars, and it became the highest-grossing horror film in Japan at the time, about one point seven billion yen. Then came the American remake, and suddenly every country in the world was scared of long black hair.

And almost everything scary about it is cheap. That's what I love. Let me go through it, piece by piece.

Step one: the tape itself. Inside the film, you see the whole cursed video before you even know the rules. A woman brushing her hair in a mirror. Japanese characters twitching across the screen like ants. A man with a cloth over his head, pointing at the ground. An eye. And a well. It feels like found footage, like something recorded on a broken V C R. But it wasn't. Those shots were filmed properly, on thirty five millimetre film, and then a lab's computer added the grain and the damage afterwards. So it's a beautiful image, made ugly on purpose. Very expensive ugly. Like distressed jeans. Okay, that's my fashion opinion.

Step two: the sound. Nakata worked closely with the composer, Kenji Kawai, and the sound team, layering things. They used about fifty tracks of background sound and about fifty tracks of effects. And for the phone, they mixed four different telephone rings together, so it doesn't sound quite like any phone you've ever owned. That's why it gets under your skin. Your ears know something is wrong, but they can't say what.

Step three: what you don't see. Nakata loved an old American film, The Haunting, from nineteen sixty three, which scares you without ever showing the ghost, and he watched it at least five times. He also took ideas from kabuki and the old Yotsuya ghost story. So in Ringu, the deaths happen off screen. The producers worried that wouldn't work. It worked. Ask four twelve year old girls in Tennoji.

And step four. The moment. Sadako, climbing out of the well on the television, then walking towards the screen, and then... ...out of it, into the room.

Sadako was played by Rie Inō, an actress from a kabuki theatre troupe, which matters, because kabuki is all about controlling every part of how your body moves. Here's the trick. They filmed her walking backwards, away from the camera, in a jerky, broken way. Then they ran the film in reverse. So when you watch it, she's walking towards you, but every movement is just... wrong. Her knees bend at the wrong moment. Her weight goes the wrong way. Your brain has watched people walk your whole life, and it knows that's not how people walk. That's the fear. Not blood. Not a jump. Just a walk that's a little bit backwards.

And the hair over her face is real, by the way. No wig, no extensions, it was Rie Inō's own long hair. And the one eye you see, in that close-up through the hair? It's not hers. It was a man from the crew. His eye was put in, in post. So somewhere in Japan there's a man whose eye has frightened millions of people, and he probably just goes to the supermarket like it's nothing. I'd tell everyone. I'd put it on my business card.

I saw it at a sleepover when I was twelve, with a cushion up to my nose. I didn't scream once. I still mention that more than I need to. And even now, my television in Pyrmont faces away from the bed. Not because I'm scared. Because I have a plan.

The lesson for your Z R, and your game: you don't need a big budget to make something feel wrong. Shoot it backwards. Shoot it from too low. Hold the shot one second longer than is comfortable. The scary part comes slowly.

DIRECTOR

And while we're being spooky and clever about cameras, let's do the most famous camera move in horror. A little boy on a tricycle.

The Shining, nineteen eighty, Stanley Kubrick. Jack Nicholson, Shelley Duvall, and Danny Lloyd as their son Danny, in the enormous empty Overlook Hotel for the winter. The outside of the hotel is a real lodge on a mountain in Oregon, but almost everything inside was built as sets at Elstree Studios, near London. Corridors, the huge lounge, the kitchen, even the hedge maze.

And the reason those corridors feel so alive is a machine that had only just been invented. The Steadicam. A man called Garrett Brown had built it a few years earlier: a vest, a spring-loaded arm, and a long post with the camera at one end and weights at the other. The operator walks, runs, climbs stairs, and the camera floats. Before that, if you wanted a smooth moving shot, you laid down tracks and pushed the camera on a dolly. Kubrick wanted the camera to follow people anywhere a person could go. So he hired the inventor himself to operate it.

And here's my favourite part. Danny rides his Big Wheel tricycle through the hotel corridors, round and round, and the camera follows right behind him, very low, about the height of his head. The normal Steadicam couldn't go that low, so Brown adapted the rig into what's now called low mode, with the camera hanging close to the floor, and followed the trike at speed, for take after take. Imagine running bent over behind a little boy on a tricycle for weeks, carrying a camera on a pole. That's not a camera job. That's cardio. Sorry. Okay. Back to film school.

Listen to that scene next time, too. The wheels are loud on the wooden floor, then go silent on the rugs, then loud again. Rumble, quiet, rumble, quiet. Nothing has happened, but you're already holding your breath, because the sound keeps going away and coming back. And then he turns a corner, and there are two girls at the end of the hallway. And you know.

Kubrick also loved a wide lens and a very symmetrical frame. Long corridors, with the vanishing point dead in the middle, and the person small in the centre. A symmetrical frame feels calm, and orderly, and safe. And that's exactly what makes it creepy. Too orderly. Too still. Like the hotel is holding its breath with you. Nothing in nature is symmetrical. Except Sailor Moon's hair, and the Overlook Hotel. And look where that got them.

Okay, the lesson for your Z R, and it's a good one for travel video too.

One: get low and follow. If you've got a gimbal, most of them have an underslung or flip mode. Hold the camera at knee height and follow a friend, a dog, a suitcase rolling down a hotel hallway. Same distance the whole way, same speed. It feels like you're being taken somewhere.

Two: wide and centred. Put your widest lens on, stand in the middle of a corridor, a train platform, a long avenue of trees in the Botanic Garden, and put the vanishing point dead centre. Then wait for one person to walk into it.

Three: record the sound of the floor. Footsteps on wood, then on grass, then on stone. You don't need a ghost. You just need the sound to stop for a second.
