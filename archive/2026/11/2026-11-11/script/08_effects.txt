[excited] Effects! And today it's the monster from The Host. Bong Joon-ho's creature, crawling out of Seoul's Han River in broad daylight.

[curious] Most monster films hide the monster. Darkness, rain, quick cuts, a glimpse of a tail. Bong did the opposite. In the first twenty minutes, on a sunny afternoon, in a riverside park full of people, the creature just... runs up the bank. In one long shot. You can see everything. [impressed] And on a budget that Hollywood would spend on catering. So, how do you build that, piece by piece?

[calm] Step one: design. Bong's idea came partly from a real newspaper story, about a deformed fish with an S-shaped spine, caught in the Han. The creature designer, Jang Hee-chul, started work when the script was finished at the end of twenty oh three, and made more than two thousand drawings. It took about a year and four months to get the design approved. [warm] He said it felt like being a parent, giving birth to a child.

[thoughtful] And the design was the key decision. Bong didn't want a giant, majestic monster. He wanted something the size of a truck, clumsy and ugly, a mutant that might really have grown in a polluted river. So: a fish-like body, a tail that can grab things, and a mouth that splits open in petals.

[calm] Step two: who builds it? The film's whole budget was around eleven million US dollars, and Bong said about four and a half million of that went on the effects. The famous big companies were too expensive. So Jang sculpted a model of the creature at Weta Workshop in New Zealand. It was scanned in Los Angeles. And the computer animation went to a smaller company in San Francisco, The Orphanage, with Kevin Rafferty supervising.

[happy] And, there's an Australian bit! For about a dozen close-ups, including the gross scenes where the creature coughs things up, John Cox's Creature Workshop in Queensland built a full size animatronic head, with a jaw and tongue that really moved. The Orphanage said it ended up saving them a lot of money compared with doing every shot in C G.

[calm] Step three: animation, and this is my favourite part. The flippers were animated by hand, key frame by key frame, with a layer of muscle jiggle added on top. And the animators studied real things. Sea lions and elephant seals for how it runs. Crocodiles for how it swims. A snake climbing a tree for the tail. And a man called Johnny Eck, from the nineteen thirty two film Freaks, who walked on his hands, for how the creature carries its weight on its front limbs. [surprised] For the scene where it's burned, they set a real fish on fire, to see what the skin does.

[delighted] And Bong had a brilliant note. He told the animators to treat the creature like an actor still getting its legs. So early in the film, it's clumsy. It slips on the bank. It tumbles. It looks almost embarrassing. And by the end, it's moving better. It's learning. [sweet, innocent] [speaking slowly] It's getting... [pause] better at catching people. [little giggle] [beat] [sweet, innocent, slow] Practice makes perfect.

[calm] Step four: the shoot. About eighty per cent of the film was shot on location along the Han, under the real bridges, and in real sewers by the river, where the cast and crew needed tetanus shots. And there was no blue screen, no green screen, and no motion control rig at all. Bong and his cinematographer, Kim Hyung-koo, moved the camera freely, as if the monster was really there.

[thoughtful] So how do you put a computer creature into a free, handheld shot? On every set-up, the crew photographed chrome balls and grey charts, to {record|rih-kord} exactly how the light fell, and three hundred and sixty degree panoramas of the location, so the creature would reflect the real river and the real sky. Actors were pulled on harnesses and wires when the tail grabs them, and the wires were painted out, or hidden behind the creature. And when the creature lands on a truck, the crew dropped real heavy weights onto it from a crane, so the metal really buckled.

[impressed] And that daylight attack? It's one long take of about thirty seconds, about eleven hundred frames. It was the first shot they started, and the last one they finished. Their trick was to sort every shot by cost and difficulty, and spend the money on fewer, longer, harder shots, instead of lots of quick cheap ones. Bong even flew to San Francisco for a couple of weeks, and the teams reviewed shots across a sixteen hour time difference.

[warm] The practical lesson? Light is the whole illusion. The creature works because it sits in real sunlight, with real reflections, interacting with real things: a truck that dents, people who are really pulled off their feet. [curious] And there's one for your Z R, too. If you ever add anything to a shot afterwards, a graphic, a title in the scenery, a bit of effects work, take ten seconds on location to film the scene empty, and hold up something grey or shiny where the effect will go. That's the same trick, on your scale.
