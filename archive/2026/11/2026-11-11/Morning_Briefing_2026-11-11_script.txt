So, how was it made? In Japan it's called Shin Sōseiki Ragnacënty, and in Europe it was called Soleil. It came out in Japan in June nineteen ninety four, published by Sega, and reached Europe and America in early nineteen ninety five, where Atlus put out only a very small run. That's why an original American cartridge can cost hundreds of dollars today.

It was made by a studio called Nextech, which grew out of Wolf Team, the people behind games like El Viento. And here's the fun bit: it didn't start as Crusader of Centy at all. It started as a Shining series action R P G, working title Shining Rogue, before it was restarted as its own game. The writer and planner was Yayoi Onda, the lead programmer was Yukihiko Tani, and the music is by Motokazu Shinoda.

And Tani was very honest about what he was up against. He told Time Extension: "I was desperate to create something that could not be defeated or beaten by The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past." No pressure, then.

So how do you beat Zelda on weaker hardware? The Mega Drive has a Motorola sixty eight thousand processor at about seven and a half megahertz, a separate Z eighty chip mostly handling the sound, sixty four kilobytes of main memory, and only around sixty colours on screen at once, out of a palette of five hundred and twelve. It can only draw so many sprites on each line of the screen, and it has no hardware to rotate or scale them.

Tani's answer was the hero himself. Instead of one sprite in four directions, like Link, Corona is built from separate parts: his upper body, his lower body and his sword swing are all animated separately. That gives him smooth movement and attacks in eight directions, which feels almost analogue. And there's a lovely detail: the sprite is never simply mirrored, so his sword and shield always stay in the correct hands. Most games of the time just flipped the picture and hoped you wouldn't notice. Add smooth eight-way scrolling, and the music from the Mega Drive's Yamaha F M chip, and you get that bright, bouncy feel you played through.

And then there are the animals. Sixteen companions, three of them only for rent, and you carry two at a time, so it's really a game about combinations. Inferno the lion sets your thrown sword on fire. Chilly the penguin freezes enemies. Flash the cheetah doubles your speed. Monarchy the butterfly lets you steer your sword in mid-air. Rio the armadillo becomes a plank to cross gaps. And Moa does nothing at all on its own, but makes the others stronger. Every party needs one of those.

And the story is much stranger than Zelda's. On his fourteenth birthday, Corona inherits his father's sword and goes out to fight monsters, like every boy in his town. But for the first half of the game, he can't understand people at all, only animals and plants. And later, travelling through time, he learns where the monsters really came from, and what they really wanted. Time Extension calls it darker and more mature than Zelda, and one retrospective compares it to Terranigma.

Back then, it scored very well in Britain, ninety two per cent in both Mega and Mean Machines Sega, with Electronic Gaming Monthly and Famitsu a bit cooler. It never sold like Zelda, and it's become one of those overlooked gems people keep rediscovering. GAME TECH

Game tech! And today, something that makes pixel art look expensive, for almost no effort. Shaders.

You've seen it a hundred times without noticing. An enemy flashes white for a split second when you hit it. Grass sways in the wind. Water ripples. A defeated monster dissolves into sparkles. In most modern pixel art games, none of that is extra animation frames. It's a shader. A tiny program, a few lines long, running on the graphics card.

Let's start with the practical bit, in Godot, and then I'll explain what's actually going on.

The hit flash. It's the most useful shader in any action game, so it's the perfect first one. Select your enemy's Sprite two D. In the Inspector, find Material, under Canvas Item, and choose New Shader Material. Click it, then give it a New Shader, and pick the canvas item type, which is the one for anything 2D.

Then the shader itself is four lines. First line: shader underscore type canvas underscore item. Then a setting you can control from your code, called a uniform: uniform float flash, from zero to one. Then the fragment function, which runs once for every pixel. Inside it, you read the pixel's colour from the texture, and you mix it towards pure white by the amount in flash, keeping its transparency the same. That's it. When flash is zero, the sprite looks normal. When flash is one, it's a solid white silhouette.

And to make it flash, you use a tween. When the enemy gets hit, set the shader parameter flash to one, then tween it back down to zero over about a tenth of a second. Instant crunch! Your hit suddenly feels like it landed. And yes, if a certain vegetable were to get hit, this is exactly how it would blink. That's my one radish joke today. Spent.

One gotcha. A material is shared by default. So if ten goblins use the same material and you flash one, all ten flash. Which is funny once. Fix it by ticking Local to Scene on the material, so every enemy gets its own copy.

Now, what's actually happening? Here's my favourite way to picture it. Imagine your screen is a giant wall mosaic, with hundreds of thousands of tiles. Your computer's main processor is one very clever painter, who'd have to repaint every tile, one after the other. A graphics card is different. It's like having thousands of not-very-clever painters, one standing in front of every tile, all holding the same recipe card. Someone shouts "go", and they all follow the recipe at once, for their own tile only. That's why shaders are so fast. The recipe is the shader. Each painter is running the fragment function for one pixel. And none of them can see what their neighbours are doing... unless the recipe tells them to peek.

Which is how you make an outline. In the fragment function, you look at the four pixels next to this one, up, down, left and right. Godot gives you the size of one pixel in the texture, as texture pixel size, so you just nudge the position by that much. If this pixel is see-through, but one of its neighbours isn't, you're on the edge, so paint it the outline colour. That's how games draw that bright outline around the thing you can pick up, or the enemy you're targeting. Turn the outline on and off with another uniform, and you've got a "this is interactive" highlight for your whole game.

Two more you'll want for a farming game. Swaying grass and crops: in the vertex function, which runs for the corners of the sprite rather than every pixel, you move the top corners left and right with a sine wave over time. Leave the bottom corners where they are, so the plant stays rooted, and the top bends like it's in a breeze. Give each plant a slightly different starting point, so the whole field doesn't sway in perfect unison like a dance troupe. Unless you want the crops to look like they're all listening to the same music. That only you can't hear. Which is a good idea for later, actually.

And water: in the fragment function, nudge the position you read from sideways, by a tiny sine wave based on the time and the height on screen. The texture wobbles, a pixel or two, and suddenly a flat blue tile is a pond.

A word about pixel art specifically. A shader can easily break the pixel look, by blurring, or by moving things half a pixel. So keep your project's texture filter on Nearest, and when you wobble or move things, think in whole texture pixels: multiply your movement by texture pixel size, and keep the amounts small. One pixel of sway reads as wind. Five pixels reads as an earthquake.

Here's your short tip list.

One. Start with the hit flash. It's four lines, and it makes combat feel twice as good.

Two. Tick Local to Scene on any material you'll change per enemy.

Three. Use uniforms for anything you want to animate, and drive them with tweens from your code.

Four. Godot's visual shader editor lets you build all of this by connecting boxes, if you'd rather not type code. Same result.

And five. Don't put a shader on everything on day one. Pick one: the hit flash. Get it working, feel the difference, and then add sway to the crops. A little shimmer goes a long way.

EFFECTS

Effects! And today it's the monster from The Host. Bong Joon-ho's creature, crawling out of Seoul's Han River in broad daylight.

Most monster films hide the monster. Darkness, rain, quick cuts, a glimpse of a tail. Bong did the opposite. In the first twenty minutes, on a sunny afternoon, in a riverside park full of people, the creature just... runs up the bank. In one long shot. You can see everything. And on a budget that Hollywood would spend on catering. So, how do you build that, piece by piece?

Step one: design. Bong's idea came partly from a real newspaper story, about a deformed fish with an S-shaped spine, caught in the Han. The creature designer, Jang Hee-chul, started work when the script was finished at the end of twenty oh three, and made more than two thousand drawings. It took about a year and four months to get the design approved. He said it felt like being a parent, giving birth to a child.

And the design was the key decision. Bong didn't want a giant, majestic monster. He wanted something the size of a truck, clumsy and ugly, a mutant that might really have grown in a polluted river. So: a fish-like body, a tail that can grab things, and a mouth that splits open in petals.

Step two: who builds it? The film's whole budget was around eleven million US dollars, and Bong said about four and a half million of that went on the effects. The famous big companies were too expensive. So Jang sculpted a model of the creature at Weta Workshop in New Zealand. It was scanned in Los Angeles. And the computer animation went to a smaller company in San Francisco, The Orphanage, with Kevin Rafferty supervising.

And, there's an Australian bit! For about a dozen close-ups, including the gross scenes where the creature coughs things up, John Cox's Creature Workshop in Queensland built a full size animatronic head, with a jaw and tongue that really moved. The Orphanage said it ended up saving them a lot of money compared with doing every shot in C G.

Step three: animation, and this is my favourite part. The flippers were animated by hand, key frame by key frame, with a layer of muscle jiggle added on top. And the animators studied real things. Sea lions and elephant seals for how it runs. Crocodiles for how it swims. A snake climbing a tree for the tail. And a man called Johnny Eck, from the nineteen thirty two film Freaks, who walked on his hands, for how the creature carries its weight on its front limbs. For the scene where it's burned, they set a real fish on fire, to see what the skin does.

And Bong had a brilliant note. He told the animators to treat the creature like an actor still getting its legs. So early in the film, it's clumsy. It slips on the bank. It tumbles. It looks almost embarrassing. And by the end, it's moving better. It's learning. It's getting... better at catching people. Practice makes perfect.

Step four: the shoot. About eighty per cent of the film was shot on location along the Han, under the real bridges, and in real sewers by the river, where the cast and crew needed tetanus shots. And there was no blue screen, no green screen, and no motion control rig at all. Bong and his cinematographer, Kim Hyung-koo, moved the camera freely, as if the monster was really there.

So how do you put a computer creature into a free, handheld shot? On every set-up, the crew photographed chrome balls and grey charts, to record exactly how the light fell, and three hundred and sixty degree panoramas of the location, so the creature would reflect the real river and the real sky. Actors were pulled on harnesses and wires when the tail grabs them, and the wires were painted out, or hidden behind the creature. And when the creature lands on a truck, the crew dropped real heavy weights onto it from a crane, so the metal really buckled.

And that daylight attack? It's one long take of about thirty seconds, about eleven hundred frames. It was the first shot they started, and the last one they finished. Their trick was to sort every shot by cost and difficulty, and spend the money on fewer, longer, harder shots, instead of lots of quick cheap ones. Bong even flew to San Francisco for a couple of weeks, and the teams reviewed shots across a sixteen hour time difference.

The practical lesson? Light is the whole illusion. The creature works because it sits in real sunlight, with real reflections, interacting with real things: a truck that dents, people who are really pulled off their feet. And there's one for your Z R, too. If you ever add anything to a shot afterwards, a graphic, a title in the scenery, a bit of effects work, take ten seconds on location to film the scene empty, and hold up something grey or shiny where the effect will go. That's the same trick, on your scale.

DIRECTOR

And our director today is Wong Kar-wai, with his cinematographer, Christopher Doyle. The film: Chungking Express, from nineteen ninety four. A film made so fast, and so loose, that it really shouldn't work. And it's one of the most loved films ever to come out of Hong Kong.

Here's the setup. In nineteen ninety four, Wong was stuck in the long, painful editing of a big martial arts epic, Ashes of Time. So, during a break of a couple of months, he decided to make a small film, quickly, to clear his head. The British Film Institute says it was shot in about twenty three days. He started shooting without a finished script. He wrote the second story in a single day, in a hotel coffee shop on Nathan Road in Kowloon.

It's two stories, about two lovesick policemen. In the first, Takeshi Kaneshiro plays a young cop, counting down the days since his girlfriend left, eating tins of pineapple that expire on the first of May. He crosses paths with a mysterious drug smuggler, played by Brigitte Lin, in a blonde wig, a raincoat and sunglasses. In the second, Tony Leung plays another cop, who keeps buying a chef's salad at a snack bar, where Faye Wong works, and she starts sneaking into his flat while he's out, and quietly rearranging his life.

Now, the camera. The first story was mostly shot by Andrew Lau, and the second by Christopher Doyle, who also reshot some of the earlier material to make it all hang together. And Doyle described the approach simply: "We shot it fast... ad hoc and improvised in many situations... We had to get what we could and not what we wanted sometimes."

So, the techniques. First: handheld, almost all the time, and mostly with available light. The neon signs, the shop lights, whatever was there. That's partly a style, and partly because they were shooting in real places without permission. The first story's crowded scenes are in Chungking Mansions, a huge, chaotic building of guesthouses and shops. It was considered a fire hazard, so they had no permits, and Wong had an assistant director live there beforehand, to work out the exits. Some of the people you see in the background really lived there.

Second, the famous look. When Brigitte Lin runs through the crowd, she's sharp, but everything around her smears into streaks of colour, stuttering, like a memory. How? Two things together. They shot at a low frame rate with a slow shutter, so every frame has a lot of motion blur. Then, in the lab, they step printed it: each frame printed several times over, to bring it back up to normal speed. So the film plays at the right speed, but it moves in little jumps, and every jump is smeared. It's a whole emotional state, made in a film lab.

Third, the lenses. Wong and Doyle are famous for going very wide and very close to their actors, so faces fill the frame and the city bends around them. But TCM reports that Doyle also used long lenses, up to two hundred millimetres, to squash the frame, shooting from far away through crowds and clutter, so the characters feel watched, and alone among thousands of people. Wide and close for intimacy. Long and far for loneliness. Same film, two opposite tools.

And the locations are half the charm. The snack bar was a real place in Lan Kwai Fong, called Midnight Express. And Tony Leung's flat, the one Faye Wong breaks into? It was Christopher Doyle's own flat, right by the Mid-Levels escalator, the longest outdoor covered escalator system in the world. Doyle was still living there. He said he had to sleep on the floor. And there's a scene where they flooded the place, and the neighbours downstairs threatened to sue. His verdict: "Art has its consequences!"

Faye Wong was a pop star making her first big-screen role. They played the music live on set, so she could react to it, and that's why she's always moving, dancing behind the counter, to California Dreamin' by The Mamas and the Papas, played over and over and over. Again and again. Until you either love that song forever... or you never want to hear it again. There is no middle ground. And at the end, her own Cantonese cover of The Cranberries' Dreams plays over the credits.

Quentin Tarantino loved it so much, his own label released it in America. And Wong called it his love letter to Hong Kong.

So, the practical lessons for someone learning to shoot. One. Limitations become style. They had no time, no permits and no lights, and that's exactly what the film looks like, in the best way. Two. Choose your lens for feeling, not convenience. Wide and close when you want the viewer inside someone's head, long and far when you want them lost in a crowd. And three, for your Z R.
