GAME TECH

Game tech! And today it's something your game will need sooner than you think: a save system. Because one day, the radish monster is going to catch you, and you're going to want to load. Okay, okay. Practical stuff first, like always.

On Friday we built an inventory out of Resources: item data, an inventory, and recipes. A save system is the natural next step, because the most important thing you'll ever save is what's in that inventory. Here's the smallest version you can build in an evening, in four steps.

Step one: decide what to save, and keep it small. A save file should hold the things that change, not the things that don't. Your player's position and health. The day number and the time. The inventory, as a list of item ids and amounts. Which crops are planted, on which tiles, and how grown they are. And a handful of flags, like whether the nurikabe has had its radish cake. What you don't save is anything that's already in your game files: the art, the item descriptions, the recipe lists. You save "three radishes", not the whole radish.

Step two: give every saveable thing one job. In Godot, a lovely pattern is to put every node that needs saving into a group, say, "persist". Each of those nodes gets two little functions: one that returns its data as a Dictionary, and one that takes a Dictionary and puts the data back. So the crop tile says "here's my type, my growth stage and my water level", and the player says "here's where I am and how hurt I am". Your save function doesn't need to know anything about crops or players. It just asks everyone in the group for their dictionary.

Step three: write it to the disk. Gather all those dictionaries into one big one, turn it into text with Godot's JSON class, and write it with FileAccess to a file in the user folder: user colon slash slash, save one dot json. The user folder is Godot's safe place for save files on every platform, so you don't need to care where it lives on the Mac. Loading is the same thing backwards: read the file, parse the JSON, and hand each dictionary back to its node.

Step four: put a version number in it. This is the one everyone forgets. Add a field called version, set to one. Because one day you'll add something new, like the radish monster's tier, and old save files won't have it. With a version number, your loading code can say "this is an old save, give it a sensible default", instead of crashing. Future you will be very grateful. Present you will forget anyway. I'll remind you. That's my job.

Now, you might be wondering: why JSON, when Godot can save Resources directly, with ResourceSaver? You can, and for your own settings it's handy. But there's a catch worth knowing. A Godot Resource file can contain scripts, and loading one can run code. So if anyone ever shares save files, loading a Resource from somewhere you don't trust is a security risk. JSON, or Godot's ConfigFile, is just data. It can't run anything. For save games, plain data is the safer habit.

Okay, now the theory, with an everyday analogy. Think of a save file like a note you leave yourself before you go to bed. You don't write down the whole house. You write "keys are on the bench, washing's in the machine, the radish is in the fridge". In the morning, the house is already there. You just need the note to put things back where they were. That's why good save systems are small. The game rebuilds the world from its own files, and the save just tells it what's different.

And here's a design question that's very Stardew. When does your game save? Stardew Valley only saves when you go to sleep at the end of the day. That's a deliberate choice. It means you can't save in the middle of a bad day, so a mistake sticks, and every day feels like it matters. It also means you only have to save at one quiet moment, when nothing is moving, which makes the code much simpler. For a farming game, I think saving when you sleep is perfect.

And this is where it gets interesting for the radish monster. Remember its hidden counter? The more radishes you pull, the closer it gets to bursting out, and then the counter resets. Should that counter be in the save file? Because if it isn't, a clever player pulls nineteen radishes, sees the ground start shaking, quits, reloads, and the counter's back to zero. Very clever player. Very cowardly. So yes, save the counter. And for extra fairness, save the seed of your random number generator too, so a reload doesn't reroll the monster's arrival.

And one last thought. If you save the counter... the radish remembers. Every radish you've ever pulled. Even the ones you thought it didn't see. So here's the short tip list.

One: a "persist" group, and two functions on each node, one to save to a Dictionary and one to load from it.

Two: JSON, through FileAccess, into the user folder.

Three: a version number from day one.

Four: save when the player sleeps.

Five: store item ids, never whole objects, and save the radish counter and the random seed.

And the smallest buildable step for this week: make a save button that writes the player's position and one item count to a file, and a load button that puts them back. Two buttons. One evening. And then your radishes are safe forever. Or doomed forever. Depends which side you're on.

EFFECTS

Effects deep dive! And this week, something a bit different from our usual monsters: Top Gun: Maverick, from twenty twenty two, and how they put real actors in real fighter jets. Let's go step by step, piece by piece.

First, the problem. In the original Top Gun, in nineteen eighty six, a lot of the actors' cockpit close-ups ended up being shot on the ground, with the flying added around them. Director Joseph Kosinski and Tom Cruise wanted the opposite for the sequel. They wanted the actors' faces to show real G-force, real sunlight moving across the cockpit, real fear. And you can't fake that on the ground.

Piece one: training. The actors weren't allowed to fly the jets themselves. The Navy's F-eighteens were flown by real Navy pilots, with the actors in the back seat. But to survive that, the actors needed training. Cruise designed a programme that tried to, in his words, "condense two years of flight training into three months". They started in a small Cessna propeller plane, then moved to an Extra three hundred aerobatic plane, pulling around six or seven G without G-suits, then an L-thirty nine jet trainer, and finally the back seat of the F-eighteen, where they took up to about eight G. They also did the Navy's water survival training, which includes being strapped into a cockpit that's dropped upside down into a pool. So, a normal acting job. Learn your lines, and survive being drowned upside down. I'd want a bigger trailer.

Piece two: the cameras. This is where the film made history. Sony built a special version of its Venice cinema camera, using what's called the Rialto extension. It splits the camera in two: the sensor and lens go on one end of a cable, and the body of the camera goes somewhere else. That meant a tiny camera head could be squeezed into the cockpit, pointing at the actor, while the bulky part was hidden away. Kosinski has said there were six of these cameras per jet: some on the actor, some looking forward out of the canopy, and some looking back. And when two jets flew together, that was twelve cameras at once. One honest note: behind-the-scenes photos show about four inside the cockpit, so some of those six were probably mounted outside.

Piece three: the actors operating them. Here's the fun part. There's no camera crew in a fighter jet. So the actors had to do it themselves. Cruise taught them how to turn the cameras on and off, check the framing and keep the lighting in mind, because once they were in the air, nobody could help them. The daily routine started with a two-hour briefing, there were about three thousand eight hundred storyboards, and each scene was rehearsed in a mock-up cockpit on the ground before anyone took off. And if an actor forgot to press record, that was a very expensive flight to nowhere. I'd have written "record" on my hand. In marker.

Piece four: the footage. All that flying produced about eight hundred hours of material. And the editors had to sift through all of it for those few seconds where the actor's face, the light and the sky all came together.

Piece five: the part that wasn't real. Because the film is famous for being "practical", but it also has around two thousand four hundred visual effects shots. The enemy fighters, the fictional "fifth-generation" jets, were completely digital, animated over footage of real L-thirty nine jets flying the same moves. The real F-eighteens were sometimes digitally repainted, and given digital missiles. Some formations were filled out with computer-generated planes. And the Darkstar, the hypersonic plane at the start, was a full-size physical mock-up built with input from Lockheed's Skunk Works, with digital exhaust and heat haze added. The stratosphere it climbs into was digital too.

So the trick is the mix. The faces are real, the G-force is real, the light is real, and the impossible parts are added around them. Your eye trusts the shot, because the part you look at most, the human face, is completely honest.

The practical lesson for you: when you film with the X-M5, or even your phone, put the camera where the action really is, even if it's a small, awkward spot. A phone clipped to the handlebar of a bike, or wedged on the dashboard facing you, looks more real than any wide shot from the footpath. Real movement, real light, real face. Just maybe not upside down in a pool. Unless you really want to.

Director deep dive. And it's raining in this one. It's always raining in this one. David Fincher's Se7en, from nineteen ninety five.

If you've never seen it, here's the set-up without spoilers. Two detectives, an old one about to retire, played by Morgan Freeman, and a young, hot-headed one, played by Brad Pitt, hunt a killer who's staging each murder around one of the seven deadly sins. It was written by Andrew Kevin Walker, it cost about thirty three million US dollars, and it made about three hundred and twenty nine million around the world. For a dark, grim crime film with an infamous ending, that's enormous. And it was Fincher's second feature, after the troubled experience of Alien Three. Which, given our Ripley month, feels like a nice bridge.

So how did he shoot it? Let's start with the look, because the look is the film.

Fincher worked with the cinematographer Darius Khondji. Together they wanted a city that feels wet, dirty and exhausted, a place where the light never quite gets in. So Khondji often underexposed the interiors by about two stops, letting rooms fall into deep shadow, and used smoke in the air and a technique called flashing, where the film is exposed to a little light, to control how hard the contrast got. Then came the famous part: the prints. Khondji worked with Beverly Wood at the Deluxe lab on a process called CCE, short for colour contrast enhancement. It's a kind of bleach bypass, which means leaving some of the silver in the film instead of washing it all out. That makes the blacks deeper, the colours more washed out, and everything grittier.

Here's a lovely detail. CCE was so expensive that only some of the first cinema prints got the real process. The rest were made a cheaper way, from a bleach-bypassed copy. And when the film was restored recently, it was scanned again from the original negative at eight K, and Fincher used one of those old CCE prints as his reference, to get the look right. So the restoration was matched to a print most people never saw.

Next: the rain. The film's city is never named, and it feels like it's been raining for a hundred years. The rain machines were reportedly running in overdrive. Rain does three jobs for Fincher. It makes the streets shine, so there's always a reflection of a light somewhere in a dark frame. It makes everyone look tired and cold. And it gives the sound a constant hiss underneath, so the city never feels quiet. And then, at the very end, the rain stops. The final scenes were shot in open, dry desert country near Lancaster, in California, under huge power lines. After two hours of wet, closed-in rooms, suddenly there's a wide, empty, sunlit landscape. And instead of feeling relieved, you feel exposed. That's a director using the absence of something to make you uneasy.

And somewhere in all that sunshine... a delivery van arrives. With a little box. I won't say what's inside. Nobody ever wants... to open it.

Then there are the opening titles. They were made by Kyle Cooper, at a company called R slash Greenberg Associates, and they're among the most influential title sequences ever. You see the killer's hands making his notebooks: cutting, gluing, writing, shaving the skin off his fingertips. And the credits themselves were made by hand: the type was scratched into film, and the shaky, flickering effect came from optical work, not computers. It's cut to a remix of the Nine Inch Nails song Closer. It's two minutes long, and it tells you everything about the killer before you ever meet him.

And the blocking. Watch how Fincher often keeps Freeman still and Pitt moving. The older detective stands, watches and thinks. The younger one paces, interrupts and pushes forward. You learn who they are from how they move in the frame, before they say much at all.

So, the practical lesson for you, learning Final Cut Pro and colour. You can get a little of the Se7en look on footage you already have. Bring down the exposure so the shadows really go dark. Pull down the saturation, especially in the warm colours. Then add contrast back with a curve: lift the bright parts, and press the darks down. That's a rough digital version of a bleach bypass. And here's the bigger lesson: Fincher lit for the shadows, not the faces. Next time you film at night, put your subject next to one small light, a lamp or a shop window, and let everything else fall away. What you can't see is often scarier than what you can.
