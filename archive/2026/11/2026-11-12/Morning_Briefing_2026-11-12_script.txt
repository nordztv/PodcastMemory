JAPAN

You know what I was thinking about tonight, cooking dinner? Food. Obviously. But Osaka food, specifically. Because you've never really heard me go on about it, and I can go on about it for hours.

There's an old saying in Japan. In Kyoto, people ruin themselves on clothes: "Kyō no kidaore". And in Osaka, people ruin themselves on food: "Ōsaka no kuidaore". Eat until you drop. Kyoto people dress beautifully and say polite things they don't mean. Osaka people eat everything and say exactly what they mean. I'm not biased. At all.

And there's a reason for it. Back in the Edo period, hundreds of years ago, Osaka was called "the nation's kitchen", tenka no daidokoro. All the rice from all over Japan came through Osaka's merchants and warehouses, and so did the best seafood, the kelp from Hokkaido, everything. So Osaka became a city of merchants, not samurai. Merchants spend their money on what makes them happy, and what makes them happy is dinner.

So, the classics. Takoyaki, little balls of batter with octopus inside, which were invented in Osaka in the nineteen thirties. Kushikatsu, things on sticks, crumbed and fried, with a big shared pot of sauce. And there's one rule: no double dipping. Never! In Osaka you would be arrested. Not really. But the whole bar would look at you.

And okonomiyaki. My family's food. "Okonomi" means "what you like", and "yaki" means "grilled". In Osaka style, you mix everything together in the bowl, cabbage and batter and pork and whatever you like, and cook it on the hotplate, then sauce, mayonnaise, bonito flakes and seaweed on top. In Hiroshima they layer it instead, with noodles. Hiroshima people will tell you theirs is better. Hiroshima people are wrong. Very politely wrong.

WORKSHOP

Okay, so here's a little job for you, and you can do it on this walk home, right now, in ten minutes. A moving portrait, the way I'd do it.

You know those ten second shots in films, where someone just stands there, the city behind them, breathing, looking at the camera, and it says everything? Let's make one. You'll need your Z R and a friend, or a colleague, or a kind stranger.

Step one. Find shade with light coming from one side. Under the trees on the edge of Hyde Park is perfect: open shade, soft light from the open path beside you. Put your person so the bright side is on one side of their face, not behind them.

Step two. Your settings: twenty five frames, the shutter at one fiftieth, the aperture as wide as your lens goes, and focus on their eyes. If your camera shows a face or eye box, let it follow them.

Step three. Stand close. Closer than feels polite. About an arm and a half away. Hold the camera at their eye height, not looking down on them.

Step four, the most important one, and this is my secret from five years of photographing nervous hotel guests and my own family. Never say "smile". Everyone's face dies when you say smile. Instead, say something silly. Ask them what they had for lunch, or tell them you're pretending to be a famous director. Let them laugh. Then stop talking, and just keep filming. The second after the laugh, when their face relaxes... that's your shot.

Step five. Film for twenty seconds, then do it once more. That's it.
