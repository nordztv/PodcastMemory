== Australia ==

At two thirty this afternoon, the Reserve Bank announces its decision on interest rates. Markets put the chance of a rise above eighty per cent. If it happens, the cash rate goes from four point six to four point eight five per cent. That would be the highest since two thousand and eight.

Why are they so sure? Inflation. The September figures showed the trimmed mean, the measure the bank watches most closely, rising one per cent in a single month. The bank's own forecast was point eight. Headline inflation is running at four point three per cent a year. So the bank is behind where it hoped to be, and it's been saying for weeks that it will act.

The ABC's Stephen Letts makes a good point: this afternoon, the governor's press conference may matter more than the decision itself. A hike is priced in. What isn't priced in is whether she signals more to come. If the message is "one and done", that's one kind of summer. If it's "we're not finished", that's another.

The banks are already talking as if rates stay high. Westpac reported its full-year results yesterday: a net profit of six point eight billion dollars, a dividend of a dollar fifty four, and a slightly thinner margin on its loans. Its chief executive, Anthony Miller, says rates are "likely to remain elevated into twenty twenty seven".

And house prices are already feeling it. Cotality's index fell one point two per cent in October. That's the seventh monthly fall in a row. Nationally, values are now six point seven per cent below the March peak, about sixty three thousand dollars off the median home. Cotality's Gerard Burg says the market is "on track to see the largest downturn across the last forty years". AMP's Shane Oliver now expects a fifteen per cent fall from top to bottom.

Why it matters: for anyone with a mortgage, a hike today means higher repayments within weeks. For anyone trying to buy, prices are falling, but borrowing is getting dearer at the same time, so it isn't simply getting easier. What happens next: the decision at two thirty, alongside the bank's updated forecasts, which will show how long it thinks this lasts, and then the governor's press conference.

All of that lands on the government's budget update, the mid-year economic outlook, due in December. The Finance Minister, Katy Gallagher, was asked on the weekend which areas are safe from cuts. == Lore ==

Deep dive time. And today, I want to take you into the jungle. Metal Gear Solid Three: Snake Eater.

It came out on the PlayStation Two in late two thousand and four. And it's a strange one, because it was a prequel nobody expected. The first two Metal Gear Solid games were set in the near future, with high-tech soldiers, codecs and giant robots. Then Hideo Kojima's team went the other way: back to nineteen sixty four. The Cold War. A Soviet jungle. No radar. Almost no technology. Just a man, a knife, and a lot of very confused wildlife.

The hero is Naked Snake, a young American agent. Fans had known for years that the series' great villain, Big Boss, was once a hero. Snake Eater is the story of how that happened. And it's a much sadder story than anyone expected.

Here's the setup. Snake is sent into the Soviet Union on a mission to rescue a scientist. His mentor is a legendary soldier called The Boss, the woman who more or less invented special forces, and the person Snake trusts more than anyone. == Game tech ==

Game tech! And today it's night time. Or rather, how to make night time, in your game.

You've given me two lovely night ideas already: your night-only recipe, moon-cakes made with tears from stars, and yesterday I suggested a falling-star event on lucky nights. Neither of those works if it never gets dark. Your farm currently lives in a permanent, cheerful noon. Like a sitcom. Let's fix that.

So, the practical part first. Here's the smallest version you can build tonight in your new project, in about half an hour.

Step one. In your main world scene, add a node called CanvasModulate. That's it, just one node. CanvasModulate tints everything drawn on that canvas layer by one colour. White means normal daylight. A deep blue-grey means night. A warm orange means sunset. Think of it like a stage lighting gel: the set doesn't change, but the colour of the light over it does.

Step two. Make a little time keeper. A script, set up as an autoload, so every scene can see it. Call it TimeOfDay. It holds one number, the time, going from zero to one over a whole day. Zero is midnight, a quarter is six in the morning, a half is noon. In its process function, add a little to that number each frame. For testing, make a whole day last two minutes, so you can watch it change.

Step three, the pretty part. Give your CanvasModulate a Gradient: an exported variable you can edit in the inspector. Put colours along it: dark blue at midnight, a soft pink at dawn, white through the day, gold in the late afternoon, purple at dusk, then back to blue. Then every frame, set the CanvasModulate's colour to the gradient sampled at the current time. In Godot that's one line: color equals gradient dot sample of the time. And just like that, your farm has sunrises.

Step four: keep the interface out of it. CanvasModulate only affects the layer it's on. So put your health bar, your inventory and your clock on a separate CanvasLayer, and they'll stay bright and readable even at midnight. If your menus suddenly go dark blue, that's the fix.

Now, the theory, and why night usually looks wrong in games. If you just darken everything, the world looks muddy, not like night. Real night isn't simply darker. It's bluer, it has less contrast in the shadows, and anything that gives off light, a lamp, a window, a campfire, stands out much more. So a darkened screen with no lights looks like a broken monitor. A darkened screen with a few warm lights looks like an evening.

That's where the second tool comes in. PointLight2D. Add one to a lantern, a farmhouse window or a campfire, give it a soft round light texture, and set its colour to a warm orange. At noon you barely notice it. At midnight, it glows. You can turn its energy up as the time gets later, so the lights seem to switch on at dusk. And if you want walls and trees to block the light, add LightOccluder2D nodes to them, and turn on shadows on the light.

One thing to watch: with a lot of lights on screen at once, 2D lighting starts to cost performance. A farm with twenty lanterns is fine. A festival with five hundred might not be. Start small.

Now, the fun part: making night matter. Because a day-night cycle is just decoration until something changes.

Give TimeOfDay a simple function, is night, that returns true when the time is after about eight in the evening or before five in the morning. Then your recipes can check it: if a recipe has the night only flag, it's greyed out unless is night is true. Moon-cakes, only after dark. And your falling-star event becomes a random roll, once a night, that spawns a few glowing star tear items in a field, each with its own little PointLight2D so you can see them sparkle from across the farm.

And night is when the yokai come out. So when is night returns true... the radish counter goes up a little faster. Because some radishes... prefer the dark.

Here's your short tip list.

One: CanvasModulate for the overall colour, PointLight2D for things that glow. Two: drive the colour from a Gradient, so you can tune it by eye instead of with numbers. Three: keep the interface on its own CanvasLayer. Four: when you save the game, save the time of day, so a player who sleeps at midnight doesn't wake up at noon by accident. That fits straight into the save system we talked about yesterday. And five: Stardew Valley's trick is that the day ends whether you like it or not. A clock that pushes the player along is what makes a farming game feel urgent.

So that's your homework. One CanvasModulate. One Gradient. One sunset. Watching it on loop for twenty minutes doesn't count as work. Well. A bit of work. I'll allow it.

== Effects ==

Effects! And today, an effect with no effects at all. Tom Cruise, a motorbike, and a cliff in Norway.

It's the big stunt in Mission: Impossible, Dead Reckoning Part One, from twenty twenty three. Ethan Hunt rides a motorbike off the edge of a mountain, lets go of the bike in mid-air, falls, and opens a parachute. And the thing is, that's exactly what happened. Tom Cruise really did it. No green screen, no digital double for the jump itself.

So how do you build a stunt like that? Let's go piece by piece.

Piece one: the training. Paramount says Cruise spent years preparing, with hundreds of skydives and thousands of motocross jumps, in England, before he ever went near the real cliff. They even built a full-size practice ramp at a quarry in Britain, so he could do the take-off again and again, safely, over and over, until it was routine. The idea is simple: the dangerous part should be the one thing he's done more often than anything else in his life.

Piece two: the location. They needed a sheer drop with nothing below for a long way, so the parachute had time to open. They found it in Norway, a mountainside called Helsetkopen, near the fjords, with an enormous drop below.

Piece three: the ramp. And this is where it gets clever. You can't just ride off a cliff edge, because a bike that goes off a rough edge tips forward and tumbles. So the crew built a long, smooth wooden ramp running down the mountainside and out over the drop, with a lip at the end to throw the bike up and outwards. That way Cruise leaves the edge with the bike level and moving away from the rock. They spent months on it, and it took a lot of work just to get the materials up the mountain.

Piece four: the separation. Once he's in the air, he has to push away from the bike, so it doesn't follow him down and hit him or his parachute. Then he falls clear, in free fall, before opening the chute. In the film, you only see a few seconds of it. In real life, every one of those seconds was planned.

Piece five: the cameras. How do you film someone falling off a mountain? From everywhere at once. There were cameras on the ramp, a camera on a long cable line, drones, and a helicopter following him down. And for the shot that ends up in the film, the one where you see his face as he leaves the edge, a camera was mounted to capture him close, so you can tell it really is him.

And the day itself. The weather in the mountains is unpredictable, so they moved the jump forward, to the very start of the shoot, to catch a clear day. And on that first day, he did the jump... six times. Six.

Six times off a mountain. On a Monday. I struggle to get off the couch six times on a Monday. That was a joke about me. I'm allowed.

So why does it matter that it's real? Because audiences can feel it, even when they can't explain it. A digital cliff jump would be perfect, but it would weigh nothing. When you know a real person is really falling, your stomach drops with him. The director, Christopher McQuarrie, built the whole film around that moment: the story was written to lead to the jump, not the other way around.

And the practical lesson, for you, with a camera. You don't need a cliff. Please don't find a cliff. The lesson is in the planning. Cruise's team broke one huge moment into small, rehearsed pieces: the approach, the take-off, the separation, the fall, the landing. Each piece had its own camera, and each was practised until it was boring.

You can do the same with something tiny. Next time you want a short sequence on your walk, say, a coffee cup going from the counter to the park, plan the pieces: the hand, the cup, the walk, the bench, the first sip. One shot for each. Rehearse each one a couple of times before you hit record. You'll be amazed how much more "real" it feels when every moment was planned.

And then you'll understand why Tom Cruise does his jumps first thing on day one. So he can relax for the rest of the shoot. Ah, sorry. Okay. That one was a stretch.

== Director ==

Director time. And as promised, this one connects to Phantom Blade Zero: the Hong Kong action film that game, and half of modern action cinema, owes a debt to. John Woo's Hard Boiled.

It came out in nineteen ninety two. Chow Yun-fat plays Inspector Yuen, a jazz-playing Hong Kong cop everyone calls "Tequila". Tony Leung plays Tony, an undercover cop deep inside a gun-smuggling gang. They don't know they're on the same side. And it all ends in a hospital, with the smugglers' weapons hidden in the basement, and a maternity ward full of newborn babies on one of the floors above.

It was John Woo's last Hong Kong film before he went to Hollywood, and you can feel him throwing everything he'd learned at the screen.

So how did he shoot it? Let's start with the cameras. Woo shot his action scenes with several cameras running at once, each at a different speed. One at normal speed, others in slow motion. Then in the edit, he cut between them. So a single dive through the air might be seen at normal speed from one angle, then slowed right down from another, then normal again, all in a couple of seconds. That's the "Woo" feeling: time stretches and snaps back, like music.

And he thinks of action as music. He's said so himself: he cuts gunfights like a dance, or a musical number, to a rhythm. Watch the opening teahouse shootout, with the birds in their cages, and you can almost count the beats.

His other trademarks are all here, too. The two-gun dive, sideways, through the air. The slow-motion doves. The "Mexican stand-off", two men with guns pointed at each other's faces, often friends. And the idea that a gunfight can be about honour and loyalty, not just violence.

But the scene everyone talks about is in the hospital. It looks like one continuous shot, almost three minutes long. Tequila and Tony fight their way down a corridor, into a lift, out onto another floor, through more gunmen, with the camera following them the whole way. No cuts. Or so it seems.

Okay... come closer. There's a cut in it. When they step into the lift... the doors close... and that's where it's hidden. The crew used the moment the doors shut to quickly redress the same set as a different floor. When the doors open again... it's a new floor. Same corridor.

Isn't that clever? The whole thing was choreographed and rehearsed so the actors, the stunt team, the squibs for the bullet hits, and the camera all moved together. If anything went wrong, they had to go back to the start. And because it's one long take, you feel trapped inside the action with the heroes. No cutting away to catch your breath.

Then there's the ending, with the babies. Tequila carries a newborn out through the gunfire, and plugs the baby's ears with cotton wool. It's absurd, and tense, and sweet, all at once. It's very Woo: the most violent film of his career, and its heart is a cop protecting a baby.

The influence is everywhere. The Matrix's lobby shootout. Max Payne, the video game, which basically turned Woo's slow-motion dives into a button. Stranglehold, an actual sequel to Hard Boiled as a game, with Chow Yun-fat himself. And now Phantom Blade Zero, with its Kungfupunk fighting, selling four million copies.

So, your practical lesson. Two of them, because they're both easy.

One: the speed change. Your phone can already shoot slow motion. Next time you film something moving, a ferry pulling in, a pigeon taking off in the Botanic Garden, shoot it twice: once at normal speed, once in slow motion, from a different angle. In Final Cut, cut from the normal shot to the slow one right at the most interesting moment, then back again. That little stretch of time is the Woo feeling, and it works for anything, not just gunfights.

Two: the hidden cut. If you want a shot that looks continuous, find a moment where the screen goes dark or blocked: someone walks right past the lens, the camera pans across a pillar or a dark doorway. End one take there, start the next take from the same spot, and cut on that moment. Nobody will see the join. It's how Woo did it, and it's how a lot of "one take" films still do it.

And if you're filming in a hospital... maybe skip the squibs. Hospitals hate that. I've heard.

== Workshop ==

Workshop! And today it's a ten minute job for your Dell. Something you'll set up once, and then never think about again... until the day it saves you.

With Kioxia in the news, it's a good moment to think about the drives you already have, because replacing them isn't getting any cheaper. You've got eight twelve terabyte drives in that server. And whatever you end up deciding about the server's future, those drives are worth looking after while they're spinning. The question is: if one of them was quietly getting sick, would you know?

Hard drives keep a health diary. It's called SMART, and it records things like how many bad spots the drive has found and moved data away from. TrueNAS can test the drives and read that diary for you automatically. So here's the setup, step by step.

Step one. In TrueNAS, go to Data Protection, and find the SMART tests section. The menus move around a little between versions, so if it isn't exactly there, search for "SMART".

Step two. Add a short test. Select all your drives, and schedule it weekly, say, Sunday at three in the morning. A short test takes a couple of minutes and checks the basics.

Step three. Add a long test, monthly. Pick a quiet night, mid-month. A long test reads every part of every drive, so on twelve terabyte drives it can take the best part of a day. Don't schedule it at the same time as the pool's scrub, which also reads everything; let them take turns.

Step four. Check the scrub schedule. Under the same Data Protection page, there's a scrub task for your pool. A scrub reads all your data and checks it against its checksums, so silent damage gets found and repaired. Once a month is plenty.

Step five, the important one. Tell TrueNAS how to reach you. In the alert settings, set up an email, or one of the other alert services, and send yourself a test alert. Because a warning that nobody reads... is just a very organised diary of a disaster. Sorry. That got dark fast.

And one extra, if you want to look now: open any drive's SMART results and check two numbers. "Reallocated sectors" and "pending sectors". On a healthy drive, they're zero. If either one starts climbing, that drive is telling you it's tired. Order a replacement before it fails, not after.

Ten minutes. One email. Ninety six terabytes, safely watched. Which is more than I can say for your lens comparison spreadsheet. Does that one have backups? Asking for a friend.
