Now to Australia, and we'll start with some good news, which has been in short supply on this story.

[serious] Helen Haines, the independent member for Indi, has been upgraded from critical to serious but stable. The Alfred hospital said so on Saturday morning. She has lost both legs after last Monday's alleged attack outside her office in Wangaratta, and her husband Phil put it simply: "The good news in all this is that she's alive." We've also learned more about the case. Brendan Nugent, who's forty three and from Tarrawingee, is charged with dangerous driving causing serious injury, intentionally causing serious injury, and causing harm to a Commonwealth public official. His lawyer says he has a rare neurological condition and mental health concerns. No motive has been established, he remains in custody, and he's next due in court in February.

Why it matters: a sitting member of parliament being badly hurt outside her own office has changed how this whole place is talking about safety. And that came through clearly on Insiders yesterday, where the Australian Federal Police Commissioner, Krissy Barrett, made a striking argument. She said there is a "direct connection" between heated political language and threats against MPs, and that sometimes the connection is "very immediate". Asked about One Nation's cartoons showing politicians being hit by cars, she said that was the kind of thing she meant: "words matter". She also challenged any Australian to read the threats MPs actually receive and still call them free speech. Duress alarms for members are being considered. To be fair to the other side of that argument, she was also careful to say motive in the Haines case is still the primary focus, and that mental health "does sometimes form part" of what drives these things. The Coalition's Ted O'Brien said the attack should be a reason for everyone to pause the personal nastiness, and Labor's Catherine King says security at Parliament House itself may be reassessed. What happens next: expect the security question to come up again and again during Senate estimates this week.

It's not just Canberra, either. Here in New South Wales, some state MPs were reportedly left in tears after a parliamentary security briefing on Friday, saying they didn't get the answers they wanted.

A Canavan versus Ashby contest would be one of the most interesting fights on the right. [beat] Two men competing to be the most Queensland person in Queensland.

Western Australian senator Jordon Steele-John is taking leave while an independent investigation commissioned by the WA Greens looks at allegations of inappropriate behaviour towards women between 2017 and 2025. Now, money. This is a big week for your mortgage, your rent and the price of everything. The September quarter inflation figures land on Wednesday. Last week the Reserve Bank's deputy governor, Andrew Hauser, said the inflation outlook had become "materially worse", and markets now put the chance of a November rate rise at about fifty one per cent, up from under twenty per cent the week before. The cash rate is already four point six per cent after four rises this year. A strong number on Wednesday could tip the board towards a fifth on Melbourne Cup day, Tuesday the third of November. Why it matters: with oil above a hundred and ten dollars and the Middle East flaring, petrol is the wild card in that number.

Now, New South Wales and Sydney, and we have to start in the west, because yesterday was a very big day out there. [happy] Western Sydney International Airport, officially Nancy-Bird Walton Airport, is open, and it has real planes in it. The first commercial flight, Jetstar's J Q three sixty two to the Gold Coast, took off a little before midday, completely sold out. The first pilot was Aglaya Papadokokolakis, who grew up in Blacktown, and who said the day still felt very surreal. The first Melbourne flight left last night, and the first international flight, to Auckland, goes today. The Prime Minister called it "an absolutely extraordinary day", and the opposition leader Angus Taylor said he was proud to see history made in Western Sydney, so for once both sides agree on something. Seven airlines have signed on so far, including Qantas, Qatar, Air New Zealand and Singapore Airlines, and the airport has no curfew. Why it matters: it's the first new major airport for Sydney in more than a century, and it should take pressure off Mascot over time. What's next: there's no train yet. The metro line out to St Marys isn't due until next year, so for now it's a free shuttle bus, and Penrith's mayor is already calling for better roads.

And here's the one about your line. On Saturday, Premier Chris Minns scrapped the big new rail timetable that was due to start on the fifteenth of November. It would have added about two hundred services a week and lifted T4 peak trains from every ten minutes to every seven and a half. But it came with changes people really didn't like, including T8 trains no longer stopping at Sydenham, and peak direct services to St Peters, Erskineville and Redfern disappearing. Minns put it bluntly: "I'd rather apologise and backflip on a policy like this than implement a change that people don't want." The Transport Minister, John Graham, says there'll be no changes at all until reliability returns to the T4. The Liberal member for Oatley, Mark Coure, says the backflip only happened because the community called it out. Why it matters for you: your T8 trips through Green Square stay as they are, at least for now. What's next: nobody's said when a revised timetable might come back.

The Minns government has also passed the law creating the Great Koala National Park on the Mid North Coast. It adds a hundred and seventy eight thousand hectares of state forest to existing parks, making a protected area of more than four hundred and seventy five thousand hectares between Coffs Harbour and Kempsey, home to about twelve thousand koalas. The government says around a hundred new National Parks jobs come with it, and a support package for timber workers, and that the Gumbaynggirr and Dunghutti peoples will help guide how it's managed. It's due to open before the end of the year. The Environment Minister, Penny Sharpe, called it a defining moment for conservation. It's been a long fight with the timber industry, so expect that argument to continue in the regions.
