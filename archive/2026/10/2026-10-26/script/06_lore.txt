[happy] Deep dive time. And today's lore is one I've been saving: the Witcher, and the two big ideas that hold Andrzej Sapkowski's whole world together. The Conjunction of the Spheres, and the Elder Blood. If you've ever wondered why the Continent is crawling with monsters, why witchers exist at all, and why absolutely everyone wants Ciri, it all starts here.

Let's go back about fifteen hundred years before Geralt's story. The world we call the Continent was one of many worlds, or spheres, each sitting in its own dimension. Then something happened that nobody in the books fully understands. The spheres collided. For a moment, the walls between worlds thinned and broke, and things fell through. That's the Conjunction of the Spheres.

What fell through? Monsters, mostly. Ghouls, graveirs, vampires, all sorts of creatures that had no business being in this world, and when the spheres pulled apart again, they were stranded. That's why so many of the beasts Geralt hunts feel wrong for their surroundings. They're not natural predators. They're refugees from other realities who can never go home, which is a surprisingly sad way to think about a ghoul. [dark] Imagine being a perfectly respectable creature in your own world, minding your own business, eating whatever it is ghouls eat at home, and then one day the sky opens and you're in a cemetery outside Vizima, very hungry, and the locals have pitchforks. [punch] Nobody even offered you a map.

Magic came through too, or at least the raw power of it, which the books call Chaos. Sorcerers are people who learned to tap into it, and you'll notice they always talk about magic as something dangerous and borrowed, never quite natural. That's the Conjunction again.

And here's the bit that matters most: humans may not belong on the Continent either. The elves were there long before, having arrived on white ships, and the elves certainly see humans as latecomers who spread like a weed. The books keep it a little murky, but the human arrival, the "Landing of the Exiles", came long after the elves, and it reshaped everything. That's the root of the bitter elf-versus-human conflict that runs through the whole saga: the Scoia'tael guerrillas, the pogroms, the elves pushed into the mountains. The ancient owners of the land and the newcomers who took it.

So, where do witchers fit? With monsters loose everywhere and no army able to deal with them, mages created witchers. They took boys, put them through brutal training and the Trial of the Grasses, a mutation process that most didn't survive, and turned out professional monster hunters with cat-like eyes, fast reflexes and a resistance to poisons. Geralt of Rivia is one of the products. Witchers were a solution to the Conjunction. And here's the irony that Sapkowski enjoys: by Geralt's time, the monsters are fewer, the world is more civilised, and nobody really wants witchers anymore. They're a tool that's outlived its job, distrusted by the people they protect. It's one of the reasons Geralt is such a great character. He's a professional at the end of his profession.

Now, the Elder Blood. And this is where it gets romantic, and tragic, which is the Witcher's favourite combination.

Among the elves, there was a bloodline with a special gene, the Elder Gene, which gives power over space and time: the ability to move between worlds and even between eras. The elves had been carefully breeding it, a long, cold, deliberate programme. The most important carrier was an elven sorceress named Lara Dorren. And Lara Dorren did the one thing she wasn't supposed to do. She fell in love with a human mage, Cregennan of Lod.

Elves and humans were already at each other's throats, and the relationship was scandalous on both sides. Cregennan was murdered. Lara, pregnant and hunted, fled. The story goes that she asked a human queen for shelter and was refused, and in her rage and grief she laid a curse on that queen's line. Lara died, but her daughter, Riannon, survived. And so the Elder Blood passed into a human family.

Follow that line down through the generations, and you get to Queen Calanthe of Cintra, the Lioness. Then to her daughter, Pavetta. And then to Pavetta's daughter: Cirilla. Ciri. That's why everyone wants her. She isn't just a princess or a witcher's ward. She carries the Elder Blood, the gene the elves spent centuries trying to perfect, and it surfaced in a human girl.

And then there's the prophecy. An elven seer called Ithlinne foretold the Tedd Deireadh, the Time of the End: the Time of the Sword and Axe, the Time of Contempt, and finally the White Frost, an endless, killing cold. Within the prophecy is the hint that a child of the Elder Blood will either save the world or destroy it. Every power on the Continent hears that and reaches the same conclusion. [punch] Get the girl.

That's the engine behind the whole saga. Emhyr, the Emperor of Nilfgaard, wants her. Sorcerers want her. And the elves want her back. In the Witcher three, that's the Wild Hunt: the spectral riders who are really the Aen Elle, the Alder Folk, elves from another world, led by Eredin. They want Ciri's power because their own world is dying to the White Frost, and they need a way out. Avallac'h, the elven sage who travels with her, has his own agenda tied up with Lara Dorren's old story, too.

And look at how CD Projekt Red pays it off. The Witcher three's big finale is about Ciri facing the White Frost herself, stepping through into it to stop it. Whether she comes back, and as what, depends on how you treated her through the game. Did you let her make her own choices? Did you have a snowball fight? Did you go with her to smash up a lab? All those small fatherly moments decide whether Ciri ends as a witcher, an empress, or doesn't return at all. That's the fifteen-hundred-year-old Conjunction coming home: a story that began with worlds crashing together ends with a young woman deciding the fate of all of them.

Which, by the way, is exactly why the Witcher four, with Ciri as the lead, makes so much sense. She's the one person who can walk between the spheres. Every monster contract she takes is, in a way, cleaning up a family mess that's fifteen centuries old.
