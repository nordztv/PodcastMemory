Australian politics. It's been a busy sitting fortnight in Canberra, and the theme is a Labor government that has the numbers in the House but keeps running into walls in the Senate.

Let's start with the Australia European Union free trade agreement. It was signed earlier this year, and on paper it's a big deal. Lower tariffs, more access for Australian exporters into a market of around four hundred and fifty million people. But it still has to be ratified by Parliament, and that's where it's in trouble.

On Friday, Nationals senator Bridget McKenzie said the Coalition will oppose it. Nationals leader Matt Canavan put it even more bluntly in The Australian newspaper, writing that the Liberals and Nationals oppose the deal. His main argument is dairy. Under the agreement, tariffs on European cheese come down, and Canavan says European governments subsidise their farmers, so Australian dairy would be competing against foreign treasuries, not just foreign farms. The National Farmers' Federation agrees with him, calling the deal one sided.

Here's why that matters. Trade Minister Don Farrell has roughly thirty Senate votes lined up, and he needs thirty nine. Without the Coalition, the maths gets very hard. The government is now trying to turn this into a test of the opposition. Industry Minister Tim Ayres says supporting the deal is a test of the Coalition's economic credibility, and of Opposition Leader Angus Taylor himself.

And there's real tension on the other side too. Remember, it was a Coalition government that started negotiating with Europe in the first place. The free trade wing of the Liberal Party is reportedly uneasy about opposing a deal they began. Liberal senator Sarah Henderson's line is that the Coalition wants a stronger deal, not no deal. The party room hasn't formally locked in its position, and the agreement still has to go through the parliamentary treaties committee. So, it's not dead. But it is wounded.

Next, private health insurance, and a messy week for the government. Labor wants to wind back the private health insurance rebate for people aged sixty five and over, starting next April. The extra rebate older Australians get would be removed, and the roughly three billion dollars saved would go into aged care.

On Wednesday, the Coalition, the Greens and independent senator Jacqui Lambie teamed up to throw the bill out of the Senate. Not amend it. Discharge it entirely. Health Minister Mark Butler was furious. He called it a political stunt and an absolute travesty, and accused the new Greens leadership under David Shoebridge of reviving what he called a Coalition Greens alliance. Labor said it would reintroduce the bill and look at amendments in good faith. But the politics here are tricky. Older voters notice their premiums, and the Treasurer, Jim Chalmers, needs those savings for the budget. Softening the measure costs money he doesn't have.

There's more on the tax front. The government introduced legislation for a minimum thirty per cent tax on discretionary trusts. Those are the family trusts that wealthier Australians often use to spread income around the family and pay less tax. Angus Taylor called it greedy. The Greens are interested, but they've spotted what they think is a loophole. Their deputy leader, Steph Hodgins-May, says families could pay private school fees from trust funds and avoid the tax. So the Greens are waiting for an inquiry before they decide.

And gas. A bill to make exporters set aside up to twenty per cent of the gas they extract for the domestic market. The idea is to push down local energy prices. Resources Minister Madeleine King says that's exactly what it will do. The Greens want something different, a minimum twenty five per cent tax on gas exports, which they say would raise seventeen billion dollars a year.

A smaller story, but a very human one, about tone in Parliament. Liberal MP Angie Bell said the Prime Minister and Small Business Minister Anne Aly should be, quote, shamed and stoned in the square. That was over a decision by the Tax Office to ban credit card payments, which the government then delayed. Aly called the comment racist and misogynist. Bell withdrew it and called her to apologise. Pauline Hanson defended Bell.

Now, a parliamentary inquiry that's been getting a lot of attention. The House committee on elections is looking at the role of the Plymouth Brethren, a small, very private Christian church, in last year's federal election campaign. The Nine newspapers reported that church members allegedly paid far right influencers to follow and heckle the Prime Minister. On Friday the church's senior figure, Bruce Hales, appeared, after being told he'd be subpoenaed if he didn't. He denied any coordinated campaign for the Liberals and denied using antisemitic language. One revealing moment: he said he found out about one of his sons' involvement from a photo in the newspaper. His son Gareth said he volunteered for the Liberals as a private citizen. Coalition senators were angry about the inquiry itself. Bridget McKenzie called it religious discrimination, and Sarah Henderson called it a hyper partisan attack.

Defence had a hard week. A hidden recording device was found in a women's bathroom on the navy ship HMAS Leeuwin while it was docked in Cairns. A member of the defence force has been identified as a person of interest. The navy chief, Vice Admiral Matthew Buckley, called it despicable and a betrayal of trust. The navy has checked the rest of the fleet for devices. That landed the same week as the government's first women veterans' strategy, and its numbers are grim. More than half of serving women reported unacceptable behaviour at work, and military police logged two hundred and fifteen sexual offences, up twenty nine per cent. Jacqui Lambie says she'll bring a private member's bill on defence culture next year.

On a more positive note in our region, Australia will supply Bluebottle maritime drones to Fiji. These are uncrewed surface boats, a bit like a sailing robot, built here in Australia. They'll help Fiji watch for drug smuggling and illegal fishing, and the first one arrives early next year. Minister Pat Conroy wouldn't say how many, arguing that would tip off the smugglers.

And one more from foreign affairs. Israel's ambassador, Hillel Newman, said the audio from the drone strike that killed Australian aid worker Zomi Frankcom in Gaza won't be released, for security reasons. Foreign Minister Penny Wong called that insulting, and said Zomi's family deserves answers.

Now, to New South Wales.

The biggest state story this week is in the Hunter. Parliament has passed the Future Jobs and Investment Bill. It sets up a fund and a public authority to help coal dependent communities find new industries, and it requires coal mine owners to support workers when mines close. That matters, because the first major Hunter mine closures are expected within about four years. Community groups have campaigned for this for years, and Hunter Renewal called it an exciting moment. But there's a catch. The government currently puts in about twenty five million dollars a year, shared across four coal regions. The Hunter Jobs Alliance wants a hundred and fifty million a year, which they point out is only about five per cent of what the state collects in coal royalties. They also want the worker support, like paid retraining, to be enforceable, not optional. The bill goes back to the lower house for some small amendments before it becomes law.

Knife crime. Parliament has made the police knife scanning powers permanent. Officers can stop people and scan them with handheld metal detectors in declared areas. That started as a trial in twenty twenty four, modelled on Queensland's Jack's Law. The new law adds major entertainment venues to the list, alongside shopping centres, stations and sporting grounds, and it doubles how long an area can be declared, from twelve hours to twenty four. Police say they've taken more than fifteen thousand knives off the streets since April twenty twenty three. The reforms also follow a recommendation from the Bondi Junction coronial inquest. Both the Liberals and Nationals voted for it.

Animal welfare. The Minns government has introduced a bill for the state's first independent Office of Animal Welfare. It would oversee how the RSPCA and the Animal Welfare League use public money for cruelty enforcement, and take public complaints about their enforcement actions. Those two organisations get about twelve point eight million dollars a year for that work. The government says it's the last piece of its animal welfare reforms, after the puppy farm ban.

Out west, a group of councils has put a number on something everyone in outer western Sydney already feels. They say residents there spend around thirty nine thousand dollars a year more on cars than people in the inner city, simply because there isn't enough public transport. That will be a big argument heading toward the state election in March next year.

And, if you were watching the rugby last night, I'm sorry. The All Blacks beat the Wallabies forty three to twenty one in Sydney. That's thirteen straight Bledisloe losses to New Zealand.

One last New South Wales story to finish, a lovely one. Reema El Qassem spent three hundred and twenty days in hospital. She's now a wheelchair basketballer playing for the Sydney Uni Lions, and she's the first hijab wearing, non verbal player to reach the top of the sport. A good story to sit with for a moment.
