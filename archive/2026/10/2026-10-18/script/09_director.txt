Deep dive number four. The director's chair. And since it's October, let's talk about John Carpenter, and how he made Halloween in nineteen seventy eight.

The budget was around three hundred thousand dollars. Tiny, even by the standards of nineteen seventy eight. It was shot in about twenty days in the spring of nineteen seventy eight, mostly around South Pasadena in California, pretending to be a small town in Illinois in the autumn. If you look carefully, you'll see green trees and even a palm tree or two in a film that's supposed to take place on the last day of October. The crew bought fake autumn leaves, scattered them for a shot, then gathered them up and carried them to the next location to use again.

So how do you make a film this cheap feel this frightening? Carpenter's answer was technique. Let's go through it.

Number one. The opening shot. The film begins with a long, unbroken point of view shot. We're outside a house at night. We walk around it, look through the window, go inside, pick up a knife from the kitchen, climb the stairs, and put on a mask. Then we see the world through the eyeholes of the mask. The camera is the killer. And only at the end does the camera pull back to reveal that the killer is a six year old boy in a clown costume. To get that floating, walking movement, cinematographer Dean Cundey used a brand new kind of camera stabiliser, called the Panaglide, Panavision's answer to the Steadicam. In nineteen seventy eight that was cutting edge. A cheap film using one of the newest tools in the business.

Number two. The widescreen frame. Carpenter shot Halloween in the wide Panavision format, two point three nine to one. Low budget horror films usually didn't. Why bother? Because a wide frame gives you empty space. Space at the edges, in the background, in the dark corners of a room. And Carpenter uses that space to hide Michael Myers. Watch the film again and notice how often he's just standing in the background of a shot. Behind a hedge. Across the street. By the clothes line. Out of focus. Sometimes the character on screen doesn't see him, and sometimes, on a first watch, neither do we. The fear comes from the space in the frame, not from a monster jumping out.

Number three. The mask. Michael's mask is famous, and it was bought from a shop for a couple of dollars. It was a Captain Kirk mask, a mask of William Shatner from Star Trek. The crew spray painted it white, widened the eyeholes, and roughed up the hair. The result is a face that is human, but with all the humanity taken out. Expressionless. Pale. That's the point. Michael is credited in the film not as Michael, but as The Shape.

Number four. The music. Carpenter composed the score himself, reportedly in about three days. The main theme is played on piano in five four time, a rhythm that feels slightly off balance, like something limping toward you. Carpenter has said his father, a music teacher, taught him to play in five four on the bongos when he was a boy. And there's a famous story that when the film was first shown without music, it wasn't scary. With the score, it was terrifying. Music doing half the work.

Number five. Restraint. There's very little blood in Halloween. The violence is mostly suggested, and the camera lingers on the waiting rather than the killing. Carpenter learned from Alfred Hitchcock that suspense is about information. If the audience knows the danger is there, and the character doesn't, every quiet moment becomes unbearable. And it's no accident that Donald Pleasence's character is named Doctor Sam Loomis. Sam Loomis is the name of a character in Hitchcock's Psycho, whose star, Janet Leigh, was the mother of Halloween's star, Jamie Lee Curtis, in her very first film role.

So, if you're picking up a camera, here's what Halloween teaches you. You don't need a budget to make an image frightening. Use the edges of the frame. Use stillness. Use movement that feels like someone watching. Use sound. And never show the audience everything.
