We'll start with the most serious story in federal politics, and I'll be careful with it, because it was still developing late last night.

The independent member for Indi, Helen Haines, was critically injured on Monday afternoon outside her electorate office in Wangaratta, in north east Victoria. Victoria Police believe she was struck by a white Toyota LandCruiser on Murphy Street at around half past three to four in the afternoon. Reports differ slightly on the time. A forty three year old man from Tarrawingee was detained at the scene by a passer-by, and police say a knife was seized from the vehicle. As of Monday evening, no charge had been reported.

Police are investigating whether it was intentional and whether the two knew each other. Acting Superintendent Ashley Mason described it as "an isolated incident at this point in time", with no intelligence of any further threat.

At a press conference on Monday night, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said it was deliberate and "wasn't an accident", though he wouldn't speculate on a motive. He said the joint counter-terrorism team had been activated and that he'd been briefed by the A F P Commissioner, Krissy Barrett. He said there is "no place for political violence in a democracy", called for the temperature of political debate to come down, and described Dr Haines as a "thoroughly decent human being, full of integrity".

Tony Burke noted that reported threats and harassment against high office holders rose from nine hundred and fifty one to one thousand and sixty seven last financial year. There were statements of support from right across the parliament, including Opposition Leader Angus Taylor, Pauline Hanson, the Greens' David Shoebridge and Victorian Premier Ben Carroll. Her family thanked the bystanders who helped and asked for privacy.

Helen Haines has represented Indi since twenty nineteen, and is widely respected across party lines. What to watch today: her condition, whether charges are laid, and a wider conversation about the safety of MPs in their own electorate offices. Our thoughts are with her and her family.

Now to the rest of the federal picture.

The Greens' internal row over Senator Jordon Steele-John has deepened. Leader David Shoebridge is insisting that Steele-John should take leave while a W A Greens inquiry into allegations about his conduct runs its course. Steele-John denies all of the allegations, and he also denies that he ever agreed to step back, saying, quote, "I asked him, more than once, not to announce that I had requested leave." He argues that factional rivals are using the complaints ahead of a preselection for the W A Senate spot left by Dorinda Cox. A Greens spokesperson says Shoebridge's position "in no way prejudges" the inquiry. It's a party that prides itself on process, now arguing in public about what the process actually is. Expect this one to keep running.

On trade, the Australia and European Union free trade deal is still stalled. It was announced in March after eight years of negotiations, but hasn't been ratified. Trade Minister Don Farrell says it's worth ten billion dollars from the start, "quickly rising to thirty billion". The Coalition's treasury spokesman Tim Wilson says they'll oppose the legislation, and meat farmers and steelmakers say they lost out in the negotiations.

On the logic of it: the government is leaning hard on the headline value, but hasn't yet answered the specific complaints from the farm and steel sectors in detail. And the Coalition, which has long championed free trade, is now opposing a free trade deal while some of its own members disagree. Neither side's argument is quite finished yet. Remember yesterday we heard the party room decision has been pushed into next year, so this could drag on.

In defence, the navy's Ghost Shark drone submarines are getting, in the words of Defence Industry Minister Pat Conroy, "teeth". The ABC reports they'll be able to carry Anduril's Altius aerial drones, which can do reconnaissance or carry four kilograms of explosives, and up to twelve sea mines each. They can also drop supplies for special forces. They're on a one point seven billion dollar contract for dozens of the vessels, the first delivered in January, and they're being tested this week off Southern California with the United States and the United Kingdom, as part of AUKUS Pillar Two.

On the economy, the Grattan Institute has proposed lifting the G S T to fifteen per cent, which it says would raise seventy six billion dollars a year. Minister Tanya Plibersek says the government isn't considering it. And an analysis reported by SBS of One Nation's proposed migration cuts suggests they'd mean eight hundred and twenty thousand fewer workers by the mid twenty thirties, and two hundred and seventy five billion dollars more debt over a decade. Economist Saul Eslake says the policy would "eliminate any prospect" of a surplus within ten years. One Nation has argued lower migration would ease pressure on housing and services, so that's the trade-off being debated. Bold policy, but the numbers are going to need an answer.

There's also a cyber security story. Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto says an Israel based company used online advertising auctions to deliver so-called zero-click spyware to millions of devices, and that the targets included the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation, ANSTO. ANSTO says it was not breached, and the Australian Signals Directorate declined to comment.

In South Australia, the Whyalla steelworks story is getting worse. The ABC reports Australia could be without domestic structural and rail steelmaking for up to five years, and about five hundred jobs are going. The state and federal governments have pledged almost two billion dollars towards a new electric arc furnace, with Jindal and M Resources shortlisted to buy the works.

And in the Pacific, Vanuatu has said in a joint statement under its Nakamal Agreement with Australia that it expects China's police presence in the country to fall. The agreement is worth about five hundred million dollars over a decade, and the A F P's presence there could nearly double, although Prime Minister Jotham Napat says he's not aware of any increase.

Now to New South Wales.

The Minns government says it will legislate "in coming weeks" to let MACH Energy's Mount Pleasant coal mine in the Hunter lift its output from twelve and a half million tonnes a year to sixteen and a half, until twenty thirty two. The government says it's about saving four hundred jobs before Christmas. This follows a High Court ruling that the planning commission failed to properly consider Scope Three emissions, the emissions when the coal is burned overseas. The bill would require limits on those emissions and sales only to countries in the Paris Agreement.

Chris Minns says the case is "unique" and that he "did agonise over it". Greens MP Sue Higginson calls it "significant overreach" and a "dangerous precedent", and the community group that won the court case calls it "an inappropriate use" of legislation. The tension here is plain: the government is using parliament to fix a decision a court found was made improperly, which is legal, but it does raise the question of what happens the next time a court finds a project assessment was flawed.

The government also put out a health warning about peptides. Health Minister Ryan Park says one thousand one hundred and eighty emergency department presentations were linked to peptide use between July last year and September this year, rising from about five a month to more than two hundred and seventeen. None of the products are approved in Australia. Chief Health Officer Dr Kerry Chant put it neatly: a professional looking label "is not proof of what is inside".

The Law Enforcement Conduct Commission has begun five weeks of hearings into police conduct at the protest outside Sydney Town Hall on the ninth of February, including the use of pepper spray on an elderly couple. More than eight hundred complaints were made, a record for a single incident. Public hearings with senior officers will follow.

Some other Minns government announcements from the weekend. Deputy Premier Prue Car announced a new funding program for more than seven hundred community preschools, which the government says will create room for over ten thousand more children, fully fund three year old places for the first time, and cap out-of-pocket fees at twenty dollars a day. And Roads Minister Jenny Aitchison says a crackdown on dodgy pink and blue slips has led to a hundred and twenty seven licences being cancelled and eighty four suspended for issuing slips "sight unseen", with thirteen hundred unsafe vehicles taken off the road. That's the government's account, and the detail will matter when the program rolls out.

Locally, Hunter's Hill councillor Marc Lane has resigned, citing what he called a "toxic chamber".

To sport. The second Test against South Africa in Gqeberha has been badly hit by rain. Only twelve overs were bowled on day one, with South Africa two for twenty, and Scott Boland, recalled in place of Nathan Lyon, took wickets with consecutive balls. Day two was delayed by rain last night, but the forecast for the rest of the week is better, so a result isn't out of the question.

The women's T twenty side play New Zealand again tonight at North Sydney Oval, leading the series after that two wicket win. The Diamonds lost the first Constellation Cup game in Auckland, fifty six to fifty. And at the Rugby League World Cup, the Kangaroos are through their opening game with a win over New Zealand, though Kalyn Ponga broke his arm after scoring two tries on debut. Brutal luck.
