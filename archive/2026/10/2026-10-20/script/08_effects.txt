Okay Scott, effects time, and I've been looking forward to this one all morning. Today we're going back to Antarctica, nineteen eighty two, for John Carpenter's The Thing. It has some of the most famous creature effects ever put on film, and not one of them was made in a computer.

If the name Carpenter sounds familiar, that's because we spent Sunday's director deep dive with him and Halloween. Back then he made a horror classic for about three hundred thousand dollars with a cheap mask and a lot of clever camera work. Four years later Universal gave him a proper studio budget, usually reported at around fifteen million dollars, and he spent a very large part of the film's soul on one young man's workshop.

First, the story. The Thing is based on a nineteen thirty eight novella by John W. Campbell called Who Goes There? It had already been filmed once, as The Thing from Another World in nineteen fifty one. Carpenter loved that film, but he went back to the original idea it had mostly dropped. The alien doesn't just attack you. It absorbs you, copies you perfectly, and then walks around wearing your face. So twelve men at an American research station, led by Kurt Russell's helicopter pilot MacReady, slowly realise that any one of them might not be human any more.

That idea created the film's huge effects problem. The creature has no fixed shape. Every time it's revealed, it's halfway through turning into something else. You can't put an actor in a suit for that. Somebody had to invent a new monster for nearly every scene.

That somebody was Rob Bottin. He's generally reported to have been just twenty two when he took the job. Bottin had come up under the makeup legend Rick Baker, and he'd already done the werewolf transformation in Joe Dante's The Howling. On The Thing he led a crew of dozens of artists for well over a year.

He worked himself into the ground. The well known version of the story is that he lived at the studio, barely slept and practically never went home, and when it was all over he was admitted to hospital with exhaustion. Which, honestly, is the most relatable thing anyone has ever done for a monster. I suspect anyone who's spent a few late nights wiping server drives understands the feeling.

Because Bottin was so overloaded, Carpenter brought in another future legend to help, Stan Winston. Winston's team built the dog-thing, the creature that bursts open in the kennel scene early on. Winston reportedly turned down a full screen credit so as not to take the spotlight from Bottin, and he's thanked in the credits instead. A few years later he'd go on to Aliens, Terminator two and Jurassic Park, so you've met his work on this show already.

Now for how they actually did it, because this is the fun bit. The materials were wonderfully low-tech. There was foam latex and rubber for the bodies, and cables, rods and air bladders under the skin so the flesh could ripple and bulge. And then there was slime, and lots of it. Crew members have talked about using things like K Y jelly, food thickeners and gelatine, with some melted or bubbling material on top, so everything looked wet and alive. If a creature looked dry under the lights it looked fake, so everything glistened.

There's real MeatCanyon energy in picturing that workshop. Imagine the poor cleaner arriving on a Monday morning, wondering why the fridge is full of jelly, half a dog and a bucket marked "do not microwave again".

Several of the movements were filmed in reverse. If you want a tentacle to whip out and grab something perfectly, start with the tentacle wrapped around the thing and pull it away. Then run the film backwards. Some of the creature's most unnaturally smooth movements come from that trick, and it works because the creature is supposed to move in ways nothing on Earth should.

The film's most famous scene is the defibrillator. One of the men, Norris, collapses with what looks like a heart attack. The doctor puts the paddles on his chest, and the chest opens up into a giant mouth that bites the doctor's arms off at the forearms.

To pull that off, the production reportedly used a stand-in who had lost both arms. He was fitted with fake forearms built over wax bones and filled with gooey stuff, so the jaws could snap down and tear through them convincingly, with no camera trick at all. Then Norris's head stretches away from his body on long strands of goo, drops to the floor, sprouts spider legs, and scuttles off. One of the men watches and says the line everyone quotes: "You've got to be kidding."

There's a second unforgettable scene, and it needs almost no creature at all: the blood test. MacReady works out that every piece of the Thing is alive and will try to escape if it's attacked. So he ties everyone up, takes a blood sample from each man, and touches each one with a hot wire. One by one, nothing happens, and the tension becomes unbearable. Then one sample shrieks and leaps out of the dish. It's a masterclass in suspense: a lot of waiting, and then a very small thing that moves when it shouldn't.

Here's the twist I find a bit heartbreaking. The Thing opened in the United States in June nineteen eighty two, about two weeks after E T and on the same day as Blade Runner. Audiences that summer wanted a friendly alien who phones home, not one that turns your chest into a mouth. The film underperformed badly, and a lot of critics hated it, calling it disgusting and cold. Carpenter has said for years that its failure really hurt.

But time has been very kind to it. Home video and late-night television found it a new audience, and the reputation kept climbing. Today it's on almost every list of the greatest horror films ever made. Its effects are taught as a benchmark, and its paranoid ending is still argued about. When a prequel came out in twenty eleven and covered a lot of the practical effects with digital work, fans noticed straight away, and they weren't happy. Bottin's slime turned out to be timeless.

My favourite takeaway is that the scariest moments weren't the most expensive ones. It's a petri dish, a hot wire, and a room full of men who don't trust each other.
