Okay, game tech time, and I've been looking forward to this one. Today we're doing ray tracing, properly, from the very beginning. It's a term that gets thrown around on every graphics card box and every spec sheet you've ever read on the walk through the Botanic Garden, Scott. So let's actually work out what it is.

Start with how games have drawn pictures for most of their history. It's called rasterisation. Every object in a game world is made of tiny triangles. Rasterisation takes each of those triangles, works out where it lands on your screen, and fills in the pixels it covers. Then it colours those pixels using rules about where the lights are.

Here's an analogy. Rasterisation is like painting a scene by holding up a window frame and tracing the shapes you see onto the glass, one object at a time. It's incredibly fast, and graphics cards have been built for decades to do exactly that. But there's a catch. When you're painting one triangle, you don't really know about all the other triangles. The floor doesn't automatically know the lamp above it should cast a shadow, or that a red wall next to it should give it a faint red glow. So for years, developers faked all of that with clever tricks: shadow maps, baked lighting, reflection probes, screen space reflections. Some of those tricks are brilliant. But they're tricks, and you can catch them out. Ever noticed a reflection in a puddle vanish when the object it's reflecting goes off the edge of the screen? That's a screen space trick being found out.

Now, ray tracing flips the whole thing around. Instead of asking "where does this triangle land on the screen?", it asks "for this pixel, what would my eye actually see?"

It works like this. For every pixel, the game fires an imaginary line, a ray, out from the camera into the world. The ray travels until it hits something. Say it hits a stone wall. Now the game asks questions from that point. Is there a light that can see this spot? Fire another ray towards the light. If something's in the way, this spot is in shadow. A real shadow, with no faking. Is the surface shiny? Fire a ray off at the mirror angle and see what it hits. That's your reflection, and it works even for things that are off screen, or behind you.

And then there are the bounces. In real life, light doesn't just come from the lamp. It hits the floor, bounces up onto the ceiling, picks up a bit of colour from a red couch, and fills the room with that soft, warm glow. That's called global illumination, and it's the thing that makes a room feel real rather than like a stage set. Ray tracing can follow those bounces. One bounce, two bounces, more.

Which brings us to the dark bit, because I can't help myself. Spare a thought for the poor little ray of light that gets fired into a hall of mirrors. It bounces. And bounces. And bounces. Every bounce it gets a little dimmer, a little more tired, and somewhere in the code a developer has written a line that says, after four bounces, just stop. And so it stops. Mid-air. Forever. Never reaching the eye it was born to reach. Sleep well.

So why don't all games just do this? Because it's ferociously expensive. A four K screen has about eight point three million pixels. Fire even one ray per pixel, plus shadow rays, plus reflection rays, plus bounces, sixty times a second, and you're into the hundreds of millions, or billions, of rays every second. And every single ray has to work out which of the millions of triangles in the scene it hits first.

That's the real problem. Not drawing, but searching. And the fix is a lovely idea called a B V H, a bounding volume hierarchy.

Here's the analogy. Imagine you're looking for one particular sock in a huge house. You don't check every drawer in every room. You ask: is it upstairs or downstairs? Upstairs. Which bedroom? This one. Which cupboard? That one. Which drawer? Got it. A B V H does that with the game world. It wraps the whole scene in big invisible boxes, then smaller boxes inside those, then smaller boxes inside those, all the way down to the triangles. A ray checks the big box first. If it misses, it skips everything inside it in one go. So instead of testing millions of triangles, a ray might only test a few dozen boxes.

That's exactly what the R T cores in Nvidia's R T X cards were added to do, starting in twenty eighteen. They're dedicated hardware for the two jobs ray tracing does over and over: walking through that box hierarchy, and testing whether a ray hits a triangle. A M D and Intel's Arc cards have their own ray tracing hardware too. Speaking of which, I know how you light up when someone says "dedicated hardware", Scott. Very attractive quality in a man, a deep appreciation for specialised silicon.

Even with special hardware, games can't afford many rays per pixel. Maybe one, maybe less. And with that few rays, the image comes out noisy, speckled, like an old photo taken in a dark room. So the next step is denoising. Clever filters, these days often trained A I models, look at that speckly image, plus information about the surfaces and what the previous frames looked like, and smooth it into something clean. A huge amount of what you think of as "ray tracing" is actually really good denoising.

Then there's the deep end: path tracing. That's ray tracing everything, all the lighting, all the shadows, all the bounces, with no rasterised fakery underneath. It's how Pixar renders films, except Pixar can spend hours on a single frame. Games started doing it in real time in the last few years. Portal with R T X, from twenty twenty two, rebuilt the original Portal with full path tracing. And Cyberpunk twenty seventy seven got its Overdrive mode in twenty twenty three, which path traces Night City. If you've seen the neon in a puddle on a wet street in that mode, that's the real thing, with light bouncing exactly where it should.

And the reason any of that is playable at all is upscaling and frame generation. D L S S renders the game at a lower resolution, so far fewer rays, and then an A I model rebuilds it to look like full resolution. Frame generation goes further and creates in-between frames. Nvidia's latest D L S S model update this month reportedly even cuts four K video memory use by about twenty per cent, according to Tom's Hardware. For a man who checks V RAM numbers the way other people check the footy scores, that's a nice bit of news.

So, the short version. Rasterisation paints triangles fast and fakes the light. Ray tracing follows the light, and gets shadows, reflections and bounces right. A B V H makes it possible, R T cores make it fast, denoising makes it clean, and upscalers make it playable.

Now, your Godot game. Godot's two D lighting isn't ray traced. But it gives you something that looks and feels a lot like it, very cheaply, and it's perfect for a Secret of Mana meets Stardew Valley world.

Here's the tip. Add a PointLight2D for each light source, like a torch on a dungeon wall, or a lamp glowing in a farmhouse window at night. Give it a soft round gradient texture. Turn on shadows in the light's settings. Then, on the things that should block light, like walls, pillars, the edge of a cliff, add a LightOccluder2D with a simple polygon. Godot casts the shadows from the light, past the occluder, almost like a single ray from the light to every pixel. You can even draw occluders right into your tilemap's tiles, so every wall tile casts shadows automatically.

For night time, add a CanvasModulate node and set it to a deep blue-grey. That darkens the whole scene, and your lights punch through it. Sunset to night becomes a simple colour change over time.

Two practical cautions. Keep those occluder polygons simple, four to six points is usually plenty, because every extra point is extra work for every light. And don't put shadows on every single light. Give them to the hero lights, the torch by the dungeon door, the lantern on the porch, and let the little fireflies glow without them.

Try it on one room tonight. Put one torch in a stone corridor with a couple of pillars and watch the shadows swing as your character walks past. It's that moment where the game suddenly feels like a place. And it's not ray traced. It's just very good at pretending.
