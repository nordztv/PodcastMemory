[happy] Now, it's Wednesday, and that means it's time for the story behind this week's retro game: The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past.

It came out in Japan in November nineteen ninety one, in North America in April ninety two, and in Europe that September. It was made by Nintendo's E A D team, directed by Takashi Tezuka, produced by Shigeru Miyamoto, with music by Koji Kondo. And it shipped on an eight megabit cartridge. That's one megabyte. Your phone takes photos bigger than the entire game. At the time, though, that was a big cart. Most Super Nintendo games of the era were half that size.

What's clever is how much they squeezed out of that megabyte. The headline trick is the Dark World. Almost every place in the Light World has a twisted twin in the Dark World, with a similar layout but different, corrupted art, enemies and secrets. Link moves between them with the Magic Mirror. That design is beautiful on its own, because once you learn one map, the other becomes a puzzle: where does this cliff lead on the other side? But it's also efficient. Two worlds sharing one structure is a brilliant way to double your game without doubling your cartridge.

Then there's how the Super Nintendo draws it. The S N E S builds its pictures out of layers: background layers made of tiles, which are little eight by eight pixel squares, and sprites, the moving objects like Link and the enemies, drawn on top. A Link to the Past uses those layers for depth. That's how you get bridges you can walk under, upper floors in dungeons, and the way some rooms let you drop down a level. It's all layers stacked like sheets of glass, each one scrolling and drawing on its own. The world map, the one that zooms and tilts, is widely credited to the console's famous Mode seven scaling trick.

Then there's Koji Kondo's music, made for the Super Nintendo's Sony-designed sound chip, which plays back short recorded samples of instruments rather than pure electronic tones. That's why the Hyrule Castle theme and the Dark World music sound so orchestral for the time. It's still one of the best soundtracks of the era.

And the legacy is enormous. It sold over four and a half million copies on the Super Nintendo. It set the template for nearly every top-down Zelda that followed, and for a whole genre of top-down action adventures. The item-gated world, the dungeon that teaches you its own tool, the overworld that opens up as you collect things. You'll see its fingerprints on Secret of Mana, on countless indies, and, yes, on your own game. If you want to keep playing, it's in the Nintendo Switch Online Super Nintendo library.
