[happy] Okay, lore time. And I've been looking forward to this one, because today we're going to the Continent. The making of The Witcher three: Wild Hunt.

Let's start with where it came from, because the Witcher didn't begin as a game. It began as short stories and novels by the {Polish|Poh lish} writer Andrzej Sapkowski, from the mid nineteen eighties onwards. Geralt of Rivia is a witcher: a monster hunter for hire, mutated as a child into something not quite human, with cat-like eyes, slowed emotions, and a very dry sense of humour. The books are full of Slavic folklore, and they're surprisingly political. The monsters are often the least monstrous thing in the room.

CD Projekt, the company, started out in Warsaw in the early nineties, distributing and translating games in Poland. Its development studio, CD Projekt Red, made the first Witcher game in two thousand seven, and The Witcher two in twenty eleven. Both were well liked, but they were cult hits, a bit rough and a bit niche. The third one was the big swing. The plan was simple to say and almost impossible to do: an open world, as large and alive as the biggest Western R P Gs, but with the writing quality of a hand-crafted story game.

The game director was Konrad Tomaszkiewicz, and the team built it on a new version of its own engine, REDengine three. Development took several years, and the team grew to hundreds of people. And the story they settled on was personal. Not saving the world first, but finding a girl. Ciri, Geralt's adopted daughter, his "child surprise" from the books, is being hunted by the Wild Hunt, a host of spectral riders from another world, led by their king, Eredin. Geralt spends the game following her trail across a war-torn land, always a few steps behind.

[happy] That structure is the secret of the whole thing. Every region is a chase. And because you're always arriving after Ciri has left, every place you go is full of people who met her, and each of them has their own problem. Which means the side quests aren't filler. They're how you hear about her.

The regions are wonderful. Velen, also called No Man's Land, is a swampy, war-ravaged countryside of hanged men and hungry villages. Novigrad is a huge free city, rich, crowded and paranoid, where the church is burning mages at the stake. And Skellige is a chain of windswept islands full of Norse-flavoured clans, longships and feuding jarls. Then there's Kaer Morhen, the crumbling witcher keep in the mountains, where Geralt grew up.

And the quest everyone remembers is the Bloody Baron. You arrive in Velen looking for Ciri and find a warlord who drinks too much, whose wife and daughter have vanished. Over a few hours, the quest pulls apart a family. You learn about the violence in that house. You meet a creature born of a miscarried, unwanted child, a botchling, and you decide whether to lift its curse or kill it. And the endings are messy, because you only find out later what your choices actually did. There's no clean, good option. That quest became the example people used when they talked about what game writing could be.

The game was announced in early twenty thirteen and shown off that summer, and the footage at the time looked spectacular. Then the release date slipped, from late twenty fourteen, to February twenty fifteen, and then to May. When it finally came out on the nineteenth of May, twenty fifteen, some players compared it to those early trailers and argued the graphics had been "downgraded", with less dense lighting and foliage. The studio talked about having to choose a rendering approach that would work across a world that size. The argument faded pretty quickly, though, because the game itself was so good.

[happy] It won Game of the Year at The Game Awards in twenty fifteen, and piles of other awards. Then came the two expansions. Hearts of Stone, in October twenty fifteen, is a tight, dark fairy tale about a man who made a deal with a very polite and very terrifying being called Gaunter O'Dimm. And Blood and Wine, in twenty sixteen, took Geralt to Toussaint, a sunny, wine-soaked, Arthurian-flavoured duchy, and gave him a vineyard to retire to. Many people still say Blood and Wine is better than most full games.

The music deserves a mention too. Composer Marcin Przybyłowicz worked with the {Polish|Poh lish} folk band Percival to give the game that raw, Slavic sound, with old instruments and haunting vocals. "The Fields of Ard Skellig" is the one you've probably heard.

And then there's Gwent. Inside this grim, adult fantasy game, the developers put a card game you could play in taverns. It was meant as a little diversion. People became obsessed with it. Entire playthroughs were derailed by Geralt, the legendary monster hunter, wandering from inn to inn trying to win rare cards off innkeepers while the fate of the world waited. [punch] Ciri could wait. The Gwent deck could not.

[dark] I do think about the poor innkeepers. Every inn on the Continent, a strange white-haired man comes in. Covered in blood. Smelling of swamp and drowner guts. Doesn't order food. Just sits down at your table and quietly says, "Gwent?" [beat] And you say yes. [punch] Because the last innkeeper who said no is in the well.

What does it all add up to? The Witcher three sold over fifty million copies, a remarkable number for a single-player R P G. It turned CD Projekt Red from a well-liked studio into one of the biggest names in games. It inspired the Netflix series. And it raised the bar for side quests across the industry. After Witcher three, a "go and kill ten wolves" quest suddenly felt like an insult.

It also set up the next chapter of that studio's story, which we'll get to one day: the troubled launch of Cyberpunk twenty seventy seven, and its comeback. And, of course, the current one. As we heard earlier, a new Witcher three expansion is planned for next year, it's still getting patches eleven years after launch, and The Witcher four is in the works, with Ciri now taking the lead. Which feels right. We spent the whole of the third game chasing her. Now she gets to be the one we follow.
