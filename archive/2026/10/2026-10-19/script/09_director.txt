Our director deep dive this week is perfect for Halloween month. Stanley Kubrick, and how he shot The Shining, from nineteen eighty.

The film is based on Stephen King's novel. Jack Nicholson plays Jack Torrance, a writer who takes a job as the winter caretaker of the Overlook Hotel, high in the mountains, with his wife Wendy, played by Shelley Duvall, and their young son Danny. They're snowed in. The hotel has other ideas.

The thing that makes The Shining feel so unsettling is the way the camera moves. And that's down to a new invention. A few years earlier, a cameraman called Garrett Brown had invented the Steadicam, a harness with an arm and a balanced rig that let an operator walk or run with the camera and get smooth, floating shots without laying down tracks. Kubrick was fascinated by it, and Garrett Brown himself operated it on The Shining.

The most famous shots are of Danny riding his big wheel tricycle through the hotel corridors. The camera follows close behind him, just above the floor, gliding smoothly. You hear the wheels go loud on the wooden floor, then soft over the rugs, then loud again. That sound pattern is pure tension. You know something's around the next corner. To get that low, the Steadicam was rigged close to the ground, and Brown has described riding along in a modified wheelchair for some of those shots so he could keep up.

Those floating, gliding shots give the film its mood. Normal handheld camerawork feels human and nervous. Tracks feel mechanical. The Steadicam feels like something else, like the hotel itself is watching and following. That's exactly what Kubrick wanted.

He was also obsessive about the space. The hotel interiors were built as huge sets at Elstree Studios near London, and the layout of the Overlook doesn't really make sense if you try to map it. Windows appear where there shouldn't be windows, and corridors lead to places they can't. Viewers often can't say why, but they feel lost. The hedge maze, too, was built on a soundstage.

Then there's composition. Kubrick loved one point perspective. That's where the lines of the walls, floor and ceiling all meet at a single point in the middle of the frame, with everything balanced and symmetrical. It looks calm and ordered, and that's what makes it creepy. The Grady twins standing perfectly still at the end of a corridor. A perfect, symmetrical frame, with something deeply wrong in the middle of it.

The lighting, by cinematographer John Alcott, often comes from the set itself. The lamps, the windows and lights built into the ceiling. That made the hotel feel bright and real, and it gave the Steadicam freedom to go anywhere without lights getting in the way. A horror film that's bright, not dark. It's another thing that throws you off balance.

And Kubrick was famous for doing many, many takes. It's often said some scenes took dozens, even more than a hundred, takes. The process was exhausting for the cast, particularly Shelley Duvall, and it's something people still talk about today. The result is a film that feels like nothing else.

Now, here's your practical lesson, for someone learning filmmaking. Camera movement should mean something. Before you move the camera, ask: whose point of view is this? Is it a person, or something watching? A smooth glide that follows a character from behind makes the audience feel like they're being led, or hunted. You don't need a Steadicam for this. A gimbal, or even careful walking with the X M five held low and close to your body, will do it.

So here's a little homework for the walk. When you come up out of St James and head into Hyde Park, look for one point perspective. That long avenue of trees, with the paths running into the distance. Stand in the dead centre, and frame it so everything meets in the middle. Perfectly symmetrical, perfectly calm. Just, if a pair of twins appears at the far end, walk the other way.
