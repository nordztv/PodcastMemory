=== 7. GAME TECH ===

Game tech, and today it's practical first, because I want your radish monster to be able to find you. Right now, a monster that runs straight at the player gets stuck on the first fence post. What you want is pathfinding, and the classic way to do it is called A-star.

Here's how to do it in Godot four, two ways.

Option one, if your farm is built on a grid of tiles, which it is: use the A-star grid two D class. You create one in code, tell it the size of your map and the size of a tile, and call its update function. Then, for every tile the monster can't walk through, a fence, a tree, a well, the farmhouse, you mark that cell as solid. When the radish monster wants to reach you, you ask the grid for a path from its tile to your tile, and it hands you back a list of cells. The monster walks to the first cell, then the next, then the next. You can also choose whether it's allowed to move diagonally, which changes how sneaky it looks.

Option two is Godot's navigation system: give your tile map layer some navigation shapes, and give the monster a navigation agent two D node. You set the agent's target position to the player, and every frame you ask it for the next position to walk towards. This one's smoother and handles monsters bumping around each other, but there's a bit more to set up, and the docs still mark the agent as experimental.

For a top-down farm, I'd start with the grid. It's simple, it's fast, and you can see exactly what's happening.

Three tips that will save you a weekend. One: don't recalculate the path every single frame. Do it every quarter of a second or so, or when the player moves to a new tile. Two: give the monster a leash. If you get more than, say, ten tiles away, it gives up and wanders back to the field it came from, grumbling. Three: draw the path while you're testing. A little line of dots from radish to player will show you every bug instantly.

Now, how does A-star actually work? Imagine you're walking from Green Square to work, and you don't know Sydney at all. You stand on the corner and look at the streets next to you. For each one, you think about two things: how far you've already walked to get there, and roughly how far it still is to work, as the crow flies. Add those together, and walk towards the corner with the smallest total. Then repeat from there.

That's A-star. The distance you've already walked is the cost so far. The rough guess of what's left is called the heuristic. The magic is in combining them. If you only looked at the distance walked, you'd spread out in every direction like a puddle, slowly checking every street in the city. If you only looked at the straight-line guess, you'd charge towards the office and walk into the harbour. Together, A-star heads towards the goal, but it's willing to go around the Botanic Garden fence when it has to.

As long as your guess never overestimates the real distance, A-star is guaranteed to find the shortest path. On a grid where you can only move up, down, left and right, the usual guess is to add the horizontal and vertical distances together; with diagonals allowed, you use a slightly different formula, and the Godot class lets you pick.

You can also make some tiles more expensive instead of solid. Mud, tall crops, a shallow stream. The radish monster can wade through your carrots, but it costs more, so it'll go around if there's an easy path.

Which raises a question about your radish monster. When it paths through the carrot patch, does it know them? Are they cousins? Does it slow down out of respect, or speed up out of guilt?

Either way, by Monday it should be able to chase you round the farmhouse properly. And if it ever gets stuck on a fence post, that's not a bug. That's a radish having a bad day.


=== 8. EFFECTS ===

Effects! Today it's the shot that every action film copied for about five years afterwards. The Matrix, 1999. Neo on the rooftop, leaning back as the bullets crawl past him, and the camera swinging round him in what looks like frozen time. They called it bullet time.

What makes it special is that the camera appears to move at normal speed while the action is almost stopped. That's impossible with one camera. To move a camera that fast around an actor, you'd need a rig travelling at a ridiculous speed, and even then you couldn't slow the actor down. So they cheated, beautifully.

The visual effects supervisor, John Gaeta, and the team at Manex built a curved row of still cameras, about a hundred and twenty of them, arranged in an arc around Keanu Reeves, who was hanging on wires in front of a green screen. There was a film camera at each end of the arc. A computer fired the still cameras in sequence, a tiny fraction of a second apart, or all at once if they wanted time completely frozen. Each still camera captures one frame from a slightly different angle. Play those frames one after another, and it looks like a single camera flying round him.

Of course, a hundred and twenty frames from a hundred and twenty cameras won't line up perfectly. So the team used computers to interpolate, to invent the in-between frames and smooth out the motion, which was cutting edge at the time. The bullets, their ripples in the air, and the rooftop behind him were all added afterwards. The background was built from photographs of the real location, turned into a virtual set so the camera could swing through it.

The idea wasn't completely new. In the eighteen seventies, Eadweard Muybridge set up a row of cameras along a racetrack, triggered by a galloping horse, to prove that all four hooves leave the ground at once. Artists had played with time-slice photography for years, and the Wachowskis were inspired by anime and comic books, where a single frozen moment can be held and studied. What The Matrix did was put all of that into one moment of story, and make it mean something: Neo is starting to bend the rules of the system.

It worked. The Matrix won the Academy Award for visual effects, beating, of all things, Star Wars Episode one. And then bullet time was everywhere: in adverts, in parodies, in Shrek, and in games. Max Payne made it a button you could press. Which is what games do best: someone does something amazing in a film, and a game says, wonderful, now you do it, as many times as you want.

Spare a thought for camera number sixty three, though. A hundred and twenty cameras, and it's the one in the middle. Every take, it fires exactly when it's told, and nobody ever mentions it. The cameras at the ends get credited on the making-of. Sixty three just gets dusted.

Now, here's today's question, and it comes straight from this. If you could add one bullet time moment to your game, what would it be? The instant the radish monster bursts out of the soil? The cannon launch? My answer: a slow-motion shot of you watering your crops, camera swinging round, dramatic music, a single drop of water hanging in the air. Absolutely nothing happens. It's just very important watering. Your answer space is on the answer page.


=== 9. DIRECTOR ===

Our director today is Akira Kurosawa, and the film is Seven Samurai, from 1954. If you've ever enjoyed a film where someone assembles a team, a heist crew, a band of misfits, a party of heroes in a Final Fantasy game, you've enjoyed something this film helped invent.

The story is simple. A poor farming village knows bandits will return after the harvest. They can't fight, so they go to town to hire samurai, and all they can offer is rice. Takashi Shimura plays Kambei, the calm, greying veteran who agrees to lead them. Toshiro Mifune plays Kikuchiyo, a wild, loud man pretending to be a samurai, who turns out to be the heart of the film. Seven men. Forty bandits. One village.

It was a huge production for Japan at the time. The shoot ran for about a year, well over budget, and the film runs nearly three and a half hours. The studio, Toho, was nervous more than once. What came out of all that time is one of the most influential action films ever made.

Let's talk about how he shot it, because there are three things you can steal.

The first is multiple cameras with long lenses. For the action scenes, Kurosawa ran several cameras at once, often with telephoto lenses placed far from the actors. That does two things. The actors can't tell which camera is on them, so they stop performing for it and just fight, run and fall. And the long lens squashes the distance, so riders, rain and mud all stack up into one dense, chaotic picture. Then in the edit he had several angles of the same moment, so he could cut with real rhythm.

The second is weather. The final battle takes place in pouring rain and deep mud, shot in the cold. You feel the exhaustion. The horses slip. The swords look heavy. Kurosawa used weather as emotion his whole career: wind, rain, fog, heat. It's not decoration; it's telling you how the characters feel.

The third is clarity. A battle with forty bandits and dozens of villagers could easily be a mess. Kurosawa makes it easy to follow. Kambei draws a map of the village, and keeps a scroll with circles for each bandit, crossing one out every time one falls. So during all the chaos, you always know the score, where the fight is, and what's at stake. He also gives each samurai one clear trait, so you know who's who even in the mud.

The film's legacy is enormous. Hollywood remade it as The Magnificent Seven in 1960. The idea of gathering a team, each with a skill, then a big siege at the end, has been reused in westerns, science fiction, animation and games ever since.

Here's your practical lesson for the X-M5. Next time you film something with movement, a friend on a bike, a dog at the park, your son toddling across the grass, try it from far away with your longest lens, rather than up close with a wide one. Shoot it twice, from two different spots. You'll get that compressed, layered Kurosawa look, the subject won't feel watched, and in Final Cut you'll have two angles to cut between. And if the weather turns, don't run inside straight away. Ten seconds of rain on the lens might be the most emotional shot of the day.

Also, the most Kurosawa thing about Seven Samurai is the deal itself. Seven professionals risk their lives for a village, and the payment is rice. Offer you a twelve terabyte drive and I think you'd defend a village too.


=== 10. WORKSHOP ===

Workshop time, and since you told me your dream job for a local A I is a voice, let's make that a weekend project for the home server. A small text to speech engine, running on your own hardware, no cloud.

The good news is you don't need the big graphics card for this one. Small voice models are tiny. Kokoro, the one I'm built on, is about eighty million parameters, and the new one I mentioned, Phonon, is even smaller. Those can run on an ordinary processor. A Dell T330 can read a paragraph aloud without breaking a sweat. It'll just take a few seconds longer than a G P U would.

Here's the simplest way in. On TrueNAS, the easiest route is a container. There are community-made Docker containers that wrap Kokoro in a simple web page or a small A P I: you type text, it gives you audio. Run one of those as a custom app, give it a couple of processor cores, and you've got your own voice service on your network. Check the project's page before you install it, and pick one that's maintained.

Then the fun part: voices. Most of these models come with a set of voices, and some let you blend them. My own voice, if you're wondering, is a mix: seventy per cent of one voice and thirty per cent of another, played a little slower than normal. That's the trick to making a voice sound warmer and more like a person. Try blending two voices you like, and try a speed of about nine tenths. Faster sounds like an advert. Slower sounds calmer.

The other trick is in the writing, not the model. Short sentences. Commas where you'd breathe. Numbers written the way you'd say them. A model reading a wall of text sounds robotic; the same model reading something written to be spoken sounds human.

And once it's running, you can plug it into the rest of your setup. Your Raspberry Pi channel could have a station voice announcing what's up next. Your server could tell you when a drive scrub has finished. Ninety six terabytes, all checked, every bit present and correct, announced in a soothing voice at eleven at night. Romance isn't dead. It's just running on TrueNAS.
