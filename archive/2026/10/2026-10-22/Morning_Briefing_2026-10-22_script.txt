==== GAME TECH ====

Game tech. And today, the most famous graphics trick of the Super Nintendo: Mode seven. We mentioned it with the A Link to the Past world map yesterday, so let's open the box. And I'll start with the practical bit, because you can fake it in your own game this weekend.

Here's the Godot version, in one minute. In Godot four, the easiest way to get a Mode seven look is to cheat with three D. Put your big map image on a flat plane, a MeshInstance three D with a plane mesh. Turn the texture filter to nearest, so the pixels stay crisp. Tilt a Camera three D down at the plane, low and at an angle. Then move and rotate the camera, not the ground. That's it. You get the classic swooping, tilting world map. If you want your characters to stay pixel art, use Sprite three D nodes with billboard turned on, so they always face the camera.

If you'd rather stay purely in two D, the other way is a shader on a TextureRect: for each row of pixels below the horizon, you work out how far away that row is, divide by that distance, rotate the coordinates, and sample your map texture there. Rows near the horizon get squashed and tiny, rows near the bottom stay big. It's a few lines of shader code, and it's lovely for a flying sequence or a world map screen.

Now, how the real thing worked.

The Super Nintendo draws its picture in layers, as we said yesterday. Normally those background layers can only scroll: slide left, right, up, down. Mode seven is a special mode where one background layer gets superpowers. It can be rotated and scaled. Spun round, zoomed in, zoomed out. Under the hood, the console applies a little bit of maths, a matrix, to every pixel, which tells it where in the big background map that pixel should come from.

That on its own gives you a flat, spinning, zooming map. Pretty, but flat. Think of a photo on a turntable under a magnifying glass.

The magic is the next step. The Super Nintendo draws the screen one line at a time, from top to bottom. And it has a feature called H D M A, which lets it quietly change settings between lines, while the picture is being drawn. So programmers changed the zoom on every single line. Lines near the top of the ground are zoomed far out, so they look distant and tiny. Lines near the bottom are zoomed in, so they look close and big. And your eye reads that as perspective. A flat floor, stretching away to a horizon.

That's F-Zero. That's Super Mario Kart. That's Pilotwings. It's not three D at all. It's a flat picture being re-zoomed two hundred and twenty four times every frame. The Super Nintendo didn't render a world. It just lied very convincingly, sixty times a second.

There were limits. Mode seven only works on one background layer, so the floor is all you get. Everything standing on it, the karts, the characters, the trees, has to be sprites drawn on top, which is why the pipes in Mario Kart always look a bit like cardboard cutouts. And the map itself had to fit in a fixed size: a grid of a hundred and twenty eight by a hundred and twenty eight tiles, so a thousand and twenty four pixels square.

Clever designers used it everywhere once they understood it. The Final Fantasy six airship world map. The tilting platform over the lava in some of Super Mario World's castle fights, which is really a background layer being rotated. The swooping world maps in Secret of Mana when you fly on Flammie. That one's especially relevant to you, because it's exactly the effect your game would want for a travel or flying sequence.

So, a practical idea for your game. Your farm and your world are top-down. But if, later, the player unlocks a way to travel, a giant bird, a flying radish, whatever you like, that's the moment for a Mode seven style map. Build your normal world map as one big image, drop it on a plane in a little three D scene, and fly a tilted camera over it. A tiny bit of work for a big "oh wow" moment. And it's straight out of the Secret of Mana playbook.

==== EFFECTS ====

Effects. And today, the films that taught a generation that the best miniatures aren't miniature at all. The Lord of the Rings, and its bigatures.

When Peter Jackson made the trilogy in New Zealand, around the turn of the millennium, digital effects were getting good. Gollum alone proves that. But for the great cities and fortresses, Minas Tirith, Helm's Deep, Barad-dûr, Rivendell, Isengard, the team at Weta Workshop, led by Richard Taylor, made a bold choice. They built them. Physically. As models.

Except these weren't little tabletop models. They were enormous. So big that the crew started calling them "bigatures". Some were bigger than a room, and some had to be shot in a car park or a warehouse just to fit. Minas Tirith, the white city carved into the mountain, was built as a huge, tiered model packed with tiny buildings, streets and walls, detailed enough for a camera to fly right down into it.

Why go physical? Because real objects behave like real objects. Light bounces off a real wall exactly the way light bounces off a wall. Shadows fall correctly. Tiny chips and dirt and weathering catch the light in a way that, back then, computers struggled to fake. Put a real camera close to a real model, and your brain believes it. The digital work, the armies, the sky, the moving figures, was then added on top, so you get the best of both.

Shooting them was its own art. A miniatures unit, with the cinematographer Alex Funke, worked for years on these models, using motion control. That's a computer-driven camera rig that can repeat the exact same move again and again, to the millimetre. So they could shoot the city once with one lighting setup, then again with smoke, then again for a different pass, and every pass lines up perfectly. Then the effects artists stacked them all together.

Then there's the other half of the trick, and it's my favourite. Forced perspective. How do you make Ian McKellen, who's quite a tall man, look like he's towering over Elijah Wood as Frodo, when they're sitting side by side on a cart?

You cheat with distance. Things further from the camera look smaller. So you put Gandalf close to the camera and Frodo further away, and line them up so they look like they're next to each other. Old trick. As old as cinema.

The problem: the moment the camera moves, the trick breaks, because the two actors shift relative to each other. So the Lord of the Rings team built something new. For the cart scene in Fellowship, the cart was actually built as two separate pieces, at different distances from the camera, with Gandalf on the near piece and Frodo on the far piece. And when the camera moved, a motion control rig moved one half of the set to keep the illusion perfectly lined up. A moving forced perspective shot. That's why those early Shire scenes feel so natural, even though you're watching an optical illusion.

They also used scale doubles, smaller or taller stand-ins for wide shots, built sets at two different scales, like Bag End, one where the hobbits fit and one where Gandalf bangs his head on the beams, and plenty of kneeling, low tables and oversized props. Hundreds of little cheats, all working together.

Forced perspective is a lovely trick until you think about it too long. A whole film where everyone is standing exactly where the camera tells them. Hobbits who must never, ever step forward. Somewhere in that Shire, there's a hobbit who took one step towards the lens on take forty seven. And now he's a giant, and no one's allowed to talk about it.

And it worked. All three films won the Oscar for visual effects. And the bigatures have aged beautifully, in a way some purely digital effects from the same era haven't. When the trilogy was remastered for four K, those models still hold up, because they're real. Your four K discs have the proof.

There's also a lesson there that applies to your own filmmaking, even with just a camera and a desk. If you want something to look big, shoot it low and wide, close to the lens, with slow movement and a bit of haze or depth in the background. That's how a model city becomes a mountain city, and it's how your Pi in a little case could look like a broadcast tower in a product shot.

==== DIRECTOR ====

Director. And today, a film that's on a lot of lists of the greatest crime films ever made, and that started one of the great careers in modern cinema. Bong Joon-ho's Memories of Murder, from two thousand and three.

Before Parasite won Best Picture, before The Host and Snowpiercer, Bong was a young director whose first feature had flopped. Memories of Murder was his second, and it made him.

It's based on a real case, the Hwaseong serial murders in South Korea, between nineteen eighty six and nineteen ninety one, which went unsolved for decades. Bong adapted it from a stage play, and the film follows two detectives who simply cannot catch the killer. Song Kang-ho plays Park Doo-man, a local cop who trusts his gut, beats confessions out of suspects, and thinks he can tell a criminal by looking into his eyes. Kim Sang-kyung plays Seo Tae-yoon, a detective from Seoul who trusts paperwork and evidence. Over the film, those two men slowly swap places, and both of them fail.

How did Bong shoot it? He's famous for planning everything in storyboards, shot by shot, long before filming. That's one reason his films feel so controlled, even when the scene on screen is chaos.

And one of the best examples is the first crime scene. A body has been found in a ditch beside a rice field. And instead of cutting it up into lots of little shots, Bong and his cinematographer, Kim Hyung-koo, follow Detective Park in one long, roving take, as everything goes wrong around him. Kids are running about. Reporters are wandering through. Locals are gawping. Someone slides down the bank into the ditch. And then a farm tractor drives straight over the one footprint that might have been evidence. Because it's one continuous shot, you can't escape the feeling that this investigation is doomed from the very first minute. The camera doesn't tell you the police are hopeless. It shows you, in real time, with no cuts to hide behind.

The look of the film matters too. It's set in the countryside in the late eighties, so it's full of golden rice fields, dusty roads and rain. The palette is earthy and slightly washed out, like an old photograph. And rain becomes a motif: the killer seems to strike on rainy nights, so whenever the sky darkens in this film, your stomach tightens. Bong uses weather the way other directors use music.

And then there's the tone. Memories of Murder is genuinely funny in places. The detectives are clumsy and petty and ridiculous. And then, without warning, it's devastating. Bong is a master of that switch, and you can see it carried all the way through to Parasite. The comedy makes you lower your guard, and then the film hits you.

The ending is one of the most famous in Korean cinema. Years later, Park, now out of the police, stops by that same ditch. A girl tells him another man recently stopped there to look, and said he'd done something there long ago. Park asks what he looked like. "Just ordinary," she says. And Park turns, and looks straight into the camera. Bong has said he wanted that look aimed at the real killer, who was still out there and might one day sit in a cinema and watch the film. In twenty nineteen, sixteen years after the film came out, police finally identified the real killer through D N A evidence. The real case's victims are the reason the film is so careful, and so angry.

So, the lesson for you as someone learning filmmaking. Block in depth, and let one shot do the work. Instead of cutting between a person and the thing they're reacting to, put both in the same frame, one near the camera and one far away, and let the action happen across the space between them. On a travel video, that might be you or a friend in the foreground, with a market, a crowd, or a street unfolding behind, all in one take that the camera follows. It's harder to plan, but it feels alive, because nothing is cheated. And when you do want an audience to feel something, a single, direct look into the lens, used once, is about the most powerful tool a filmmaker has. Use it once. Never twice.

==== WORKSHOP ====

Workshop. And since today is going to be wet, let's make the rain useful. How to film rain so it actually looks like rain.

Rain is surprisingly hard to see on camera. You stand in a downpour, film it, and play it back, and it looks like a slightly damp afternoon. There are three tricks that fix that.

First, light it from behind. Raindrops are almost invisible when the light is behind you. Turn round, so the light is coming towards the camera, a streetlight, a shop window, a bright patch of sky, and suddenly every drop catches the light and shows up. Every film you've ever seen with gorgeous rain is lit from behind.

Second, a dark background. Rain shows up against shadows, not against a grey sky. So frame the rain in front of a dark doorway, a hedge, the trees in the Botanic Garden, or the inside of the station entrance.

Third, shutter speed. This is where your X M five will matter, once it's actually in your hands. The normal rule for video is a shutter speed of about double your frame rate, so at twenty five frames a second, a fiftieth of a second. That gives rain soft streaks, which looks natural and cinematic. Push the shutter faster, a five hundredth or a thousandth, and the drops freeze into little points of light. That's the dramatic, slow motion, music video look. Both are good, just different moods. Try both on the same shot and see which one you like.

And look down, not just up. Wet ground is a mirror. A puddle in Hyde Park reflecting the trees and the sky, a wet footpath reflecting the lights on Macquarie Street at night, will do more to say "rainy Sydney" than the rain itself. Get the camera low, almost touching the puddle, and the reflection fills the frame.

One practical warning. The X M five isn't weather sealed. So for real rain, a cheap rain cover, or even a clear plastic bag with a hole for the lens and a hair tie round it, and keep a microfibre cloth in your pocket for the front of the lens.

And you can practise all of this today, on your phone, on the walk in. Find one backlit patch of rain with something dark behind it, film ten seconds, and see how different it looks. Just don't get hit by a bus doing it. I need you for tomorrow.
