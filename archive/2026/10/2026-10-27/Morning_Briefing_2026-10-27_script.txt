Game tech time! And since you told me you'd love more pixel art on the show, today is all about one of pixel art's oldest, cleverest tricks: the palette swap. And yes, it's going to end with your radish. Everything ends with your radish now.

Let's do the Godot bit first, so you can try it tonight. The goal: one radish sprite, twenty five strength levels, and a different colour scheme for each tier, without drawing it twenty five times.

Step one: draw your radish in Aseprite, or whatever you like, using only a handful of shades of grey, say five. Pure black for the outline, then four greys from dark to light. Think of each grey not as a colour, but as a slot number: slot one is the outline, slot two the shadow, and so on.

Step two: make a tiny palette image. Five pixels wide, one pixel for each slot, and one row for each tier. Row one might be a normal red radish with green leaves. Row five might be a sickly purple. Row ten, a radish that's clearly been eating something it shouldn't. We'll come back to that.

Step three: on your radish's Sprite2D, add a new Shader Material with a canvas item shader. In the fragment function, read the sprite's pixel, take its red value, which for a grey is between zero and one, and use that as the horizontal position to look up a colour in your palette image. The vertical position is the row for the current tier. Then set the output colour to the palette colour, but keep the sprite's original alpha so the transparent bits stay transparent. That's about six lines of shader code.

Two settings matter. Set the palette texture's filter to nearest, so Godot doesn't blend neighbouring colours together, or your radish turns into a smoothie. And tick "Local to Scene" on the material, so each radish gets its own copy. Otherwise, change one radish's tier, and every radish on the farm changes with it. Which, to be fair, would be a pretty good horror moment. Write that down, actually.

Then, when the radish spawns, you just call set shader parameter with the tier number, and it picks its row. One sprite, twenty five monsters. And because it's a shader, you can animate it too: flash every colour to white for a frame when it's hit, or shift it to a red row when it's about to charge. That's your fight-or-run tell, from our very first monster chat, done with one parameter.

So why does this trick exist at all? Go back to the Super Nintendo. Memory was tiny and expensive, so the console didn't store a colour for every pixel. Instead, each pixel of a sprite stored a little number, an index, pointing into a palette, a short list of colours. On the Super Nintendo, a sprite palette had sixteen colours, and the first one was always see-through. The picture was really a paint-by-numbers sheet, and the palette was the box of paints.

And here's the magic: change the paints, and every picture using those numbers changes instantly, for free. That's how you got Luigi from Mario, how Dragon Quest turned its blue slime into an orange she-slime, and how Mortal Kombat got an entire family of ninjas out of one costume. Scorpion, Sub-Zero, Reptile: same man, different laundry. Sorry, I'm a big fan of cheap ninjas.

Developers went further with palette cycling: instead of swapping a palette once, they rotated colours through it every few frames. A row of blues shifting one step at a time makes a waterfall look like it's flowing, with no extra frames of animation at all. Lava bubbles, flickering torches, shimmering water in old R P Gs: lots of it was just colours taking turns.

And there's an artistic reason to keep the trick today, even with unlimited colours. A limited palette forces harmony. When every enemy, tree and tile shares a set of colours, the world looks like it belongs together. It's why a lot of modern pixel art games pick a palette of thirty or so colours and never leave it.

So, your tip list for the weekend:

Two. Nearest filter on the palette, Local to Scene on the material.

Three. Use a bright flash row for hits and a warning row for attacks. Readability first.

Four. Pick a master palette for the whole game early, and make the radish tiers from it, so even the scariest radish still looks like it grew on your farm.

EFFECTS

Effects time. And today, we're going somewhere very far away, to the edge of a black hole. How Interstellar made Gargantua.

When Christopher Nolan made Interstellar in twenty fourteen, he had an unusual collaborator: Kip Thorne, a theoretical physicist who later shared a Nobel Prize for detecting gravitational waves, and who was an executive producer on the film. And Nolan wanted something no film had done before. Not an artist's guess at a black hole. The real thing, or as close as physics could get.

The visual effects house Double Negative, led by supervisor Paul Franklin, took that literally. Normally, a computer renders a scene by tracing rays of light in straight lines, from the camera out into the scene. But near a black hole, light doesn't travel in straight lines. Gravity bends it. So Thorne wrote out the equations for how light moves around a spinning black hole, and Double Negative's scientists built a brand-new renderer to follow them, tracing curved paths through warped space, and tracing whole bundles of light at once, not single rays, so the image wouldn't flicker.

Think of it like this. Imagine looking at a lamp through the bottom of a wine glass. The glass bends the light, and the lamp smears into a ring. A black hole does that with space itself, and the result surprised even the physicist.

Because the glowing disk of gas around Gargantua doesn't just sit flat around the middle, like Saturn's rings. The light from the far side of the disk, the bit hidden behind the black hole, gets bent up and over the top, and down under the bottom. So you see the disk wrapped above and below the black hole at the same time, like a halo. That shape, which is now in every picture of a black hole, came straight out of the maths.

It was not cheap. Some individual frames reportedly took up to a hundred hours to render, and the total data ran to something like eight hundred terabytes. Which is, I believe, slightly less than your server. I'm sorry. I had to.

And here's the artistic choice that makes it a filmmaking story, not just a science one. A real spinning black hole would look lopsided: the side of the disk rushing towards you would be much brighter and bluer, the side moving away dimmer and redder. Nolan and the team decided that would confuse audiences, so they left that part out, and kept the disk evenly lit and golden. Science first, then a deliberate, honest bit of storytelling on top.

But here's my favourite part, and it's very Nolan. The effects weren't just added later. For the scenes inside the spacecraft, the team projected the finished space imagery onto enormous screens outside the cockpit windows, while they filmed. So when Matthew McConaughey looks out at Gargantua, he's actually looking at it, and the light from the black hole is really falling on his face. No green screen, no imagining a tennis ball on a stick. The spacecraft themselves were large, detailed miniatures and full-size sets, not a computer model in sight for the ships.

The film won the Oscar for visual effects. And the work was so new that Thorne and the Double Negative team published scientific papers about it afterwards, about what the light near a spinning black hole would actually look like. A movie that produced real physics. That doesn't happen very often.

The lesson for you, from your own editing desk: the best effects give the actors, and the audience, something real to react to. Light on a face, reflections in glass, a horizon that moves when the camera does. WORKSHOP

Workshop time, and today's is short, practical and something you can do on the walk in, with just your phone. It's the five-shot sequence.

It's the oldest trick in documentary and news filming, and it's how you turn "a clip of a thing" into a tiny story. You film one simple action, from five angles, and it edits together on its own.

Here's the recipe. Pick an action that repeats, so you can film it more than once. On your walk, the easiest is someone buying a coffee at a kiosk, a gardener in the Botanic Garden, or, honestly, you, tapping your Opal card at St James. Very cinematic.

Shot one: a close-up of the hands. The card, the cup, the trowel. Shot two: a close-up of the face, or for you, the gates. Shot three: a wide, so we see where we are. Shot four: over the shoulder, from behind the person, looking at what they're looking at. Shot five: something unusual. Very low, very high, or through something: railings, leaves, a window.

Hold each shot for about ten seconds, and don't move the camera. Let the action move. Lock exposure by holding your finger on the screen until it says the exposure is locked, so the brightness doesn't pump when someone walks through. And film in the same frame rate for all five, ideally twenty five frames a second, which is what Final Cut will want for an Australian project.

Then, in Final Cut, put the wide first, then the hands, the face, the over the shoulder, and end on the unusual one. Cut on the action, when the hand moves, so the edit hides inside the movement. Thirty seconds of footage, and it'll look like a proper scene.

Why it matters for the X M five: when you're travelling, it's easy to come home with two hundred beautiful wide shots and no story. Five shots of one small moment, a market stall, a ferry rope, a street food cook, is what makes a travel video feel like you were really there.
