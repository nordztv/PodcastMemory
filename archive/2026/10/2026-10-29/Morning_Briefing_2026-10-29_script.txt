GAME TECH

Game tech! And today, talking. Specifically, how to make the characters in your game talk back. Dialogue systems. Because Chen Stormstout is not going to tell jokes about being hungry all by himself. Although he'd try. Practical first. Here's the smallest dialogue system that will still carry your whole game, in Godot four, in four pieces. Piece one: keep the words out of the code. Make a custom Resource called DialogueLine, with three exported properties: the speaker's name, the text, and an array of choices. Then a second Resource called DialogueChoice, with the choice text and the name of the line it leads to. Now each conversation is just a file you can edit in the inspector, like the Resource save system we talked about a couple of Fridays ago, and you never have to touch a script to fix a typo in Chen's joke. Piece two: the box on the screen. A CanvasLayer with a Panel, a Label for the speaker's name, and a RichTextLabel for the text. For that classic typewriter effect, set the RichTextLabel's visible ratio to zero, then use a tween to bring it up to one over the length of the line, say three hundredths of a second per letter. Let the player press the button once to finish the line instantly, and a second time to move on. That one detail, being able to skip, is what separates a dialogue system people tolerate from one they enjoy. Piece three: choices. When a line has choices, clear a VBoxContainer, and for each choice, create a Button, set its text, and connect its pressed signal to a function that jumps to the line that choice points to. Give the first button focus, so it works with a controller. Your Secret of Mana fans will be on a gamepad. Unlike some Final Fantasy fourteen typing games I could mention. Too soon? Piece four: memory. This is the bit that makes a world feel alive. Make an autoload, a script that's always loaded, called something like GameState, with a dictionary of flags: "met_chen", "bought_stout", "radish_tier_ten_defeated". When a conversation happens, set a flag. And give your DialogueLine an optional condition: a flag that has to be true for that line to appear. So the first time you meet Chen, he introduces himself. The second time, he says, "Back again? The stout's that good, huh?" And after you've beaten your first big radish, he can say he heard about it. Twenty lines of code, and suddenly your merchant remembers you. Now, the theory, with an everyday analogy. Think of a dialogue system like a choose-your-own-adventure book. Each page is a line, each "turn to page forty" is a choice, and the reader's bookmark is your game state. Game writers talk about a few shapes. A tree branches every time you choose, which explodes fast: three choices, three times, is twenty seven endings. So most games use a hub instead. You start at a central question, branch out to a topic, then loop back to the hub. "Tell me about the valley." "What are you selling?" "Goodbye." That's how most role-playing game shopkeepers work, and it's perfect for Chen. The other big idea is that the choices don't always change the story. Often they just change the flavour, how the character feels about you, or what they say next time, and players still feel heard. That's much cheaper to write than truly branching plots, and for a farming game, where you'll talk to the same villagers hundreds of times, the memory of small things matters far more than big branching endings. And there's a dark little trick... where a character says something slightly different every time you visit. Just a little more tired. A little less cheerful. Until one day, the shop is closed, and there's just a note on the counter. And it's in your handwriting. Quick tip list, for later. One. You don't have to build it all yourself. Dialogue Manager, by Nathan Hoad, is a popular free Godot plugin where you write conversations in a simple script format, with conditions and choices built in, and Dialogic is another with a visual editor. Building the small version yourself first will teach you what you actually need. Two. Write the conversation in a plain text file before you build anything. Read it out loud. If it doesn't sound right, no system will save it. Three. Add a tiny sound per letter while the text types out, with a slightly random pitch, like Animal Crossing's chatter. It gives every character a voice without recording a single line. Chen gets a deep, round blip. The radish gets a squeak. A menacing squeak. Four. Keep every line under about a hundred characters, so it fits in the box without scrolling, and break long speeches into several lines. And that's dialogue: words in Resources, a typewriter box, choices as buttons, and a dictionary that remembers. Now your villagers can finally say something other than "the radishes are watching". Although they should keep saying that. For safety.

EFFECTS

Effects! And since Godzilla Minus Zero is a week away, let's go back to how the first one pulled off a miracle. Godzilla Minus One, from twenty twenty three, the film where a small team in Tokyo beat Hollywood at its own game. Here's the headline. Godzilla Minus One reportedly cost somewhere under fifteen million U S dollars to make, a fraction of a Hollywood blockbuster, and in March twenty twenty four, it won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects, beating films with budgets ten times bigger. It was the first Japanese film ever to win that Oscar, and the first Godzilla film to win an Oscar at all. And the visual effects team at the studio Shirogumi was reported at about thirty five people, which is roughly the number of people who work on a single explosion in some Marvel films. Okay, slight exaggeration. Only slight. The key is the director. Takashi Yamazaki didn't just direct and write it. He was also the visual effects supervisor, and he came up through visual effects himself. He'd even put Godzilla in a film before, in a dream sequence in his two thousand and seven drama, Always: Sunset on Third Street two. So when he planned a shot, he already knew exactly how it would be made, and what it would cost. Nothing was shot "just in case". Every shot had a purpose. Let's take the two most famous sequences. First, the sea chase, where Godzilla pursues a tiny wooden minesweeper boat across the open ocean. Water is one of the hardest things in visual effects, because it has to move, splash, foam and reflect, all at once, and your eye knows exactly how real water behaves. Shirogumi simulated it with software like Houdini, where the computer works out the physics of the water. Yamazaki has talked about how much time the team spent getting the water right on a limited setup. And the clever part is the framing: the camera stays low, with the crew, so you see Godzilla the way they would, rising over waves, fin first. And those dorsal plates, clicking upwards one by one, glowing blue... like a very large countdown. Second, the attack on Ginza, in Tokyo. Godzilla walks through the district in broad daylight, crushing a train carriage with the heroine on it, then unleashes his atomic breath, and the blast rises into a mushroom cloud over the city. For a Japanese audience, that image carries enormous weight. And it works because of the human scale. Yamazaki keeps cutting back to people on the ground, running, looking up, so the effects never float free of the story. Then there's the practical side. The fighter plane in the finale, the Shinden, was a real wartime design that never saw combat, and the production built a life-size replica of it, so the actors had something real to climb into and react to. The rubble, dust and debris in many shots mixed real elements with digital ones, because a real pile of dust next to your actor does half the work for you. When the four members of the team went up to accept the Oscar, Takashi Yamazaki, Kiyoko Shibuya, Masaki Takahashi and Tatsuji Nojima, they each held a little Godzilla figure. That photo is one of the loveliest moments in recent Oscar history. So, the lesson, and it applies to your game as much as filmmaking. Constraints force good decisions. A small team can't do everything, so it has to choose what matters, and put all its effort there: the water, the scale, the faces of the people looking up. Yamazaki didn't try to out-spend Hollywood. He out-planned it. For your Godot game, it's the same idea: you don't need a hundred effects, you need the right four. Shake, freeze, squash, flash, from yesterday. Pick the moment that matters, the radish bursting out of the ground, and spend everything on that one. Godzilla would approve. And here's a little secret about that two billion particle shot in Minus Zero... two artists. The same studio, two people, one shot of the sea. Some things are still made by hand... even when they're made by computer.

DIRECTOR

Director time. And for Halloween week... a cabin in the woods. Sam Raimi, and The Evil Dead, from nineteen eighty one. The story is as simple as horror gets. Five college friends drive to a remote cabin in the Tennessee woods. In the cellar they find a strange book and a tape recorder. They play the tape, which reads out an incantation, and something in the woods wakes up. Rule one of horror: never press play in a cellar. Honestly, these people. Sam Raimi was barely twenty. He and his friends, producer Robert Tapert and actor Bruce Campbell, had grown up making Super eight films in Michigan. To raise money, they first shot a short horror film called Within the Woods, and used it to show local investors what they could do. Then they made the feature for a reported budget of around three hundred and fifty thousand U S dollars, mostly raised from those local investors back in Michigan. They shot on sixteen millimetre film in a real abandoned cabin in Tennessee, over a long, freezing, miserable shoot, with stories of the crew burning bits of the furniture to keep warm. And the thing everyone remembers is the camera. The evil force in the woods is never really shown. Instead, the camera is the evil. It rushes low and fast through the trees, over logs, across a swamp, smashing through windows and doors, chasing the characters through the cabin. It's one of the most influential point-of-view shots in horror. How do you do that with no money? You get inventive. The best-known trick was a camera bolted to the middle of a long plank of wood, with a person holding each end, running through the forest together. The plank's length smooths out the bumps a bit, like a very cheap Steadicam, so you get speed and motion without total chaos. They called these rigs things like the Shaky Cam. For other shots, the camera was mounted on a board that slid along, or carried low by a runner close to the ground. And it was always low, at the height of something crawling, which is exactly the height... you don't want to be looking at. The sound matters just as much. There's a low, constant rumble in the woods, wind that sounds like breathing, and the deadites' voices, mocking and sing-song. And Raimi uses Dutch angles, tilting the camera, and sudden close-ups on eyes, to make the cabin feel wrong long before anything happens. The film almost vanished. It was rough, gory and low budget, and then, at the Cannes market in nineteen eighty two, Stephen King saw it and called it "the most ferociously original horror film of the year". That quote went on the poster, and it got the film distributed around the world. It was also cut or banned in several countries for its gore. Raimi went on to make Evil Dead two, Army of Darkness, the Spider-Man films, and Bruce Campbell became a cult hero. Okay, the lesson, and you can try this one this weekend. Movement can be a character. Make your own little "plank cam". Take a broom handle or a length of timber about a metre and a half long, tape your phone to the middle, or later the X M five on a small clamp, and hold it with both hands out wide. Then walk, fast and low, through the Botanic Garden, ideally not straight at a family having a picnic. Please don't get arrested for my homework. The width of the stick smooths out side-to-side wobble, and keeping it low makes the world feel big and you feel small. Shoot at a higher frame rate if you can, fifty or sixty frames a second, so you can slow it down a touch in Final Cut. Then add a low rumble under it, and you'll have a shot that says "something is coming", without a single monster. And a little secret about the end of the film... the final shot, that rush through the cabin, through every door, straight at Bruce Campbell... is the evil, finally catching him. And you never see what it is. You only ever were it.
